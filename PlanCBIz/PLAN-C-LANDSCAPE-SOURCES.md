# The Mechanics of Short-Video Recommendation: Follower Graph → Interest Graph, and What a Creator Can Actually Control

## 1. COLD START & SEEDING

### 1.1 What the TikTok "Monolith" paper actually says (and doesn't)

**Source:** Liu, Zhu, Liu et al., ByteDance, *"Monolith: Real Time Recommendation System With Collisionless Embedding Table"*, ORSUM@ACM RecSys 2022. **[P-ACAD / P-PLAT]**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07663
- Full text: https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3303/paper8.pdf

Concrete mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Detail | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Embedding table | Cuckoo hashmap, two tables T₀/T₁ with hash functions h₀(x), h₁(x); worst-case O(1) lookup/delete | — |
| Memory control | Occurrence-threshold filtering before ID admission + probabilistic filtering | tunable per model |
| Expirable embeddings | IDs expire after a predefined inactivity period, tunable per table | — |
| Sparse param sync | **Minute-level** to serving PS | ~400 MB/min for 100,000 IDs at 1024-dim |
| Dense param sync | **Day-level**, scheduled at lowest-traffic hour (e.g. midnight) | — |
| Snapshot cadence | Daily; explicitly accepts losing one day of updates on PS failure | — |

Sync-staleness experiment (Criteo public dataset, average online AUC):
- 5-hour sync: **79.66 ± 0.020**
- 1-hour sync: **79.78 ± 0.005**
- 30-min sync: **79.80 ± 0.008**

Live A/B (ByteDance ads model, 7 days): online training vs batch training gave **+14.443% to +18.081% AUC** improvement.

**Critical caveat — [ABSENT]:** The Monolith paper contains **no cold-start section**. It does not describe how a new video gets its first impressions, does not state an initial impression budget, and does not mention follower count. Monolith is an *infrastructure* paper about making ID embeddings update fast; it is widely miscited in marketing content as "the TikTok algorithm paper." What it *does* imply mechanistically is important: because a new video's item-ID embedding is nearly random at birth and only becomes informative after real feedback arrives, **the system is architecturally committed to buying information about every new item with real impressions**. Minute-level sparse-parameter sync is what makes a new video's fate resolvable within minutes rather than days.

### 1.2 The 2021 NYT leaked "TikTok Algo 101" document

**Source:** Ben Smith, *"How TikTok Reads Your Mind"*, The New York Times, 8 Dec 2021 **[P-LEAK]**
- Original (paywalled): https://www.realclearinvestigations.com/2021/12/08/how_tiktok_reads_your_mind_806994.html
- Reporting with the formula: https://gizmodo.com/leaked-tiktok-doc-reveals-its-obvious-secret-to-an-addi-1848166901 and https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/what-makes-tiktok-tick/

The disclosed scoring equation:

```
Plike × Vlike + Pcomment × Vcomment + Eplaytime × Vplaytime + Pplay × Vplay
```

- P = model-predicted probability of the behaviour; E = estimated playtime; V = the company's assigned *value weight* for that behaviour.
- The document states the system "gives scores to all the videos based on this equation, and returns to users videos with the highest scores."
- Stated ultimate goal: **adding daily active users**, decomposed into **retention** (do they come back) and **time spent**.
- The document itself says the formula is "highly simplified" and "the actual equation in use is much more complicated."
- Secondary refinements reported: videos from previously-watched creators get a ranking boost; content categories already watched that day get a diversity penalty; videos explicitly asking for likes get penalised.

**No initial-distribution numbers appear in this document.** It describes the *ranking objective*, not the *seeding budget*.

### 1.3 TikTok's own documentation

**Source:** TikTok Newsroom, *"How TikTok recommends videos #ForYou"* (2020, still live) **[P-PLAT]**
- https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you

The single most load-bearing sentence in the whole corpus:

> **"Neither follower count nor whether the account has had previous high-performing videos are direct factors in the recommendation system."**

Also:
> "Sometimes you may come across a video in your feed that doesn't appear to be relevant to your expressed interests or have amassed a huge number of likes. This is an important and intentional component of our approach to recommendation: bringing a diversity of videos into your For You feed…"

**Source:** TikTok Help Center, *"How TikTok recommends content"* **[P-PLAT]**
- https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/exploring-videos/how-tiktok-recommends-content

Signals stated: (a) **user interactions** — likes, shares, comments, watched-in-full vs skipped; (b) **content information** — sounds, hashtags, *number of video views*, country of publication; (c) **user/device information** — device settings, language, location, time zone, device type.

Dedup/diversity rules stated explicitly: "we typically don't recommend content you've already seen"; "we generally won't recommend two posts in a row from that creator."

**Note the inconsistency:** For LIVE video, TikTok *does* list "number of followers of that creator" as a content factor. So the "follower count is not a factor" claim is scoped to the video For You feed, not the whole product.

### 1.4 Meta's published cold-start machinery

**Source:** Meta AI, *"The AI behind unconnected content recommendations on Facebook and Instagram"* (2023) **[P-PLAT]**
- https://ai.meta.com/blog/ai-unconnected-content-recommendations-facebook-instagram/

- Content understanding stack named: **MViT, XLM-R / XLM-V, FLAVA, Omnivore** — multimodal models producing visual recognition, object detection, OCR, audio analysis, topic classification, hashtag prediction, and similarity matching.
- Retrieval: hierarchical deep neural retrieval, billions → thousands → hundreds "in hundredths of a second."
- Ranking: pointwise and listwise predictions; very large-scale attention models, GNNs, few-shot learning, ensembles with heterogeneous interaction modules.
- **Cold start is named and productised:** a system called **"Meta Interest Learner"** — a few-shot learning system that "matches fresh content to audiences based on interests despite minimal engagement history." Meta also states it uses **online learning to distribute new content broadly** so quality posts reach relevant audiences.
- Model scale: "tens of trillions of parameters."
- Reported outcome: **+15% watch time** in Reels, with reduced skip and hide rates.

This is the clearest published statement by any platform that **new content is matched by its *content embedding*, not by its creator's audience size**. That is the mechanical basis of the interest-graph claim.

### 1.5 Instagram's account-level embeddings — the most important finding for Question 4

**Source:** Ivan Medvedev et al., Instagram Engineering, *"Powered by AI: Instagram's Explore recommender system"* (Nov 2019) **[P-PLAT]**
- https://instagram-engineering.com/powered-by-ai-instagrams-explore-recommender-system-7ca901d2a882

Instagram built **IG2Vec**, a word2vec applied to *accounts*:

> Account IDs a user interacts with are treated "as a sequence of words in a sentence," so the model can "predict accounts with which a person is likely to interact in a given session."

And the assumption that makes it work, stated explicitly by Instagram:

> **"If an individual interacts with a sequence of accounts in the same session, it's more likely to be topically coherent compared with a random sequence of accounts."**

Mechanics: cosine distance / dot product between account embeddings → KNN across "millions of accounts" → identify accounts similar to a user's **seed accounts** → pull media from those accounts → "tens of thousands of eligible candidates for the average person."

Embedding-quality validation: Instagram trains classifiers to **predict an account's topic from its embedding** and compares to human-labelled data. That is, Instagram's own quality metric for the account embedding is *topical legibility*.

Ranking funnel disclosed: **500 candidates → 150** (distillation model) **→ 50** (lightweight NN) **→ 25** (deep NN, first page of Explore grid).

**Source:** Meta Engineering, *"Scaling the Instagram Explore recommendations system"* (Aug 2023) **[P-PLAT]**
- https://engineering.fb.com/2023/08/09/ml-applications/scaling-instagram-explore-recommendations-system/

The 2023 architecture is **two-tower (user tower / item tower)**, with four retrieval source types: heuristic (trending), ML-based, real-time (recent interactions), pre-generated (long-term interests). Funnel: billions → hundreds → **thousands (first-stage ranker)** → **100 (second-stage MTML ranker)**. First-stage ranker is distilled from second stage with label `PSelect = {media in top K results ranked by the second stage}`. Value model: `P(click)·W_click + P(like)·W_like − P(see_less)·W_see_less`.

**Important:** The 2023 post makes **no mention of account-level embeddings, account clustering, or account-to-account retrieval**. The entity is *user × item*. Whether IG2Vec still exists inside the "pre-generated" retrieval source is undisclosed. **[ABSENT]**

**Source:** Instagram for Creators, *"Helping creators of all sizes break through"* **[P-PLAT]**

### 1.7 YouTube Shorts

**Source:** YouTube Help, *"Search & discovery tips — Shorts"* **[P-PLAT]**
- https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225

### 1.8 Google's published exploration-allocation mechanism

**Source:** Dong Wang, Junyi Jiao, Arnab Bhadury, Yaping Zhang, Mingyan Gao (Google LLC), *"Item Level Exploration Traffic Allocation in Large-scale Recommendation Systems"* (2025) **[P-ACAD]**
- https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09033v1

Mechanism: predict each new item's **"discoverability"** — the probability it can be recommended a minimum number of times by the *exploitation* engine — then allocate exploration traffic in three tiers:
1. **High-discoverability** items → traffic solved for a target confidence level CF_high
2. **Moderate-discoverability** → **MaxCap** (maximum allocation)
3. **Low-discoverability** → allocated proportionally to user feedback, still capped at MaxCap

Model performance: **AUC 0.96, PR-AUC 0.84**.

**[ABSENT]:** No published number for the total exploration traffic budget as a share of impressions, no impression threshold for "discoverable," no corpus-coverage results. Google says only that "the enhanced efficiency in traffic allocation yielded a measurable impact on key performance indicators."

**This is the single most important documented absence in the whole corpus:** *no platform has ever published the size of the cold-start impression budget.* Every number circulating in creator education (see §1.10) is invented.

### 1.9 Douyin's official 2025 algorithm disclosure

**Source:** Douyin Safety and Trust Center (抖音安全与信任中心), launched **30 March 2025** at **95152.douyin.com**; disclosure by Douyin President **Han Shangyou (韩尚佑)** **[P-PLAT]**
- Reporting: https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20250331A0895B00 (Tech星球 / Tencent News, 31 Mar 2025); https://finance.sina.cn/tech/2025-04-16/detail-inetkpaq3131830.d.html (Sina, 16 Apr 2025); https://www.rmlt.com.cn/2025/0331/726642.shtml

> **综合预测用户行为概率 × 行为价值权重 = 视频推荐优先级**
> ("aggregate predicted user-behaviour probability × behaviour value weight = video recommendation priority")

— structurally identical to the 2021 leaked TikTok formula, now confirmed on the record by the platform four years later.

The disclosure that matters most for the niche question:
> **"抖音的推荐系统，已经几乎不依赖对内容或者用户打标签"**
> ("Douyin's recommendation system already almost does not rely on tagging content or users.")

Instead the system "直接预估每一个用户对每一个内容的目标行为…的概率" — directly predicts each user's probability of each target behaviour (like, follow, share, comment) for each item.

Also disclosed: **more than 100 distinct objectives** (100多个不同目标) are balanced, including bookmarking, specifically to prevent convergence on repetitive content; and there is **dedicated exploration traffic** (专门的探索流量) to help users expand interests.

**[ABSENT]:** No cold-start mechanics, no initial-exposure numbers, no statement on follower vs recommendation traffic split.

### 1.10 The Chinese "traffic pool" model is folklore — flag it

Chinese-language practitioner literature is dominated by a tiered "traffic pool" (流量池) model: initial push of **200–500 views** (scaled by "account weight"), then **1,000–5,000**, then **10,000+**, with advancement gated on completion rate (完播率), like rate (~10%), comment and share rates within the first hour; accounts under 100 views/week labelled "低级号或废号" (low-tier / dead accounts). I searched Chinese, English and Thai for a primary ByteDance statement of pool sizes and found none.

### 1.11 Manual intervention: "heating"

**Source:** Emily Baker-White, Forbes, 20 Jan 2023 — internal TikTok "MINT Heating Playbook" and "TikTok Heating Policy" **[P-LEAK]**
- https://www.forbes.com/sites/emilybaker-white/2023/01/20/tiktoks-secret-heating-button-can-make-anyone-go-viral/

## 2. VARIANCE: How unequal and unpredictable is a single creator's output?

This is where the evidence is thinnest in the popular literature and strongest in one specific academic paper.

### 2.1 The best available measurement — peak-to-median ratio and within-account Gini

**Source:** Benjamin Guinaudeau, Fabio Votta, Kevin Munger, *"Fifteen Seconds of Fame: TikTok and the Supply Side of Social Video"*, **Computational Communication Research 4(2), 2022** — peer-reviewed, primary measurement **[P-ACAD]**
- PDF: https://kmunger.github.io/pdfs/tiktok.pdf
- Journal: https://journal.computationalcommunication.org/article/view/4755
- Publisher summary: https://www.psu.edu/news/liberal-arts/story/tiktok-lowers-barriers-virality-keeps-tight-control-through-algorithm

**Sample:** 11,546 TikTok accounts primarily posting political content, **1,998,642 videos**, collected through 6 Oct 2020. Comparison set: 1,712 YouTube political channels, ~1,000,000 videos, collected May 2020.

| Metric | TikTok | YouTube | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peak-to-median view ratio** (median across accounts) | **64×** | **40×** | ratio of an account's most-viewed video to its median video |
| **Within-account Gini of views** (median across accounts) | **0.70** | **0.62** | Gini, 0 = perfect equality |
| **Follower elasticity of median plays** | **0.54** | **0.73** | % change in median video plays per 1% change in followers |
| Fit of that elasticity | R² = 0.56, r = 0.75 | R² = 0.67, r = 0.82 | — |
| Commenters who created ≥5 videos | **78.05%** | 18.47% | % of commenters |

Exact wording: *"The median value of the peak-median ratio is 64 for TikTok, meaning that the median accounts' most popular video has 64 as many views as their average (median) video. For YouTube, that number is only 40."* And: *"The Gini Coefficient indicates severe inequality with a median of 0.62 for YouTube and a median of 0.70 for TikTok."*

**This is the answer to the peak-to-median question, and it is the only clean academic measurement of it I could find.** Note the sample is *political* TikTok accounts in 2020 — a niche vertical, pre-Reels-maturity, US-centric, and possibly higher-variance than commercial verticals. It should be treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate for the platform generally, not a calibrated constant.

**Practical translation:** a typical TikTok account's best video does ~64× the views of its typical video. A within-account Gini of 0.70 is worse than the income inequality of the most unequal countries on Earth. **An account's own output is, distributionally, a lottery ticket portfolio.**

### 2.2 The theoretical ceiling on predicting any individual outcome

**Source:** Travis Martin, Jake Hofman, Amit Sharma, Ashton Anderson, Duncan Watts, *"Exploring Limits to Prediction in Complex Social Systems"*, WWW 2016 **[P-ACAD]**
- https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~ashton/pubs/limpred.pdf

**Sample:** 852 million tweets containing URLs, February 2015; 51.6M users; 1.806 billion retweets. Restricted (full-feature) subset: 183M tweets, 7.2M users, 1.299B retweets.

| Result | Value |
|---|---|
| Best achievable R² predicting cascade size, unrestricted data | **0.42** |
| Best achievable R² predicting cascade size, restricted (full-feature) data | **0.48** |
| Prior-work baseline | ~0.34 |
| **Variance left unexplained** | **>52%** |

Their conclusion, verbatim: *"even with a much larger dataset and a far more extensive list of features… the best R² that can be achieved… is 0.48, leaving more than half of the variance in cascade sizes unexplained."*

Simulation with *perfect* knowledge of the process:
- Products of identical quality: R² ≈ **0.93**
- Products with 15% quality variation: R² drops to **0.60**
- With realistic 20–30% error in quality estimation: R² falls **below 0.60**

*"Even a relatively small amount of error in estimating a product's quality leads to a rapid decrease in one's ability to predict its success."*

### 2.3 The canonical unpredictability experiment

**Source:** Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, Duncan Watts, *"Experimental Study of Inequality and Unpredictability in an Artificial Cultural Market"*, **Science 311(5762): 854–856, 2006** **[P-ACAD]**
- https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1121066
- Full text PDF: https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/salganik_dodds_watts06_full.pdf

**Design:** 14,341 participants total (Experiment 1: 7,149; Experiment 2: 7,192); **48 songs**; **8 independent "social influence" worlds** plus an independent (no-social-information) control. Same songs, same starting conditions, eight parallel universes.

| Condition | Gini (inequality) | u (unpredictability: mean market-share difference for the same song across worlds) |
|---|---|---|
| Experiment 1, independent | ≈0.12 | ≈0.008 |
| Experiment 1, social influence | ≈0.30 | ≈0.015 |
| Experiment 2 (stronger signal), independent | ≈0.15 | ≈0.009 |
| Experiment 2, social influence | ≈0.40 | ≈0.020 |

*(Figures read from the paper's figures; treat as approximate to ±0.02.)*

**Finding:** increasing the strength of social influence increased **both** inequality **and** unpredictability. Quality set a floor and a ceiling — the best songs rarely did terribly, the worst rarely did well — but *within* that band, outcome was essentially path-dependent noise.

**Counter-evidence worth noting:** Ormerod & Glass, *"Predictability in an 'Unpredictable' Artificial Cultural Market"* (2009), https://arxiv.org/pdf/0908.1320, re-analysed the same data and showed that although *ex ante* prediction is near-impossible, the eventual winner becomes identifiable **very early** — at roughly **3–8% of the way through** each experiment, ~1% of total downloads; rankings had stabilised by step 10 in 13 of 18 experiments (p<0.05); the eventual winner was unequivocally identified in 4 of 18 and was within the joint-leader group in 9 of 16 social-influence experiments. **Translation to short video: you cannot predict a video before posting, but you can read its fate from the first few hours of data.** This is the strongest theoretical justification for Trial Reels / early-signal monitoring.

Terms searched: "top video share of channel total views"; "concentration of views within channel"; "outlier videos percentage of views". The Guinaudeau et al. Gini (0.70) is the closest proxy and implies extreme concentration, but no one has published the top-k share directly.

- **[ABSENT]** Any controlled experiment posting identical content from multiple matched accounts to isolate account effects from content effects. This is the single most valuable missing experiment in the entire field, and it is cheap to run.

## 3. DECOUPLING: Does follower count still predict views?

### 3.1 The strongest single measurement — TikTok's follower graph is nearly irrelevant to consumption

**Source:** *"Analyzing User Engagement with TikTok's Short Format Video Recommendations using Data Donations"*, **ACM CHI 2024** **[P-ACAD]**
- https://arxiv.org/html/2301.04945v2 · https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613904.3642433

**Method:** GDPR data-donation. **347 TikTok users**, **9.2 million video views** logged 26 Jul 2020 – 21 Feb 2022; 1.12M like actions from 328 participants; metadata retrieved for 4.1M unique videos (83.4% coverage).

| Finding | Value |
|---|---|
| **Share of all video views coming from accounts the user follows** | **10.3%** |
| Stability of that share over time | ~10%, **constant**, even as participants' following count grew from **40 (day 1) → 350 (day 120)** |
| Median views of videos from **non-followed** accounts | **755,000** |
| Median views of videos from **followed** accounts | **85,000** |
| Completion rate, non-followed content | 44–46% |
| Completion rate, followed content | 38–42% |
| Videos watched to completion overall | 45% (55% skipped) |
| Participants completing >65% of their watch history | **zero** |

**This is the empirical core of the interest-graph thesis.** ~90% of TikTok consumption is from accounts the viewer does not follow, and following *more* accounts does not increase that share. The follower graph on TikTok is not a distribution channel; it is a bookmark list.

### 3.2 The important contradiction — the *consumer's* follows do matter

**Source:** Maximilian Boeker & Aleksandra Urman, *"An Empirical Investigation of Personalization Factors on TikTok"*, **ACM Web Conference (WWW) 2022** **[P-ACAD]**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12271 · https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3485447.3512102

Sock-puppet audit manipulating language, location, follow-feature, like-feature, and video view rate. **Finding: "the follow-feature has the strongest influence, followed by the like-feature and video view rate."**

**Do not confuse these two results.** Boeker & Urman measure how a *viewer's* act of following shapes *their own* feed — following is the strongest personalisation lever a user has. The CHI 2024 study measures what share of a user's *consumption* comes from followed accounts — only 10.3%. Both are true: following strongly shapes *which interest clusters* the algorithm pushes you into, but the resulting feed is overwhelmingly composed of *other* creators in those clusters. **For a creator, this means a follow is valuable primarily as a signal that routes the algorithm toward creators like you — including your competitors.**

### 3.3 Follower elasticity: independent academic and vendor data converge on ~0.54

| Source | Class | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guinaudeau/Votta/Munger 2022 | [P-ACAD] | 11,546 accts, 2.0M videos, 2020 | +1% followers → **+0.54%** median plays (TikTok); +0.73% (YouTube) |
| Rival IQ 2024 TikTok Benchmark Report | [P-VEND] | 374,000+ videos, 2,000+ handles ≥1K followers, CY2023 | <5,000 followers: **~43 views per 100 followers**; 200K–1M: **~4 views per 100 followers**. Median: **21.8% of followers**. → implied elasticity **≈0.54** |
| Socialinsider Instagram Benchmarks | [P-VEND] | 35M posts, 447,613 pages, Jan–Dec 2025 | Reels avg views: **580** (1–5K followers) vs **16,035** (100K–1M) → implied elasticity **≈0.67** |

- Rival IQ: https://www.rivaliq.com/blog/tiktok-benchmark-report/
- Socialinsider Instagram: https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/instagram

**The 0.54 convergence between an independent peer-reviewed study (2020 data) and a commercial benchmark (2023 data), derived by completely different methods, is the most robust quantitative finding in this report after the peak-to-median ratio.**

**Interpretation:** followers are *not* irrelevant — the correlation is strong (r = 0.75) — but the relationship is **strongly sublinear**. Doubling followers buys roughly **+45%** views, not +100%. Views-per-follower falls by roughly an order of magnitude from nano to macro tier. Follower count is a *weak, decaying* predictor of any given video's views and explains just over half the cross-account variance in *median* views (R²=0.56) — and essentially nothing about the variance of an individual video *within* an account.

### 3.5 A genuine contradiction: 2025 may have *re-concentrated*

**Socialinsider TikTok Benchmarks** (https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/tiktok) **[P-VEND]** — **2 million TikTok videos** from **214,507 profiles**, Jan 2024 – Dec 2025:

| Follower tier | 2024 avg views/post | 2025 avg views/post | Change |
|---|---|---|---|

| 50–100K | 8,688 | 9,900 | **+14%** |
| 100K–1M | 25,198 | 34,900 | **+39%** |

Engagement rate by follower stayed roughly flat across tiers (2025: 4.40% at 1–5K → 3.95% at 100K–1M).

**Derived (my calculation, not published):** implied follower elasticity of views in the Socialinsider 2025 data is **≈0.93** — near-linear. In their 2024 data it was closer to the 0.54–0.67 range. **If this vendor series is measuring something real, the decoupling narrative weakened materially in 2025: small accounts lost ~59% of their views while large accounts gained ~39%.**

I flag this loudly because it is the strongest counter-evidence in the report to the "interest graph democratises distribution" story, and because it comes from a vendor panel with unknown composition drift (Socialinsider itself notes it labelled 2025 data as "2026" due to publication timing, which is a data-hygiene warning). **This finding needs independent replication before being used as a load-bearing claim.** It is directionally corroborated by Metricool's independent finding of a **−31.30%** YoY drop in TikTok views per post against **+72.10%** more content posted — i.e., supply inflation compressing per-post distribution — but Metricool does not break that out by follower tier.

### 3.6 Platform statements on the follower-reach relationship

**Instagram, April 2024 recommendations update** — the most explicit admission by any platform **[P-PLAT]**
- https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-algorithm-prioritize-original-emerging-creators/714777/

> **"Historically because of how we've ranked content, creators with large followings and aggregators of reposted content have gotten more reach in recommendations than smaller, original content creators. We think it's important to correct this to give all creators a more equal chance of breaking through to new audiences."**

Concrete mechanisms announced:
> "accounts that repeatedly (**10 or more times in the last 30 days**) post content from other Instagram users that they didn't create or enhance in a material way will not be shown in surfaces where we recommend content."
> "When we find two or more identical pieces of content on Instagram, we will only recommend the original one… the original content will directly replace the reposted content in recommendations."

**Instagram, 2025** (https://creators.instagram.com/blog/the-latest-with-instagram):
> "Content ranking on Instagram **historically favored creators with larger followings**. However, last year we announced updates to our recommendations of reels. Through this process, **creators of all sizes have an equal chance of breaking through** and reaching new audiences."
> "one of the most effective ways to grow is to post original content consistently, because now everyone has the potential to reach a massive audience."

**But Instagram's own ranking documentation still lists follower count as a signal** (https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained) **[P-PLAT]**. Reels signals, in Instagram's stated rough order of importance:
1. Your activity (reels you've liked, saved, reshared, engaged with)
2. Your history of interacting with the person who posted
3. Information about the reel (audio, visuals, popularity)
4. **Information about the person who posted — "follower counts and engagement levels"**

So Instagram simultaneously says follower count is a (fourth-ranked) signal *and* that creators of all sizes have an equal chance. Both can be technically true — follower count may enter as a weak prior, dominated by the first three signals — but a creator should not read "equal chance" as literal.

**Mosseri on why followers don't see your posts** (https://searchengineland.com/instagram-why-content-isnt-reaching-followers-439028) **[P-PLAT]**: two reasons given — followers don't open the app that day, and those who do can't scroll far enough. Mosseri: *"ranking and recommendations increase the amount of posts people get to, and therefore the amount of reach to go around, more than any thing else we've ever done."*

**[ABSENT]:** Neither Mosseri nor Instagram has ever published the median share of followers who see a given post.

## 4. WHAT IS ACTUALLY CONTROLLABLE — and the niche-consistency question

### 4.1 Consolidated table of documented ranking signals

| Platform | Signals as documented, with source class |
|---|---|
| **TikTok** (leaked 2021, [P-LEAK]) | P(like), P(comment), **E(playtime)**, P(play), each × a company-assigned value weight |
| **TikTok** (official, [P-PLAT]) | Likes, shares, comments, **watched in full vs skipped**; sounds, hashtags, view count, publication country; device/language/location |
| **Douyin** (official 2025, [P-PLAT]) | Predicted probability of like / follow / share / comment × behaviour value weight; **100+ objectives** incl. bookmark; dedicated exploration traffic |
| **Instagram Reels** (official, [P-PLAT]) | Your activity → your history with the poster → reel info (audio, visuals, popularity) → poster info (follower count, engagement levels) |
| **Instagram Explore** (official, [P-PLAT]) | Value model: `P(click)·W + P(like)·W − P(see_less)·W` |
| **YouTube Shorts** (official, [P-PLAT]) | **% of viewers who chose to view**, **avg. view duration**, **avg. % viewed**, likes/dislikes, **post-watch survey results** |
| **Kuaishou PEPNet** (KDD 2023, [P-ACAD]) | Six prediction tasks: **Like, Follow, Forward, Hate, Click, EffView** across three domains (Discovery Tab, Featured-Video Tab, Slide Tab) |

**What is common to every documented system:** *watch-completion / view-duration and share-type actions are first-class objectives everywhere.* **Rewatch/loop is nowhere named as a distinct documented signal by any platform** — [ABSENT]. It is universally asserted in creator education and appears in no primary source I found. TikTok's "watched in full" and YouTube's "avg. % viewed" are the closest documented analogues; neither is rewatch.

### 4.2 Does the algorithm model the ACCOUNT as an entity? Yes — with a caveat

**Evidence FOR account-level modelling:**

1. **Instagram IG2Vec** (2019, [P-PLAT], §1.5) — account embeddings learned from co-engagement sequences, with the explicit stated premise that co-engaged account sequences are **topically coherent**, and with embedding quality validated by *predicting the account's topic from its embedding*. This is the single strongest published artefact supporting the thesis that topical clarity at the account level is machine-legible and useful for retrieval.

2. **Kuaishou PEPNet** (Chang et al., KDD 2023, [P-ACAD], https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.01115) — the production ranking model takes "user/item/**author**-side features (𝓕_u/𝓕_i/𝓕_a) as personalized priors… such as user ID, item ID, **author ID (the producer of short videos in Kuaishou)**, and other side information features," concatenated as `O_prior = E(𝓕_u) ⊕ E(𝓕_i) ⊕ E(𝓕_a)` and fed to a Gate Neural Unit that rescales the DNN's hidden units per task. **The author is a first-class, learned entity with its own embedding in a deployed billion-user ranking model.** An author-ID embedding accumulates whatever is statistically stable about that author's audience across all their items — which is exactly the mechanism by which "the algorithm learns what your account is" would operate, if it operates.

3. **TikTok Monolith's expirable embeddings** (§1.1) — IDs expire after inactivity. Mechanically, this means an account that stops posting loses its learned representation. That is the closest thing to a documented mechanism behind the folk belief that "the algorithm forgets you."

**Evidence AGAINST, and it is serious:**

4. **Douyin's own 2025 disclosure**: *"抖音的推荐系统，已经几乎不依赖对内容或者用户打标签"* — the recommendation system almost no longer relies on tagging content or users, and instead directly predicts behaviour probabilities. If ByteDance's own president is accurate, the mental model of "build a clean account label so the algorithm knows who to show you to" is **explicitly obsolete on Douyin**, and by strong implication on TikTok.

5. **Instagram's 2023 Explore architecture post makes no mention of account embeddings at all** — the entities are user and item in a two-tower model. Whether IG2Vec survives inside the "pre-generated" retrieval source is undisclosed.

6. **Kuaishou's PEPNet paper contains no cold-start analysis and no creator-level analysis separate from user-item interactions.** The author embedding exists; its causal contribution to a new video's distribution is unpublished.

### 4.3 THE CENTRAL FINDING: nobody has measured whether niche consistency improves reach

I searched hard, in English, Chinese and Thai, using every term you supplied and more:

- "topic consistency" recommendation · "topical consistency" creator channel reach
- "account-level embedding" · "creator embedding" · "author embedding" recommendation
- "content pillar" algorithm evidence · "niche consistency" reach study
- YouTube channel content diversity vs specialization subscriber growth
- "content diversification" / "topic diversity" creator channel performance panel regression
- "audience overlap" channel topical coherence candidate generation
- 抖音 账号标签 垂直度 推荐算法 · 垂直度 流量
- "user interest drift" account topic switch reach penalty empirical

**Result: [ABSENT]. There is no published study — academic, platform, or vendor — that measures whether an account's topical consistency causally improves the distribution of its content.** Not one. No observational panel study, no natural experiment, no platform A/B disclosure, no vendor benchmark segmented by topic-coherence score.

What exists instead:

**(a) A mechanistic plausibility argument, from Instagram itself.** IG2Vec's entire premise is that co-engaged account sequences are topically coherent. If a retrieval system reaches candidates via "accounts similar to accounts you engaged with," then an account whose embedding sits in a tight, well-populated topical neighbourhood is *retrievable from more seed accounts* than one whose embedding sits in the sparse space between clusters. This is a strong argument. It is not a measurement, and it is from 2019.

**(b) Creator folk theory, documented as folk theory.** Florini, Watson, O'Neil, Grumbach & Gray, *"Algorithmic folk theories: Content creators' understandings of TikTok's algorithms"*, **First Monday, 2026** — 20 semi-structured interviews (60–90 min) with US creators from historically marginalised groups, Jan–Jul 2024 **[P-ACAD, qualitative]**
- https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/14362/12415

A participant: **"If you get a niche and it's popular and you stick in that niche, you're rewarded."**

The researchers characterise these factors as ones that "did not guarantee a video would do well" but whose "absence consistently led to videos earning limited views." **The paper provides no independent corroboration of the niche theory — it documents the belief, not its truth.** Their headline finding is that creators' first folk theory is that *understanding TikTok's algorithm is largely impossible*.

**(c) A counterintuitive theoretical result that cuts the other way.** Hron, Krauth, Jordan, Kilbertus & Dean, *"Modeling Content Creator Incentives on Algorithm-Curated Platforms"*, **ICLR 2023** **[P-ACAD]**
- https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.13102 · https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/poster/10716

They model "exposure games": creators choose positions on the unit sphere in an embedding space; exposure follows `softmax(τ⁻¹⟨c, sᵢ⟩)` over items, with τ the exploration temperature.

- **"High levels of exploration incentivize broadly appealing content"; "low levels lead to specialization."**
- Their stated tension: *"producers may be discouraged from creating niche content when the algorithm is exploring too much."*
- Theorem 3: for sufficiently large τ, all producers converge on identical content aligned with the *average* consumer.
- Theorem 2: pure Nash equilibria need not exist in either hardmax (τ=0) or softmax (τ>0) games.
- Proposition 1: whether a PNE exists depends on implementation minutiae (non-negative vs unconstrained matrix factorisation).

**Implication:** the payoff to niching is *not* monotonically positive. It depends on how much the platform explores. A platform that explores aggressively (which is what all of them claim to be doing) mathematically *reduces* the return to specialisation and pushes creators toward broad appeal. This is the most rigorous piece of evidence I found bearing on the niche question, and it argues **against** the standard advice — while also being a stylised model, not a measurement of TikTok.

**(d) Platform statements that are agnostic or negative.**
- YouTube: *"There is no minimum posting cadence required"*; *"Focus on what your audience likes"* — no topic-consistency claim anywhere in official YouTube documentation. [ABSENT]
- TikTok: no statement on niche in any official documentation I could find. TikTok's user-facing "#niche" discovery pages are user-generated content, not platform guidance.
- Instagram: no statement on niche or topic in the Ranking Explained page, the Reach tips page, or the Creators blog. Instagram's advice is "post original content **consistently**" — cadence, not topic.

### 4.4 Related evidence: content embeddings alone *can* cold-start a video

**Source:** Dzhoha, Mirylenka, Malykh, Buchmann, Catino (Zalando), *"Short-Form Video Recommendations with Multimodal Embeddings: Addressing Cold-Start and Bias Challenges"*, 2025 **[P-ACAD, industry, non-social platform]**
- https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19346v1

CLIP-based multimodal embeddings map user history and video content into a shared semantic space; video embeddings = average of CLIP representations over uniformly sampled frames. Online A/B results:
- Videos watched ≥25% of duration: **+41%** (95% CI 21–61%)
- Videos watched ≥50%: **+50%** (95% CI 22–78%)
- Watch-time completion rates: **+39%**

This is an e-commerce video feed, not TikTok. But it demonstrates that **content-derived semantic embeddings alone are sufficient to route a brand-new video to the right audience with large measured effect sizes** — the mechanism the interest-graph thesis depends on. It also implies the *content* of the video, not the *account*, is doing the routing.

### 4.5 The narrowing of exposure (relevant to whether niching "works")

**Source:** *"Uncovering the Deep Filter Bubble: Narrow Exposure in Short-Video Recommendation"*, 2024 **[P-ACAD]**
- https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04511v1

**Sample:** 400 million interactions, 400,000 users, 20 million items, one year, a major Chinese short-video platform. Three-level hierarchical category labels: 58 level-1, 326 level-2, 512 level-3 categories.

Findings: an obvious **decrease in overall category coverage ratio toward the end of the year**, most pronounced at level 1; probability densities for level-2 and level-3 coverage are "extremely sharp at a low value between 0 and 0.1" — users are served an extremely narrow slice of the taxonomy at fine granularity.

**Implication for the niche question:** if viewers are being narrowed into deep sub-topical bubbles, then *from the demand side* a tightly-defined creator has a well-defined bucket of users to be routed to. This is indirect support for the niche thesis — but it is about users, not creators, and it is a Chinese platform.

### 5.3 Indirect evidence, pointing in *both* directions

**Toward more randomness / more open distribution:**
- Meta's unconnected share doubled 2022→2024 (§5.1).
- Metricool 2026: FYP drives **72.70%** of TikTok video views.
- Instagram's April 2024 changes explicitly redistribute recommendation reach *away* from large accounts and aggregators.

**Toward less randomness / re-concentration:**
- The Deep Filter Bubble study (§4.5): user category coverage **decreased** over one year on a major Chinese short-video platform — the system narrows, it does not broaden.
- Socialinsider TikTok 2024→2025 (§3.5): small accounts **−59%** views, large accounts **+39%**.
- Metricool: content volume **+72.10%** YoY with views **−31.30%** — mechanical crowding independent of any algorithm change. **When supply grows 72% and total demand doesn't, the median post loses ~40% of its views by arithmetic alone.** A creator experiencing declining reach in 2025–26 is probably observing supply inflation, not an algorithm change. This is an under-appreciated point and it is well-evidenced.
- Hron et al. (§4.3, Theorem 3): more exploration → producers converge on *broadly appealing* content, which *reduces* content diversity in equilibrium. More algorithmic randomness can produce *less* content variety.

## 6. IS VIRALITY PREDICTABLE FROM CONTENT FEATURES?

### 6.1 The optimistic result — and why to discount it

**Source:** *"Slapping Cats, Bopping Heads, and Oreo Shakes: Understanding Indicators of Virality in TikTok Short Videos"*, **ACM Web Science 2022** **[P-ACAD, but weak design]**
- https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02452 · https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3501247.3531551

**Sample:** **400 TikTok videos** (200 "viral", 200 "non-viral") from 20 hashtags, March–April 2021. Manual codebook annotation by three researchers, inter-rater Kappa 0.63–0.89.

**Virality definition:** top-10 most-liked videos per hashtag = viral; bottom-10 = non-viral.

**Performance:** Logistic Regression **AUC 0.93**, **F1 0.88**, **83% accuracy**.

**Feature importance, ranked:**
1. **Popular creator (≥10,000 followers): present in 90% of viral vs 24% of non-viral videos — by far the dominant predictor**
2. Shot scale: long shots in only 1% of viral vs 17% of non-viral
3. Lifespan: 98% of viral were recent; 85% of non-viral were outdated
4. On-screen text: **67% of viral vs 30% of non-viral**
5. Second-person POV: 70% of viral vs 60% of non-viral

**Four reasons to discount the AUC 0.93 headline:**
- **N = 400.** Trivially small.
- **Selection on the dependent variable.** Sampling the top-10 and bottom-10 per hashtag creates an artificially separable binary task. Real-world prediction is over a continuous, log-normal distribution with no gap in the middle.
- **The strongest predictor is follower count, not content.** This paper is evidence *against* full decoupling, and its content-feature contribution is second-order.
- **"Lifespan" is close to circular** — recent videos are more likely to be currently top-ranked in a hashtag.

**Its honest contribution:** on-screen text (67% vs 30%) and shot scale (long shots 1% vs 17%) are real, replicable, controllable craft variables with large measured gaps. Those two findings are usable.

### 6.2 The "prediction works, but only from early engagement" result

**Source:** Berkovsky et al., *"Characterizing and Predicting Viral-and-Popular Video Content"*, **CIKM 2015** **[P-ACAD]**
- https://shlomo-berkovsky.github.io/files/pdf/CIKM15.pdf

**Sample:** 200,000 YouTube videos discovered via Twitter mentions over 24 hours, tracked for two weeks. Popular = top 2.5% by views (5,000 videos); Viral = top 2.5% by Twitter mentions.

| Task | AUC |
|---|---|
| YouTube popularity (from YouTube features) | **0.95** |
| Twitter virality (from Twitter features) | **0.88** |
| Viral-and-popular (combined features) | **0.91** |

With only **1 day** of training data, classifiers made accurate **7-day-ahead** predictions.

**Crucial caveat:** the predictive features are **engagement and metadata**, not content. This paper shows early trajectory is highly informative — it does not show that a video's content predicts its outcome. Combined with Ormerod & Glass (§2.3), the consistent picture is: **pre-publication prediction is near-impossible; post-publication prediction from the first few hours is very good.**

### 6.3 The weak result

**Source:** *"Video Popularity in Social Media: Impact of Emotions, Raw Features and Viewer Comments"* (2024) **[P-ACAD, very small N]**
- https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16272

**Sample: 50 environmental videos from a single Instagram account.** Raw content features → 0.67 accuracy (F1 0.62); comment emotions → 0.80 accuracy (F1 0.73). **N=50 from one account is not generalisable to anything.** Its only value is directional and consistent with §6.2: **post-publication audience reaction beats pre-publication content features by ~13 accuracy points.**

### 6.4 Synthesis on predictability

| Question | Best evidence | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Can you predict a specific video's views *before* posting, from content? | Martin et al. 2016 (852M tweets); Salganik 2006 | **No.** Ceiling R²≈0.48 with full features; >52% of variance irreducible. |
| Can you predict it from the first hours after posting? | Ormerod & Glass 2009; Berkovsky 2015 | **Yes, well.** Winner identifiable at 3–8% of process; AUC 0.88–0.95. |
| Can you predict an *account's median* performance? | Guinaudeau et al. 2022 | **Partly.** Followers alone give R²=0.56 for median plays on TikTok. |
| Can you predict which of an account's videos will be its best? | — | **[ABSENT].** Not studied. The 64× peak-to-median ratio suggests: essentially no. |

## 8. COMPLETE LIST OF DOCUMENTED ABSENCES

These are findings. Each was searched for deliberately.

1. **No platform has ever published a cold-start impression budget.** Not TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Meta, Kuaishou, or Google. Every "your video gets shown to 200–500 people first" claim traces to Chinese practitioner blogs with no primary source.
2. **No published study measures whether account-level topical consistency improves reach.** This is the single largest gap relative to what creator strategy assumes. Searched in EN/ZH/TH with 8+ term variants.
3. **No study measures the coefficient of variation of views within a single account at scale.** The Guinaudeau et al. peak-to-median (64×) and Gini (0.70) are the only quantitative handles that exist.
4. **No study measures what share of an account's lifetime views come from its top 1–3 videos.**
5. **No time series of view variance.** So the claim "the algorithm has become more random" is **unfalsifiable from public data**.
6. **No published exploration budget for any platform.** So "platforms are exploring more" is also unfalsifiable.
7. **Rewatch / loop rate is not documented as a ranking signal by any platform.** Universally asserted in creator education; absent from every primary source examined.
8. **No controlled experiment posting identical content from matched accounts.** The obvious study to isolate account effects from content effects has not been run publicly.
9. **Instagram has never published what fraction of followers typically see a post**, despite Mosseri publicly discussing exactly this question.
10. **TikTok's Monolith paper — the paper most often cited as "the TikTok algorithm" — contains no cold-start, no follower, and no ranking-objective content.** It is an infrastructure paper.
11. **No platform publishes the size of the Trial Reels / initial non-follower test audience**, nor the promotion threshold.
12. **US state-AG complaints against TikTok redact exactly the paragraphs describing recommendation mechanics.** North Carolina complaint ¶¶57–61 (https://ncdoj.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024.10.08-FINAL-NC-TikTok-Complaint_Redacted.pdf) reads: "The recommendation system is, in large part, composed of the following: [REDACTED]" and "Defendants' goals for the recommendation system are [REDACTED]". The one unredacted mechanical sentence: *"The For You Page provides users videos that the recommendation system predicts will keep them on the app for longer."* Litigation has not yet produced a public technical description.
13. **ByteDance patent filings do not disclose exposure quantities.** EP4418662A4 (Beijing ByteDance Network Technology, filed 2 Nov 2022, priority 18 Nov 2021, https://patents.google.com/patent/EP4418662A4/en) covers video recommendation processing but the published application does not specify cold-start mechanisms or initial exposure quantities.
14. **Thai-language sources contain no primary research.** The most technically serious Thai article found (https://everydaymarketing.co/data-ai/tiktok-algorithm-2026/) correctly describes the social-graph→vector-space shift, feature embeddings and cosine similarity, but cites one academic paper and otherwise relies on English marketing blogs; **it contains no follower-vs-views figures and no niche-consistency data.** Thai-language material on this topic is entirely derivative.

## 9. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A CREATOR WHO WANTS TO BE PREDICTABLE TO BRANDS

My honest read, with the weak points marked.

### 9.1 The core structural problem, stated precisely

A brand buying a creator is buying an **expected impression count with a tolerable variance**. The evidence says the product a short-video creator can actually supply is:

- Median video views that are **~55% predictable from follower count alone** (R²=0.56, Guinaudeau et al.) — decent.
- Individual video views where the **best video is 64× the median video** and **within-account Gini is 0.70** — catastrophic.
- With a **theoretical prediction ceiling of R²≈0.48** on any individual item's diffusion (Martin et al.).

**A creator can credibly sell a median. A creator cannot credibly sell a specific post's reach.** These are different products, and almost all creator-brand contracts are written as if they are the same one. This is the sharpest strategic implication in the report.

### 9.2 What the evidence *does* support as controllable

**Strongly supported:**
1. **Watch-completion and view-duration are the universal first-class objective.** Documented at TikTok (E·playtime term in the leaked formula, "watched in full or skipped"), YouTube Shorts ("% of viewers who chose to view, avg. view duration, avg. % viewed"), Instagram Explore, Kuaishou (EffView), Douyin (behaviour probability × value). No other craft variable has this level of cross-platform documentary support. **Duration discipline is the highest-confidence lever.** Corroborated by reach-by-duration data: Instagram Reels reach rate 5.60% at 30–60s vs 3.50% at >120s (Socialinsider, 140,000 Reels, [P-VEND]).
2. **Median-level performance is genuinely predictable and improves with audience size**, sublinearly (elasticity ~0.54 on TikTok, ~0.73 on YouTube). Audience-building is not futile; it is just a decaying-returns investment.
3. **Early signals resolve fast.** Ormerod & Glass: winners identifiable at 3–8% of a diffusion process. Metricool: 96% of a TikTok post's reach lands within 10 days. Monolith: minute-level parameter sync. **A creator can know within hours, not weeks.** Instagram's Trial Reels operationalises this. For a brand deal, the correct instrument is a **pre-tested creative** — test to non-followers, then commit.
4. **Two specific craft findings replicate at reasonable effect size:** on-screen text (67% of viral vs 30% of non-viral) and avoiding long shots (1% of viral vs 17% of non-viral). Small-N study, but the gaps are large.
5. **Posting volume protects the median** — mechanically, if each post is a draw from a Gini-0.70 distribution, more draws means a tighter estimate of the mean and more chances at the tail. Buffer's consistency data (100K+ users) is correlational and confounded, and YouTube officially denies a cadence requirement, so **treat "post more" as a statistical argument about sample size, not an algorithmic one.**

**Not supported, or contradicted:**
6. **Rewatch/loop optimisation.** No primary source documents it as a signal anywhere. [ABSENT]
7. **The "traffic pool" model (500 → 5,000 → 50,000 views with % thresholds).** Folklore. Contradicted by Douyin's own 2025 disclosure. Any Thai or Chinese strategy deck built on these numbers is built on nothing.
8. **"Build a clean account label so the algorithm knows who to show you to."** Douyin's president says on the record the system "almost does not rely on tagging content or users." This specific mental model is obsolete.

### 9.3 The niche-consistency question — my honest verdict

**The evidence is genuinely thin, and I will not pretend otherwise. Nobody has measured it.**

Arguments **for** niching, ranked by strength:
1. **IG2Vec's stated premise** (Instagram, primary source): co-engaged account sequences are topically coherent; account embeddings are validated by predicting the account's topic. A topically coherent account is machine-legible and retrievable from more seed accounts. **This is a real mechanistic argument from a real platform document — but it is from 2019, and Instagram's 2023 architecture post no longer mentions account embeddings.**
2. **Author ID is a first-class learned feature in Kuaishou's deployed ranking model** (PEPNet, KDD 2023). The account *is* a modelled entity. But the paper does not analyse what author embeddings learn or how they affect cold start.
3. **Users are narrowing into deep sub-topic bubbles** (Deep Filter Bubble: 400M interactions, level-2/3 category coverage densities concentrated between 0 and 0.1). If demand is narrow, well-defined supply is easier to route. Indirect.
4. **Creators believe it strongly** (First Monday 2026: *"If you get a niche and it's popular and you stick in that niche, you're rewarded"*). This is evidence about beliefs, not mechanics.

Arguments **against**, which are stronger than most creator-strategy documents acknowledge:
1. **Douyin explicitly disavows tagging.** If the system predicts P(behaviour) directly from a learned representation with no semantic labels, "topical clarity" has no privileged place in the objective.
2. **Hron et al. (ICLR 2023) show the return to specialisation is *decreasing* in exploration.** More exploration → broadly appealing content wins; niche creators are *discouraged*. Platforms all claim to be exploring more. If true, the niche payoff is shrinking, not growing.
3. **Every documented ranking objective is measured per-video, not per-account.** P(like), P(comment), playtime, completion, % viewed — nothing in any published objective function reads "topical coherence of this account's back catalogue."
4. **Content embeddings alone are sufficient to cold-start** (Zalando: +41%/+50% completion-rate lifts from CLIP embeddings with no creator signal at all). The routing work is being done by the *video*, not the *account*.
5. **Zero measurements exist.**

**My verdict:** the honest position is that **niche consistency is a defensible *retrieval-side* hypothesis with one good mechanistic source (IG2Vec), an actively contrary theoretical result (Hron et al.), an explicit platform disavowal of its usual folk-mechanism (Douyin on tagging), and no empirical test whatsoever.**

But — and this is the part that matters commercially — **niche consistency has a completely separate, and much better-evidenced, justification that has nothing to do with the algorithm.** A topically consistent account produces:
- a **lower-variance audience composition**, which is what makes a brand's targeting assumption hold;
- a **legible value proposition to a media buyer**, who is not buying algorithmic favour but audience definition;
- **repeatable creative** — the same format executed repeatedly reduces the variance contributed by production quality, which is the one variance component the creator actually controls.

**Recommend niche consistency as an audience-definition and variance-reduction strategy, not as an algorithm-gaming strategy.** That framing is defensible against every source in this report. The algorithmic framing is not.

### 9.4 What a creator can honestly promise a brand

Supported by evidence:
- **A median, with an explicit distribution.** "My last 30 Reels had a median of X views, an interquartile range of Y–Z, and a maximum of ~64×median." Publishing the *distribution* rather than the *average* is both more honest and, counterintuitively, more defensible — averages on a Gini-0.70 distribution are meaningless and any competent buyer knows it.
- **A defined audience composition**, which niche consistency delivers regardless of whether the algorithm rewards it.
- **A pre-tested creative** via Trial Reels or organic pre-testing, converting an unpredictable single draw into a selected one. This is the single highest-leverage practice available and it is grounded in the Ormerod & Glass early-identifiability result.
- **A portfolio, not a post.** N posts reduce the standard error of the delivered mean by √N. On a Gini-0.70 distribution, a 1-post deal is a coin flip and a 12-post deal is a forecast. **This is the most important practical recommendation in the report.**
- **Speed of resolution.** 96% of reach within 10 days means outcomes are known fast, so make-good clauses are cheap to write.

Not supported — do not promise:
- A specific view count for a specific post.
- That follower count will convert at a fixed rate (elasticity is 0.54, not 1.0, and views-per-follower falls ~10× from nano to macro).
- That the algorithm "understands" the account's niche and will therefore distribute reliably. Unmeasured, and partly contradicted.

### 9.5 Where the evidence is thin or contradictory — flag these in any document

1. **Is decoupling reversing?** Socialinsider says small TikTok accounts lost 59% of their views in 2025 while large accounts gained 39% — implying elasticity rose from ~0.54 toward ~0.93. This directly contradicts the "democratisation" thesis. **Single vendor, unaudited panel, unknown composition drift, and the vendor mislabelled its own year.** It urgently needs replication and it should be flagged as a live risk, not a conclusion.
2. **The peak-to-median 64× is from political TikTok accounts in 2020.** One vertical, one country, one pre-maturity year. It is the best number available and it is not a calibrated constant.
3. **Boeker & Urman vs the CHI 2024 data donations look contradictory but are not** — follows strongly shape *what interest clusters you're shown*, while only ~10% of what you *see* comes from accounts you follow. Any document that cites one without the other will mislead.
4. **The "Slapping Cats" AUC 0.93 will be quoted at you.** Its dominant predictor is follower count, N=400, and it selects on the dependent variable. Do not build on it.
5. **Reach decline in 2025–26 is substantially a supply phenomenon, not an algorithm phenomenon.** +72.10% content volume against −31.30% views per post (Metricool). Attributing this to "the algorithm changed" is the most common analytical error in this space.
6. **Instagram says both "creators of all sizes have an equal chance" and lists follower count as a Reels ranking signal.** Both are on the record. Treat "equal chance" as directional marketing, not a mechanism.
7. **Everything about cold-start seeding quantities is unknown.** If a strategy document states a first-pool size, it is fabricating.

# The Psychology & Behavioural Economics of Algorithmically-Mediated Creator Work — and Creator Attrition

**Research date:** 4 August 2026. All figures tagged by evidence tier: **[PEER-REVIEWED]**, **[GREY/INSTITUTIONAL]**, **[VENDOR]** (commercial party with a product to sell), **[JOURNALISM]**.

## A. VARIABLE REWARD / INTERMITTENT REINFORCEMENT

### A1. Variable-ratio reinforcement applied to *creators* — a documented near-absence

**Headline finding: the creator-side application does not exist as an empirical literature.** The variable-ratio/intermittent-reinforcement frame is applied almost exclusively to *consumers* (infinite scroll, notifications, likes received as a passive user). I could not locate a single peer-reviewed study that operationalises variable-ratio reinforcement schedules as an explanatory model for **content production** behaviour.

**Exactly what I searched (all returned only consumer-side or non-academic results):**
- `"variable ratio reinforcement schedule content creators algorithmic uncertainty research"`
- `"intermittent reinforcement" OR "variable reward" creators posting behavior slot machine algorithm academic study influencers`
- `"algorithmic uncertainty" creators experiment study behavior change posting superstition ritual "trying things" platform opacity`
- `"content creators" reinforcement learning behavior experiment views feedback loop posting decision academic economics paper creator incentives`
- `arxiv "content creators" algorithmic feedback uncertainty reinforcement learning behavior study empirical`

Returns were consistently: ABA/autism therapy explainers, UX-design blogs on habit loops, and consumer-addiction pieces — e.g. [Simply Psychology on schedules of reinforcement](https://www.simplypsychology.org/schedules-of-reinforcement.html), [Appcues on variable rewards in product design](https://www.appcues.com/blog/variable-rewards), [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/digital-world-real-world/202503/thwarting-the-social-media-algorithm-with-behavioural-science). **This is a genuine, exploitable white space.**

#### The three strongest *adjacent* evidence bases that do the work instead

**(i) Algorithmic "gamblification" of platform labour — Dubal [PEER-REVIEWED]**

Veena Dubal, *On Algorithmic Wage Discrimination*, **Columbia Law Review** 123(7), 2023.
- URL (paper): https://www.columbialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Dubal-On_Algorithmic_Wage_discrimination.pdf
- URL (open working-paper version): https://equitablegrowth.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/071223-WP-On-Algorithmic-Wage-Discrimination-Dubal.pdf
- URL (blog synopsis): https://lpeproject.org/blog/the-house-always-wins-the-algorithmic-gamblification-of-work/
- **Method:** six-year ethnography beginning 2014, San Francisco Bay Area; thousands of hours of participant observation; hundreds of drivers (Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Postmates, Wonolo). Pseudonymised.
- **Findings directly transferable to creators:**
  - Workers *most economically dependent* on the platform "frequently describe their experience of algorithmic wage discrimination through the lens of gambling."
  - Worker quote (Ben, Rideshare Drivers United): *"It's like gambling! The house always wins… This is why they give tools and remove tools."*
  - Worker quote (Dietrich): *"constant cognitive dissonance. You're free, but the app controls you. You've got it figured out, and then it all changes."*
  - Documented behavioural consequence: workers **perform the same amount of work over longer hours** while receiving lower wages — variable bonuses ("surges, quests, offers, boosts") sustain labour supply.
  - Dubal's characterisation: a practice that "preys on feelings of **hope** of vulnerable workers while limiting real possibility of economic certainty."
  - Distributional note: 69–78% of ride-hail workers identify as people of colour.
- **Caveat you must state in the BMC:** Dubal explicitly studies **gig drivers only**. She does not discuss content creators. Using this is an analogical transfer, not a direct citation.

**(ii) Skewness preference / "career lotto" — Grove, Jetter & Papps [PEER-REVIEWED]**

*Career Lotto? Labor Supply in a Superstar Market*, Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization.
- URL (working paper PDF): https://www.eur.nl/sites/corporate/files/2018-02/ecase_paper_-_career_lotto_labor_supply_in_winner-take-all_markets_-papps.pdf
- URL (journal): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167268120304856
- **Method:** longitudinal dataset of tennis players born 1977–1986 — **15,810 boys, 15,078 girls**, ages 13–30. Tennis Europe Junior Tour (U14, U16), ITF U18, WTA/ATP professional data. Probit regressions on career-continuation decisions controlling for mean, variance and **skewness** of predicted lifetime earnings.
- **Findings:**
  - Boys' probability of continuing would fall **23%** if earnings skewness fell to zero; girls' by **5%**.
  - "Boys exhibit **four times** the skewness preference of girls."
  - Skewness preferences are **20× larger** for small-stakes lotteries than for high-stakes career gambles — i.e. people accept far worse odds when the stakes are their career than when buying a lottery ticket.
- **Why it matters:** this is the cleanest quantified demonstration that a **right-skewed reward distribution itself** (not expected value) causes over-supply of labour into winner-take-all markets. The creator economy is the archetypal such market.

**(iii) Gamification and consent in platform work — Vasudevan & Chan [PEER-REVIEWED]**

*Gamification and work games: Examining consent and resistance among Uber drivers*, **New Media & Society**, 2022.
- URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/14614448221079028
- Open PDF: https://www.com.cuhk.edu.hk/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/oliver-journal-2022-gamification.pdf

#### The quantified reward distribution creators actually face [PEER-REVIEWED / PREPRINT]

Strauss, Yang & Mazzucato, *"Rich-Get-Richer"? Platform Attention and Earnings Inequality using Patreon Earnings Data*, arXiv:2509.26523v3 (posted 15 July 2026; UCL IIPP).
- URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26523
- **Data:** n = **104,719 creators**; three cross-sections (March 2018, March 2021, March 2024); official Patreon monthly earnings data **purchased directly from the platform**; restricted to creators earning ≥$10/month, single-platform affiliation.
- **Findings (monthly USD, pooled years):**

| Platform | Median | Mean | Q25 | Q75 | Max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Facebook | $47 | $149 | $30 | $99 | $35,261 |
| Instagram | $59 | $226 | $33 | $142 | $60,391 |
| Patreon | $57 | $278 | $31 | $154 | $211,321 |
| Twitch | $46 | $198 | $29 | $105 | $31,436 |
| Twitter/X | $72 | $373 | $34 | $215 | $169,106 |
| YouTube | $47 | $250 | $28 | $119 | $87,802 |

  - Power-law exponent α (lower = more unequal), 2024: YouTube **1.8**, Instagram 1.84, Twitch 1.93, Facebook 1.94, Patreon 2.24, Twitter/X 2.35.
  - YouTube α fell from ~1.9 (2018) → 1.8 (2024); Instagram from ~2.1 (2021) → 1.84 (2024). **Inequality is worsening.**
  - **Most striking finding:** the power-law (extreme-inequality) regime begins at just **$26–$70/month** — versus $100,000+ in standard income distributions. Winner-take-all dynamics start almost immediately above zero.
- **Limitations the authors state:** three snapshots, not individual longitudinal tracking; no cohort survival analysis; earnings confounded as Y = attention × conversion × revenue-per-conversion. **No Gini coefficients, no churn data.**

### A2. Algorithmic imaginary / folklore / gossip — what these studies actually document

**Bucher (2017) — the founding paper [PEER-REVIEWED]**

Taina Bucher, *The algorithmic imaginary: exploring the ordinary affects of Facebook algorithms*, **Information, Communication & Society** 20(1), 30–44.
- URL: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1154086
- Summary: https://montrealethics.ai/the-algorithmic-imaginary-exploring-the-ordinary-affects-of-facebook-algorithms-research-summary/
- **Method:** Twitter posts about the Facebook algorithm + email-based interviews. *Sample size is not clearly reported in accessible versions — treat as small-n qualitative.*
- **Definition:** the algorithmic imaginary = "folk theories and subjective understandings of how algorithms operate" — mental models that are **consequential regardless of accuracy**.
- **Findings:** discomfort at being mis-categorised; **active resistance** (deliberately trying to make the algorithm *less* accurate); anger; strategic behavioural adaptation. Core claim: imaginaries "shape social life" and "drive tangible changes in user behaviour."

**Bishop (2019) — algorithmic gossip, the single most useful paper for your service [PEER-REVIEWED]**

Sophie Bishop, *Managing visibility on YouTube through algorithmic gossip*, **New Media & Society** 21(11–12), 2589–2606.
- Full PDF: https://sophiebishop.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/bishop-algorithmic-gossip.pdf
- Journal: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444819854731
- **Method — precise:** three-year ethnography, **2015–2018**. Online ethnography tracking **30+ UK beauty vloggers** (min. 10,000 subscribers); offline ethnography at vlogging events; **10 semi-structured interviews with British beauty/lifestyle vloggers** (full-time or previously full-time) plus **9 industry intermediaries** (talent agents, a Twitter community manager, the Internet Creators Guild director). - **Definition:** algorithmic gossip = *"communally and socially informed theories and strategies pertaining to recommender algorithms, shared and implemented to engender financial consistency and visibility on algorithmically structured social media platforms."*
- **Findings:**
  - Official platform channels are seen as useless — one creator on contacting YouTube: *"about as useful as a choco[late teapot]."* On shadowbanning: *"they are absolutely insistent it doesn't happen."*

  - Content narrowing under algorithmic pressure: Astrid reports beauty content gets **"5× more views than anything else,"** pushing her back into a niche she'd tried to leave.
  - Visibility described as **"precarious"**; the algorithm "hasn't been so kind since."
- **What this documents about coping:** creators substitute **peer-generated folklore for information the platform withholds**, and they do it socially, in private networks. That is exactly the demand your advisory service would be substituting for.

**Bishop (2018) — algorithmic anxiety and self-optimisation [PEER-REVIEWED]**

*Anxiety, panic and self-optimization: Inequalities and the YouTube algorithm*, **Convergence** 24(1), 69–84.

**Cotter (2019) — the visibility game [PEER-REVIEWED]**

### B5. Algorithmic unpredictability as a *distinct* stressor

This is the best-supported causal claim in the whole report, and it holds across independent sources:

1. **Vibely (n=150, 2021):** algorithm changes ranked **#1** cause of burnout at **65%**, ahead of making a living (59%) and harassment (42%).
2. **Awin (n=300+, 2024):** "constant platform changes" ranked **#1** at **70%**, ahead of creativity (55%) and negative comments (43%).
3. **Later (n=600+, 2023):** 23% cite constant algorithm adaptation specifically.
4. **Nguyen & Duffy (2026), academic:** the mechanism is identified qualitatively as *"If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm"* — algorithmic contingency directly **blocks recovery**, which is what converts stress into burnout.
5. **Duffy et al. (2021):** "platform precarity" is theorised as analytically separate from market and industry precarity precisely because algorithm changes are **opaque** where feature changes are transparent.
6. **Are & Briggs (2023)** (below): documents anticipatory anxiety about deletion as an ongoing state distinct from the deletion event itself.

**The distinction that matters commercially:** workload is *solvable by doing less*; harassment is *solvable by moderation and distance*. Algorithmic unpredictability is solvable by **neither** — and it is the only one of the three that punishes rest. That is a structural argument for an ongoing advisory relationship rather than a one-off course.

**Counter-signal, and you should carry it:** in the 2026 Manychat survey (n=1,000 creators), algorithm changes had fallen to **#7** of ranked challenges, displaced by competition with AI-generated content. If replicated, the salience of "the algorithm" as *the* named creator anxiety may be declining relative to AI displacement anxiety.

**Related peer-reviewed work on de-platforming anxiety:** Are, C., Briggs, P. & Brown, R., *Content creators' hopes and fears about artificial intelligence*, **Convergence**, 2025 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13548565251372830

### B6. Always-on production pressure — and whether frequency actually works

**Measured time burden:**
- **~20 hrs/week** on planning, filming and editing alone — Manychat 2026, n=1,000 creators. Excludes community management, brand admin, analytics. [VENDOR]
- Linktree 2022 (n=9,576): **43% spend ≤5 hrs/week**; those earning $0–100/yr spend <5 hrs/week. [VENDOR]

**Does more posting work? The evidence says no, past a threshold.**

**[PEER-REVIEWED]** Tafesse, W. & Dayan, M. (2023). *Content creators' participation in the creator economy: Examining the effect of creators' content sharing frequency on user engagement behavior on digital platforms.* **Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services**, 73, 103357.
- URL: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joreco/v73y2023ics0969698923001042.html
- DOI: 10.1016/j.jretconser.2023.103357
- **Finding:** an **inverted-U relationship**. "As the frequency of creators' content sharing increases, user engagement behavior initially increases but then falls after reaching a peak."
- **Moderators:** follower count, following count and number of competitors all **negatively moderate** the inverted-U — i.e. the bigger and more contested you are, the *weaker* the benefit of optimal frequency. Grounded in two-factor theory.
- **Caveat:** exact n and platform were not recoverable from the accessible abstract (full text paywalled, ScienceDirect blocks fetch). Verify before quoting effect sizes.

**[VENDOR]** AIR Media-Tech, *Does posting more on YouTube help? We studied 18,000 channels.*
- URL: https://air.io/en/audience-growth/does-posting-more-on-youtube-help-we-studied-18000-channels-to-find-out
- **Method:** 18,000 English-language channels, 11 niches, 12-month snapshot; US/UK/CA/AU; Shorts, news and live-heavy channels excluded; viral outliers (>10× channel average) excluded; primary analysis on the 100K–1M monthly-views segment.
- **Findings:** frequency correlates positively with subscribers **only** in gaming and kids' content (20–40 videos/month). In most other niches the relationship **inverts**. "Channels that over-posted had **55% fewer subscribers** than channels posting at the optimal rate" — explicitly reported for Health & Fitness, with over-posting penalties also in Science & Tech, Education, Business and Food. **Consistency beat volume:** channels posting on a predictable schedule got more views per subscriber at identical video counts.
- **Caveat:** correlational, cross-sectional, no causal identification, and AIR sells channel audits off the back of it.

**Implication:** the "always-on" imperative that drives creator burnout is **not supported** by either the peer-reviewed or the largest vendor dataset. Both find an optimum and a penalty beyond it. This is a defensible, evidence-backed advisory position — and it is the opposite of what most creator advice says.

#### Partial falsifiers — three near-misses, each of which strengthens rather than refutes the point

**(1) The strongest counterexample — but exit is involuntary [PEER-REVIEWED]**

Are, C. & Briggs, P. (2023). *The Emotional and Financial Impact of De-Platforming on Creators at the Margins.* **Social Media + Society**, Jan–Mar 2023, 1–12. DOI: 10.1177/20563051231155103
- Open PDF: https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/85672301/20563051231155103.pdf
- Journal: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20563051231155103
- **Method:** anonymous qualitative survey over 3 months, circulated via the lead author's own networks (350,000+ followers across Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, TikTok) plus promotion by adult-industry outlet XBIZ. **123 respondents**; **98** reported both censorship and negative comments on Instagram/TikTok; **41** gave detailed responses. Inclusion required experiencing both negative comments and account/content deletion.
- **Findings — this is the only paper I found containing first-person accounts of ceasing creative work:**
  - *"I am forever finished doing art full time."*
  - *"My depression has been triggered so intensely… I barely paint or create at all anymore."*
  - *"I have given up on Instagram entirely."*
  - *"I don't bother using IG anymore"* — cited lack of time and energy.
  - *"It was truly a horrendous grieving process… I lost my whole community, friends and contacts. I felt very isolated."*
  - *"The situation just leaves me feeling like I'm not good enough for a platform that I put so much heart and soul into."*

- **Why this doesn't overturn the hypothesis:** the sampling frame is *de-platformed* creators — people expelled by the platform. It captures **involuntary, event-driven exit**, not the far more common quiet fade. It also recruits via the researcher's own large following, which selects for the still-connected.

**(2) Bishop (2019)** included **1 ex-vlogger no longer producing** and **3 who downshifted** from full-time (out of 10). Incidental inclusion, not a leaver-focused design — but note that Bishop is the only algorithm-and-creators author who put a leaver in the room at all.

**(3) Creative PEC (2025)** does exactly the right study — for arts/culture/heritage *employees*, not platform creators. It works because it samples via the **Labour Force Survey**, not via a platform.

#### Why this absence is structural, not accidental

Every creator dataset in this report is recruited through a channel that requires the respondent to **still be there**:
- Vibely, Later, Manychat, Awin, Linktree → the vendor's own user base.
- Duffy et al. (2021, 2022), Duffy & Hund (2019), Bishop (2019), Cotter (2019) → recruited via active hashtags, press coverage, Reddit posts, influencer hashtags, snowball from actives.
- CreatorMap → an API that only returns extant channels; cohort defined by *already having 10K subs*.
- El Sanyoura & Anderson → complete platform data, but analysed patron retention and not creator exit.

A person who stopped posting in 2023 is, by construction, unreachable through any of these frames. **The creator-economy evidence base has no exit interview.**

### D11. Does having a clear niche predict LONGEVITY? — a documented absence

**No study tests whether topical focus predicts creator persistence.** Searched: `"niche" specialization creator longevity persistence predicts survival channel topic focus study empirical YouTube`. Every return was vendor SEO content — [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/best-youtube-niches/), [TubeBuddy](https://www.tubebuddy.com/blog/choosing-youtube-niche/), [OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/blog/best-youtube-niches-for-beginners), [AIR](https://air.io/en/trending/should-you-stay-in-one-niche-or-experiment-pros-and-cons-for-creators). All assert that niching aids growth; none measures survival; none is causal.

**The two nearest pieces of real evidence:**

**(a) [PEER-REVIEWED — closest analogue, but not creators]** Wang, S., Macy, M. & Nee, V. (2023). *Specialization in the marketplace for ideas.* **PLOS ONE** 18(10): e0293355. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0293355
- **Data:** 1,504 technology-focused Meetup groups listing multiple topics, NYC metro, **2005–2019**.
- **Method:** cross-sectional linear regression **plus Cox proportional hazards survival analysis** — it explicitly models survival, which is what you want.
- **Findings — and they cut against naive "pick a niche" advice:**
  - **Narrow topical focus alone predicted *worse* outcomes.** +1 SD in narrowness → **−13.7 pp** in event attendees (p<0.01) and **−1.4 pp** retention (p<0.01).
  - **Technical exclusivity was the strongest positive predictor**, regardless of breadth: +1 SD → +0.248 logged attendees (p<0.01), **+4.311 pp retention** (p<0.01), and **−0.160 hazard ratio for inactivity** (p<0.01) — i.e. it directly predicts *survival*.
  - Rarity: +1.98 pp retention (p<0.01) but −0.086 on attracting new members (p<0.01).
  - Novelty: **−16.3 pp** attendance (p<0.01), worst in narrowly focused groups.
  - **Authors' conclusion:** *"What matters is not how specialized the organization, but how the organization is specialized."*
- **Transfer caveat:** Meetup groups, not creators. But it is the only survival-analytic evidence on specialisation I could find in any attention market, and it says **narrowness alone is harmful while defensible distinctiveness is protective.**

**(b) [VENDOR, weak]** Linktree 2022: 7% of niche creators earn >$100K vs 5% of non-niche. Cross-sectional, earnings not longevity, 2pp difference, no controls.

**(c) [VENDOR]** AIR's 18,000-channel study finds **schedule consistency** outperforms volume — an adjacent form of "focus" that predicts views-per-subscriber.

**Bottom line: the single most commonly sold piece of creator advice — "find your niche" — has no published evidence linking it to persistence.** It has weak cross-sectional evidence linking it to earnings, and the best available survival evidence from an analogous market says *narrowness per se hurts* while *distinctiveness helps*.

- J-PAL PDF: https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/The-Impact-of-Consulting-Services-on-SME_Schoar-et-al_March2018.pdf
- SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2285974

- **The commercially uncomfortable reading:** the effect took **five years** to appear, and at a delivered cost of ~$11,856/year with 70–90% subsidy. A creator paying full freight out of a median $50/month income cannot replicate these conditions, and cannot wait five years.

## THE THREE FINDINGS MOST USEFUL TO SOMEONE SELLING ADVISORY SERVICES TO CREATORS

**1. Algorithmic unpredictability is the #1 self-reported cause of creator burnout across independent surveys — and it is the only major stressor that punishes rest.**

Vibely (n=150, 2021) ranks algorithm changes first at **65%**, above income (59%) and harassment (42%). Awin (n=300+, 2024, four countries) independently ranks "constant platform changes" first at **70%**. Later (n=600+, 2023) finds 23% cite constant algorithm adaptation. And Nguyen & Duffy's peer-reviewed 2026 study (78 interviews) supplies the mechanism in creators' own words: *"If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm."*

This is a commercially perfect problem. Workload is solved by doing less. Harassment is solved by distance and moderation. Algorithmic contingency is solved by **neither** — and it converts the ordinary remedy for burnout (rest) into a perceived cost. Meanwhile Bishop (2019) documents that creators currently meet this need through *algorithmic gossip*: unverifiable peer folklore, traded privately, of the calibre of "the algorithm rewards 15-minute videos." Cotter (2019) shows they build "shared interpretive frameworks rather than achieving algorithmic literacy," and Duffy & Meisner (2022) find that a substantial coping mode is outright **resignation** (*"I've… not even really try anymore"*). Your service substitutes structured judgement for folklore, in the one domain where the customer already knows they are guessing and already knows the guessing is hurting them. Position the offer as **decision-making under irreducible uncertainty** — not as algorithm decoding, which would be a promise you cannot keep and which the literature shows nobody can keep.

**2. "Post more" is contradicted by both the peer-reviewed and the largest vendor evidence — which gives you a differentiated, evidence-backed contrarian position.**

Tafesse & Dayan (2023, *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*) find an **inverted-U**: engagement rises with posting frequency, peaks, then **falls**. The moderators sharpen it — follower count, following count and competitor density all *weaken* the benefit, meaning the bigger and more contested the creator, the less volume helps. AIR Media-Tech's 18,000-channel study finds over-posting channels had **55% fewer subscribers** than those at their niche optimum, with penalties across Science & Tech, Education, Business and Food; only gaming and kids' content rewarded high volume. And it found **schedule consistency outperformed volume** at identical output.

Set this against Manychat's finding that creators already spend **~20 hours a week** on production alone, and Duffy et al.'s Grace spending *"a complete hour right after I post"* manually gaming distribution. The always-on imperative is simultaneously the primary driver of burnout **and** unsupported by evidence. Almost every competing course and coach sells the opposite. A 1:1 advisor who can credibly tell a Thai creator "you are posting too much, here is the data, here is your optimum, here is what to do with the reclaimed 8 hours" is selling something that is (a) true, (b) immediately relieving, (c) differentiated, and (d) demonstrable within one quarter — which matters because it is the fastest route to a renewal.

**3. The advice market is built entirely on survivors, and you can be the only person in the room who says so.**

This is the report's strongest strategic asset. There is **no exit interview in the creator economy.** Ten distinct search strategies (listed verbatim in C8) surfaced not one study of voluntary quitters. Every dataset recruits through a channel requiring the respondent to still be active: vendor user bases (Vibely, Later, Manychat, Awin, Linktree), active hashtags and Reddit (Duffy, Bishop, Cotter, Duffy & Meisner), or APIs that only return extant accounts (CreatorMap). Even El Sanyoura & Anderson, holding **complete Patreon pledge data 2013–2020**, analysed *patron* retention and never computed creator exit. Both field-defining agenda papers — Peres et al. (IJRM 2023) and Maddox (2026) — omit attrition from their research priorities entirely.

Meanwhile the attrition itself is brutal and well-attested: **~78% of Twitch streamers gone within a year, ~88% within two**; **74.8% of a 2019 cohort of YouTube channels that already had 10,000+ subscribers** is now dormant, fading or gone; **59% of sub-12-month creators have never monetised at all**.

Two commercial moves follow. First, honest positioning: *"Every course you have been sold was written by someone who survived. Nobody has ever asked the people who stopped why they stopped. I will not pretend to know the winning formula — I will help you make good decisions under a reward system nobody controls."* In a market saturated with guru certainty, calibrated honesty is a differentiator, and it is defensible because it is true. Second, and more valuably: **you are positioned to generate the missing data.** Running structured exit conversations with Thai creators who have stopped — even 30 of them — would produce the only such dataset in existence, in any language. That is proprietary IP, a genuine marketing asset, and a publishable contribution.

## THE TWO FINDINGS THAT MOST THREATEN THAT BUSINESS

**1. Almost no creator can afford advisory pricing, and the ones who can afford it are the ones who least need it.**

The income data is unambiguous and it comes from the largest datasets available. Strauss, Yang & Mazzucato (n=104,719, Patreon-supplied data): **median monthly earnings of $46–$72** across every major platform. Linktree (n=9,576): **46% of self-described *full-time* creators earn under $1,000 per year**; 68% of part-timers; **67% have never done a brand deal**; **59% of creators under 12 months have not monetised at all**. In Thailand specifically, nano-influencers earn **3,000–5,000 THB per post**, and the fastest-growing entrant cohort is **45–50 year-olds who turned to content creation after layoffs** — entering under duress, with no capital.

The Patreon paper's sharpest finding compounds this: **power-law inequality begins at $26–$70/month**, versus $100,000+ in normal income distributions. There is essentially no middle. And CreatorMap's size-stratified survival data shows the same shape in persistence terms — **11.4% of 10K–50K channels survived seven years vs 66.7% of 1M+ channels**. The people with money to spend are the macro creators who already have managers, agencies, and platform partner support. The 9–11 million Thai creators below them constitute an enormous TAM and a tiny SAM.

There is no independent data anywhere on creator willingness-to-pay for coaching — I searched four separate ways and found only vendor material with no price points, no conversion rates, and no outcomes. **You would be pricing into a void.** The realistic implication is that a viable Thai 1:1 advisory business is probably serving the top single-digit percentage by income, or is being paid by someone other than the creator (brands, agencies, MCNs, platforms), or is a low-ticket group product with a 1:1 tier — not a mass 1:1 offer.

**2. The one rigorous causal study of 1:1 advisory found weak short-run effects and a five-year lag to the real payoff — which is longer than most of your clients will still be creating.**

Bruhn, Karlan & Schoar's RCT (n=432 Mexican SMEs, *Journal of Political Economy*) is the gold-standard evidence for exactly this service model: **one year of 1:1 consulting, four hours per week**. At one-year follow-up: productivity +0.21 SD and ROA +0.22 SD, both only at **p<0.10**; **profits not robustly significant; no significant effect on employment or sales**. The large, unambiguous effects — +57% employment, +72% wage bill — appeared at **five years**, and the mechanism ran through slow process adoption (accounting +8pp, marketing +13pp), not through the kind of visible growth a creator will renew a retainer for. Full delivered cost was **~US$11,856/year**, with firms paying only 10–30%.

Now collide that with the attrition data: **~78% of Twitch streamers are gone within a year; ~88% within two.** The intervention's payoff horizon is roughly triple the survival horizon of the median client. Worse, the adjacent literature on absorptive capacity finds consulting effects are **conditional on the firm's existing capacity to absorb advice** — which is itself a survivorship mechanism operating inside your own service.

Three concrete exposures follow. **(a) Attribution is near-impossible.** Under a right-skewed, algorithmically mediated reward system, a client's outcome is dominated by variance you do not control. Success will be over-attributed to you and failure under-attributed — which feels fine until a cohort of clients churns and word travels. **(b) Your own testimonials will be survivorship-biased in exactly the way you are criticising in others.** If you use finding #3 as positioning while your case studies come only from clients who happened to survive, that is a coherence failure a sophisticated buyer will notice, and it is also just true. **(c) The honest version of your value proposition may be unsellable at the price the work costs.** "I will help you make better decisions under uncertainty, the payoff is uncertain and slow, and most people in your position stop within a year" is accurate and hard to charge for. Any pitch that resolves this tension by promising growth is selling the same aspirational labour the literature documents — and Duffy's core finding is that aspirational labour's defining feature is that **the promised reward is always deferred and rarely arrives.**
