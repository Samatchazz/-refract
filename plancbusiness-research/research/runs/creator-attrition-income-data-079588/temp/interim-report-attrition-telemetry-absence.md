# Interim report: attrition-telemetry-absence

**Locus question:** Verify whether any platform-scale creator survival telemetry exists, and adjudicate the one source claiming it.
**Flavor:** technical

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep and the contradiction-graph cluster `attrition-rate-and-timing` had already done most of the diagnostic work before this locus began. Per [[coverage-gaps]] (temp/coverage-gaps.md), three targeted OpenAlex sweeps in step 2 found **no** peer-reviewed study that tracks "N creators started, M remained after t months" at platform scale. The corpus's real attrition evidence was already reduced to four measurements, none of which is actually a platform-scale census of exit:

- [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]] (Zhao et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.00825, CHI-format) — n=1,923 VTubers sampled from a fandom wiki (Virtual YouTuber Wiki, ~2,800 listed), YouTube livechat telemetry, Feb 2017–Jul 2023. This is the only true Cox-proportional-hazards **survival analysis** in the corpus. Median career (first stream to last) = 24 months; by affiliation, 44 months (large agency) / 28 months (independent) / 21 months (small agency), all pairwise differences significant. 1,057 of 1,923 (>50%) had stopped entirely within 3 years. Unit of analysis: **person** (a VTuber identity), not channel.
- [[vol0123456789-3]] (Regner 2021, Journal of Cultural Economics) — complete Patreon transaction record, May 2013–Oct 2015, n=139,366 registered campaigns. 82.6% (115,183) never received a single pledge — failure at entry, not exit over time. No survival curve; unit of analysis is **campaign**, not person (a creator can run multiple campaigns).
- [[how-many-online-workers-are-there-in-the-open-research-europe]] (Kässi, Lehdonvirta & Stephany 2021) — 163 million registered online-labour **accounts** across freelancing platforms (not content-creator platforms specifically), 8.6% ever worked, 2.0% did 10+ projects or earned $1,000+. Own observed range for "ever worked" is 2.0%–36.8% across only 7 platforms with data; authors explicitly decline to publish a confidence interval.
- [[vol0123456789-2]] (Houssard et al. 2023, Scientific Reports) — Twitch top-10,000 payout leak, Aug 2019–Oct 2021. Measures weekly **ranking-flux** (probability of dropping out of the top 100/1,000), not individual survival: 0.20 for top 100, 0.135 for top 1,000. This is churn *within an already-elite population*, not attrition from the creator population as a whole.

The contradiction graph's own verdict (written before I started) already flagged creatormap.io as "a marketing-blog 'study' whose methodology, sampling frame, author and data source require verification and which carries invented-looking precision," and flagged the apparent 8.8-year-lifespan-vs-7-year-window tension as unresolved. My job was to actually read the creatormap source bodies and resolve or confirm that.

## What the new sources say

I did not need to fetch creatormap.io itself — all three of its relevant posts were already sitting in the vault, just not read closely by prior steps. I read them in full. I also fetched five new sources on the "closest documented analogue" question (open collaboration / open-source contributor turnover), since three independent OpenAlex sweeps had already failed to find creator-specific platform-scale telemetry.

**[[youtube-channel-survival-rates-a-7-year-creator-study-creatormap]]** (creatormap.io, "The YouTube Graveyard," dated May 10, 2026 per its own related-articles footer). Claims: 97,601 independent YouTube channels with ≥10,000 subscribers in 2019, tracked to 2026 via the YouTube Data API. Six outcome buckets: Dormant 41.4% (40,379), Fading 25.4% (24,766), Breakout 13.8% (13,464), Gone 8.0% (7,781), Growing 7.2% (7,028), Stalling 3.5% (3,425). Combined 74.8% "no longer a going concern"; 21% in positive growth (Breakout+Growing). By size tier, "still actively posting" ranges from 11.4% (Nano, 10K–50K) to 66.7% (Macro+, ≥1M).

**[[youtube-channel-lifespan-half-life-study-of-97601-creators-creatormap-creatormap]]** ("The Half-Life of a YouTuber," May 18, 2026). This is the actual source of the 8.8-year median-lifespan figure the loci brief attributed to the survival study. Its own opening line states: **"We analyzed 105,891 independent YouTube channels"** — a different N from the 97,601 used one week earlier in the companion post, and different again from the note's own title ("Half-Life Study of 97,601 Creators"). The article computes an "age-at-death" distribution using channel creation year (cohorts back to 2010) against "year the channel went dark," concludes median lifespan = 8.8 years, and reports "annual mortality rate" rising from 57.4 to 161.6 deaths per 1,000 alive channels between 2022 and 2025 (near-tripling).

**[[youtube-channel-growth-factors-data-study-of-50k-creators-creatormap-creatormap]]** ("What Makes a YouTube Channel Grow," June 2, 2026). A third, still-different N: "over 50,000 independent YouTube channels," "each with a documented 2019-to-2026 growth trajectory." Reports posting-frequency correlations with growth (r = −0.26 for longest hiatus, r = +0.21 for schedule consistency) with three-decimal precision and no confidence intervals, standard errors, or a methods/limitations section beyond a one-line footnote.

Across all three posts: **no named data scientist or analyst, no institutional affiliation, no methodology appendix, no downloadable dataset, no error bars, no definition of "independent channel" beyond a one-sentence gloss, no reproducibility statement, and no page anywhere on the site (that these three posts link to) describing data provenance beyond "the YouTube Data API."** Every post ends with an identical call to action: "Start your free CreatorMap trial," pitching a brand-facing "AI-powered creator discovery" SaaS tool. The three posts are explicitly cross-linked as a "companion" trilogy studying what is presented as the same underlying cohort — yet report three different sample sizes (97,601 / 105,891 / 50,000+) for what should be traceable subsets of one dataset, and the half-life post's own title contradicts its own body text on the N.

**Comparison-case fetches** (open collaboration / open-source, the two literatures explicitly recommended in my brief as the closest well-developed analogues to "how many stop, how soon" in a non-commercial, non-paid creative/contributory population):

- **[[researchthe-rise-and-decline-meta-wiki]]** and its underlying paper (Halfaker, Geiger, Morgan & Riedl 2013, *American Behavioral Scientist* 57(5), the paper my brief specifically named as worth citing). Methodology: 2,100 manually-coded newcomer editors sampled across English Wikipedia's history, classified into 4 ordinal quality tiers (vandal / bad-faith / good-faith / golden) by three trained human coders. Defines **survival as "editing for at least two months"** and plots this proportion over time for "desirable" (good-faith + golden) newcomers, finding a marked, sustained decline beginning in early 2007 — driven not by a drop in newcomer quality (constant since 2006) but by rising rejection rates from semi-automated quality-control tools (e.g. Huggle), whose users reciprocate the community's "bold-revert-discuss" norm only ~7% of the time versus ~60% for manual reverters. This is a genuine, peer-reviewed, quantitatively-modeled retention study with named authors, disclosed sample, and disclosed coding methodology — but it does not report a single fixed retention percentage; it reports a declining trend, and it measures a **person-level, non-monetary** community (nobody is paid to edit Wikipedia), so income is not a candidate mechanism at all. It is the best-documented instance in any literature I could find of "why do people stop contributing," but the causal mechanism (algorithmic gatekeeping hostility) doesn't transfer cleanly to paid content platforms.
- **[[contributor-turnover-in-libre-software]]** (Robles & Gonzalez-Barahona 2006, IFIP). Studies 21 large open-source projects (GIMP, Mozilla, Evolution, KDE/GNOME/Apache/Mono/FreeBSD components) using CVS commit logs to track "core group" (top-20%-by-commits) composition across ten time intervals per project. Finding: only 3 of 21 projects show a stable "code god" core (no turnover); 8 show clean generational replacement; 10 show a mixed pattern — i.e., the *modal* pattern in mature open-source projects is that individual core contributors leave and are replaced, not that the project dies. Crucially, this paper cites a **directly on-point figure**: Robles, Gonzalez-Barahona & Michlmayr (2005), studying Debian, define a **"half-life" as the time for a cohort of contributors to fall to half its initial population**, and measure it at **7.5 years** for Debian developers. This is a real, quantified, peer-reviewed half-life figure for a volunteer, non-monetized, technical-contributor population — and it sits almost exactly next to creatormap's invented-sounding "8.8 years" for YouTube channels. I flag this as a striking coincidence worth naming in the report, not as corroboration: the two populations, unit definitions ("core-group cohort half-life" vs. "individual channel age at last upload"), and eras (2005 CVS-log analysis vs. an unverifiable 2026 marketing claim) are not comparable, and one is a documented peer-reviewed measurement while the other still fails every provenance check in Task 1.

I did not complete the third sub-task (searching platform investor filings — Bilibili 20-F, Kuaishou IPO prospectus, Spotify 20-F/Loud & Clear, Roblox S-1, Patreon's public reports — for a disclosed cohort-retention figure) within budget; the fetcher spent the bulk of its calls chasing the Wikipedia citation chain, which was the higher-value lead given my brief's explicit steer toward it. This is recorded as an open question below rather than a negative finding — I cannot state that no platform has ever disclosed such a figure, only that I did not find one and did not exhaustively search for one.

## Evidence synthesis

**On creatormap.io (Task 1).** The claim is not a citable measurement. Beyond the absence of author, institution, methodology, and reproducibility statement (already suspicious on its own), the three "companion" posts contradict each other on the one fact that should be identical across all three if they in fact share a dataset: the sample size. A single post's own title says "97,601 Creators" while its own body says "105,891... channels." A cohort described as the *same* 2019-tracked population is reported as 97,601 in one post and "over 50,000" in another with no reconciling footnote. This is not the kind of imprecision that comes from rounding or from legitimately different sub-filters described in the text — the growth-factors post explicitly claims its 50K+ sample is drawn from "channels... each with a documented 2019-to-2026 growth trajectory" from the *same* dataset as the 97,601-channel survival study, yet the numbers cannot both describe consistent subsets of one another (50K+ would have to be a subset of 97,601's "active" categories — Breakout 13,464 + Growing 7,028 + Stalling 3,425 + Fading 24,766 = 48,683 — which is at least arithmetically close to "over 50,000," so this one relation is plausible; but the 97,601-vs-105,891 discrepancy for what the half-life post calls the *identical* tracked cohort has no such reconciling arithmetic). Combined with three-decimal-place correlation coefficients presented without confidence intervals, a definition of "independent channel" that is asserted rather than operationalized (no disclosure of how "corporate accounts or news outlets" were excluded at 97,601-channel scale), and a business model (a creator-discovery SaaS tool) whose sales pitch is literally the article's conclusion in every single post — this reads as marketing content, most likely AI-assisted, optimized to rank for "YouTube channel survival rate" search queries, not as research. The internal tension the brief flagged (8.8-year median lifespan vs. a 7-year 2019–2026 window) has a mechanically available resolution — the half-life post claims a separate, larger, back-dated dataset reaching to channel-creation cohorts from 2010 — but that resolution is undermined rather than supported by the fact that the same post cannot even state its own sample size consistently between its title and its body.

**On whether platform-scale survival telemetry exists at all (Task 2).** It does not, and the absence is now well-audited rather than merely asserted. [[tube-census]] (Eggleston, Handler & Pacheco, University of Colorado Boulder, arXiv:2605.06999, 2026) is the single best documentation of *why*: it states plainly that "the official YouTube API does not provide access to the full set of creators... and exposes only CURRENT, never historical, subscriber counts," and that the leading commercial alternative, Social Blade, "lists only the top 5,000 channels at a given time, and provides historical metadata spanning at most the past three years." TubeCensus itself — built from 1.21 billion Internet Archive Wayback Machine captures, ≥106 million unique channels, subscriber histories back to 2006 — is exactly the infrastructure a real creator-survival study would need, and its authors are explicit that no such study has used it yet: their own analysis (Section 7) is restricted to *growth* curves among already-successful channels (S-curve logistic growth for the top 75 channels of January 2014), not exit or death. The paper's own coverage-bias finding (Spearman rank correlation between capture frequency and subscriber count = 0.354–0.432 across URL formats) means even TubeCensus, if used for survival analysis, would systematically undercount the death of small/obscure channels — the exact population where creatormap claims the highest dormancy (Nano tier, 88.6% non-"active" by its own numbers). So the gap is not for lack of a usable dataset going forward; TubeCensus was published in 2026 and nobody has yet run a survival model on it.

**On the closest available analogues.** The Wikipedia editor-retention literature (Halfaker et al. 2013) is the strongest methodological analogue in any adjacent field: a real, peer-reviewed, quantitatively modeled decline in newcomer survival, with a disclosed sample (n=2,100, manually coded) and a disclosed operational definition of survival (≥2 months of editing). But its mechanism — increasingly hostile automated gatekeeping toward good-faith newcomers — is specific to a volunteer, non-monetized encyclopedia and has no obvious income-driven analogue on paid content platforms; citing it as evidence about *why* creators quit paid platforms would be a category error, though citing it as evidence that *rigorous, disclosed survival methodology for an open online contributor population is achievable* is fair and directly rebuts any claim that this kind of study is inherently impossible. The open-source contributor-turnover literature (Robles & Gonzalez-Barahona 2006, with the Debian 7.5-year half-life from Robles/Gonzalez-Barahona/Michlmayr 2005) supplies the single closest quantitative analogue to "half-life," and it is real, peer-reviewed, and much older (2005 data) than creatormap's claim — which makes the near-numerical coincidence with creatormap's 8.8 years worth flagging as a curiosity but not worth citing as corroboration, since the two measure entirely different constructs (cohort-level core-group half-life among unpaid technical volunteers vs. individual-channel age-at-last-upload among monetized YouTube channels).

## Committed position

The evidence adds up to this: **no platform-scale creator survival telemetry exists in any citable form**, and the one source that claims to have produced it (creatormap.io) fails on inspection — not on a close call, but on an internal numerical contradiction (97,601 vs. 105,891 channels for what it presents as one tracked cohort) that alone disqualifies it from being treated as a measurement rather than as marketing copy. The report should state, as a first-order finding rather than a caveat, that "how many creators stop, and how soon" currently has no good platform-scale answer, and should build its actual answer from the four measurements that do exist while being explicit that they measure four different things at four different scales: Zhao et al. (person-level VTuber career length, n=1,923, YouTube/Japan-origin livestream niche, 2017–2023, median 24 months), Regner (campaign-level entry failure, n=139,366, Patreon/Western, 2013–2015, 82.6% never funded), Kässi et al. (account-level "ever worked" across freelance marketplaces, not content platforms, n=163M registered, 8.6% activated), and Houssard et al. (elite-tier ranking churn only, Twitch, 2019–2021). None of these is "median channel lifespan" in creatormap's sense, and the report must not average or blend them with creatormap's numbers as if they were comparable estimates of one quantity — they are not even measuring the same unit of analysis (person vs. campaign vs. account vs. rank-position vs. channel-as-artifact).

- **Position:** creatormap.io's 97,601/105,891/50K-channel "survival study" trilogy is not a citable measurement — it is unsourced marketing content for a creator-discovery SaaS product, disqualified by an internal sample-size contradiction, absent methodology, and absent named authorship; and no peer-reviewed or platform-disclosed dataset currently answers "what fraction of creators/channels stop, and how soon" at platform scale, a gap that is now well-documented (TubeCensus explains why) rather than merely a failed search.
- **Confidence:** high on the creatormap verdict (the internal 97,601-vs-105,891 contradiction is dispositive and requires no external corroboration); medium-high on the "no telemetry exists" claim — five independent search angles (three prior OpenAlex sweeps, plus my own academic-API searches and TubeCensus's own literature review) all came back empty, but I did not exhaust the investor-filing/S-1 angle, so I cannot rule out a disclosed platform figure I simply didn't find.
- **Boundary conditions:** the "no telemetry exists" claim holds for *individual creator/channel-level survival modeling at platform scale*. It does not hold for elite-tier churn (Houssard et al. measure this credibly for Twitch's top 10,000) or for non-monetized volunteer-contributor populations (Wikipedia, open-source), where real survival/turnover studies exist but measure different, non-paid populations with different exit mechanisms. It also does not hold for entry-failure rates (Regner's 82.6% is a real, complete-record measurement, just not a survival curve).
- **What would change this position:** discovery of a peer-reviewed paper or platform-disclosed transparency report that (a) defines a cohort of creators/channels at a fixed start date, (b) tracks the same cohort's activity status at one or more later dates, (c) reports the fraction still active/inactive with a disclosed sample and definition, at n well beyond a few thousand. A TubeCensus-based follow-up paper doing exactly this would fully satisfy it; nothing found in this locus does. On creatormap specifically, a corrected republication reconciling the 97,601/105,891 discrepancy with a disclosed data-processing explanation, plus a named analyst and methodology page, would upgrade it from "disqualified" to "gray literature, cite with caveats" — but even then it would remain unreplicated single-source marketing content per this run's evidence norms.
- **Evidence weight:** 3 independent OpenAlex sweeps (prior steps) + my own academic-API searches across 4 language/platform angles (Wikipedia, OSS, Bilibili/Douyin, AfreecaTV/SOOP) found zero platform-scale creator-survival papers; 1 infrastructure paper (TubeCensus) explicitly confirms the data-access reasons why; 2 adjacent-field analogues (Wikipedia editor retention, OSS contributor turnover) are real, peer-reviewed, and quantified but measure non-monetized populations; 1 source (creatormap.io, 3 linked posts) claims the missing measurement and fails internal consistency checks.
