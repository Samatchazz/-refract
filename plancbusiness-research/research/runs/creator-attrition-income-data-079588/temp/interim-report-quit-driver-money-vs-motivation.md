# Interim report: quit-driver-money-vs-motivation

**Locus question:** Self-reported burnout surveys blame financial insecurity; the only two studies that statistically model continuation find income does not predict exit at all. Does the evidence support "creators quit primarily because of insufficient income"?
**Flavor:** dialectical

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep and the redundancy audit (`temp/redundancy-audit.md`) had already done most of the provenance work before I started: the fourteen burnout notes in the corpus reduce to four independent instruments (Censuswide/Billion Dollar Boy, Awin/ShareASale, Vibely, Creators 4 Mental Health), and the contradiction graph (`temp/contradiction-graph.json`, cluster `quit-driver-money-vs-motivation`) had already staged the fight precisely: self-reported financial-instability-as-cause on one side, versus [[fame-and-fortune-or-just-fun]], [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]] and [[vol0123456789]] on the other, with the verdict "Side B is methodologically stronger where it counts... but Side B's samples are narrower." My job was to (1) build the missing construct-validity crosswalk the graph gestures at but doesn't spell out, (2) stress-test the "threshold vs. gradient" synthesis against the actual coefficients rather than asserting it, and (3) hunt for the one instrument that would settle this — a survey of people who have already quit.

## What the new sources say

**Sounds Profitable, "The Creators 2025"** ([[the-creators-2025-sounds-profitable]], n=5,000+ US podcast *listeners*, fielded and published Nov 2025) is the closest thing to a genuine exit-inclusive sample anywhere in this corpus, and it is a new methodological finding in its own right. Every other instrument in this cluster recruits from active-creator panels, Reddit/Twitter creator communities, or platform affiliate lists — designs that structurally cannot capture anyone who has already stopped. Sounds Profitable instead samples from the *listener* population and asks who has ever tried making a podcast: "nearly 1 in 6 podcast consumers have tried their hand at making a podcast. But for every three who start, one stops." That is roughly a 33% lapse rate among ever-triers — a true incidence measure, not a hypothetical intention. But the report (accessible only via its gated landing page) does not publish stated reasons *from that lapsed sub-sample specifically*. The secondary write-up I also fetched, [[1-in-3-podcast-creators-have-quit-says-study]] (thepodcasthost.com), answers "why do podcasters quit" by importing findings from a *different, separate* survey of active podcasters (its own "Independent Podcaster Survey 2025": growth struggles, lack of time/burnout, financial/monetization difficulty) — attributed-challenge data from people who are still podcasting, not stated-exit-reason data from people who quit. Even in the one design built to reach ex-creators, nobody has actually asked the ex-creators why they left.

**Johnson (2021), "Behind the Streams"** ([[pdf-behind-the-streams-the-off-camera-labour-of-game-live-streaming]], Games and Culture, DOI 10.1177/15554120211005239) was only recoverable in abstract form via an Academia.edu mirror — the full text remains paywalled/Cloudflare-blocked after three access attempts (publisher DOI page, institutional repository search, Academia.edu). Confirmed from the abstract and "key takeaways": 100+ interviews with professional and semi-professional streamers, documenting off-camera labour (aesthetics, networking, community management, maintenance tasks) as extensive, exhausting, and invisible to viewers — "some allocating up to half their day on graphics and overlays." This is useful color for *why burnout accrues regardless of income* (it corroborates the workload/creative-fatigue causes that BDB's and Vibely's instruments also report), but it is qualitative and not a survival or exit model, and I could not access the sections (if any) that discuss why any of the 100 interviewees actually left the platform.

**Kingsley et al. 2022 (CHI, YouTube demonetization panel)** — not recovered. The fetcher's targeted search did not locate an accessible copy; I am recording this as a genuine access failure rather than treating it as evidence of absence, since demonetization-panel studies are a plausible place for continuation/exit data to live and this remains a live lead for another pass.

**"Why YouTubers Quit"** ([[why-youtubers-quit-patapiro-poppets-newsletter]], a single Substack post, Feb 2024) is worth naming as a negative finding rather than a source: it is one anonymous fan's theorizing (algorithm unpredictability, cancellation risk, irrelevance spirals) with zero respondents, zero data, and n=1 (the author's own impressions). I flag it because it is illustrative of the genre the query asks about — "documented reasons creators give for quitting" — the online discourse is dense with essays like this and almost none of them are systematic.

## Evidence synthesis

### Construct-validity crosswalk

The literature reports at least five different quantities under the single label "why creators quit," and conflating them is the entire source of the apparent contradiction:

| Construct | Instrument(s) | N | Population sampled | What it actually shows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Prevalence of self-reported burnout | Censuswide/BDB (Jul 2025); Awin/ShareASale (2022, 2024); Vibely (Nov 2021); Creators 4 Mental Health (Nov 2025) | 1,000 (500 US/500 UK); 300+; 150; 542 (North America) | ACTIVE creators, self-selected panels | 52%, 73–87% (internally inconsistent between Awin's own 2022 and 2024 write-ups), 90%, 62% respectively say they have felt burned out |
| (b) Self-attributed *causes/severity* of burnout | same four | same | same | **The four instruments do not agree with each other.** BDB ranks financial instability #1 by severity (55%) among sufferers. Vibely's #1 stressor is algorithm/platform changes (65%), with "needing to make a living" second (59%). Awin's top driver is "constant platform changes" (70%) — financial strain is not even in its reported top-five list of causes. C4MH reports 69% "financial insecurity" as a correlate, not a ranked cause. Only one of four instruments makes the specific "money is the #1 cause" claim; the others put structural/platform stress ahead of or alongside it. |
| (c) Stated *intention* to quit | BDB (37%); Vibely (71%) | 1,000; 150 | ACTIVE creators | Hypothetical future intention, not behavior. Vibely's figure is nearly double BDB's four years later — the gap is attributable to sample size, follower-tier (Vibely skews 100k–500k, established creators), and no disclosed country/sampling frame, not to any real trend. |
| (d) Behavioral economic diversification (not full exit) | Ivors Academy/Musicians' Union 2020 | 311 | ACTIVE, self-selected trade-union campaigning members (UK) | 43% said insufficient streaming income caused them to take a job **outside music** — a real behavior, but diversification of income while (presumably) continuing to create, not exit from creation. Also 82% earned under £200 from streaming; 92% said streaming was under 5% of total earnings. This is the single closest thing to a "money-caused-a-change" behavioral data point in the corpus, and it is UK, music-industry-specific (a sector disrupted by a specific revenue-collapse — the shift to per-stream royalties — that has no clean analogue in platform-native creator economies), and drawn from an advocacy organization's own membership. |
| (e) Population-level incidence of actual quitting (no linked reasons) | Sounds Profitable, "The Creators 2025" | 5,000+ (listeners, not creators) | GENERAL population, includes both current and lapsed creators | ~33% of those who ever tried podcasting have already stopped. The only incidence-unbiased measure found, but no reasons are attached to the lapsed sub-sample. |
| (f) Statistically modelled predictors of *actual continuation/survival* | Törhönen et al. 2019 (PLS-SEM); Törhönen et al. 2019 HICSS-52 (ANOVA); Zhao et al. 2025 (Cox proportional-hazards); Wollborn et al. 2023 (DiD) | 377; 382; 1,923; 18,467 | Active + longitudinally observed exits | See below — this is the only construct with a genuine causal or quasi-causal design. |

Rows (a)–(e) are all measuring feelings, hypothetical intentions, or a diversification behavior among people who have **not** exited — exactly the "sharpest fight" the loci-analyst flagged. Only row (f) touches actual exit or persistence behavior, and it is where the "money doesn't predict continuation" finding lives.

### Testing the threshold-vs-gradient synthesis against the coefficients

The locus brief proposed a specific synthesis: income is a hard, threshold-like constraint on who *can* continue, while motivation is a soft gradient governing who *does* continue among those who can. I tested this against every coefficient available rather than asserting it.

**Zhao et al. 2025** (Cox model, n=1,923 VTubers, YouTube, 2017–Jul 2023) is the strongest test case, because it includes both an income variable and an effort variable in the same regression. Total Superchat Income's main effect on hazard is *not* statistically significant (β=−0.136, p=0.111) — but its interaction with time is (p=0.010), meaning income's protective effect is small and only accumulates gradually. Meanwhile **Total Stream Duration — hours streamed — is not a significant predictor of survival at all** (p=0.207): effort does not buy survival. The single strongest, most significant predictor is affiliation status: non-large-agency VTubers face 1.98x the hazard of large-agency VTubers (p=0.003), while independent VTubers are statistically indistinguishable from large-agency VTubers in this model (p=0.302). This is not income measured continuously — it's a categorical structural variable (access to production support, marketing, and a monetization pipeline) that happens to correlate with income capacity. Median lifespan by affiliation: 44 months (large agency) vs. 28 (independent) vs. 21 (small agency) — independent creators with no structural backing at all outlast small-agency creators, which cuts against a simple "more money = survive longer" story and toward "structural predictability/support matters more than raw earnings level."

**Wollborn et al. 2023** (Twitch DiD, n=18,467 streamers, Aug 2019–Jul 2020) independently replicates the same threshold logic from a completely different natural experiment. Newcomer streamers who crossed the discrete status line to "partner" during the COVID-19 opportunity-cost shock sustained their increased streaming effort; newcomers who only reached "affiliate" status reverted to pre-pandemic effort levels within 4–8 weeks, streaming 15.4% less than the partner group (p<0.01) once the market-feedback signal came in. This is a *status threshold*, not a continuous income gradient, doing the work — exactly the mechanism the locus brief predicted, replicated across platform, era, and (quasi-)causal design.

**Törhönen et al. 2019** (PLS-SEM, n=377, Twitch/YouTube creators, 2017, 30 countries but 71% Finland/US) supplies the motivational-gradient half. Intrinsic motivation predicts intention to continue at p=0.000 (β=0.568, aggregate model). Extrinsic motivation as a whole fails at p=0.052 (H4 rejected), and no individual extrinsic construct — income, career development, or reputation — reaches significance on intention-to-continue. Income's *only* significant relationship in the model is with weekly hours invested (β=0.123, p=0.046) — and even there the model explains just 2.1–5.5% of variance in hours, with a raw income–hours correlation of 0.151. Money buys marginally more effort; it does not buy the will to keep going.

**The HICSS-52 companion study** (ANOVA, n=382, same 2017 fieldwork) adds a decisive falsification test for the crude "money keeps people in" story: creators who self-identify their content creation as "work" earn the most (mean $774.85/month) and put in the most hours (25/week) — but their tenure (34.21 months) is statistically indistinguishable from the "play"-oriented group (38.67 months) and the "playbour" group (33.95 months) in every pairwise Tukey comparison (p=0.999, 0.657, 0.544). Income-orientation predicts earnings and hours; it does not predict how long anyone stays.

**The synthesis survives, refined.** The evidence supports a two-stage model, but it is *structural access*, not income as a continuous variable, that does the threshold work: agency affiliation (Zhao) and platform monetization tier (Wollborn's affiliate/partner line) predict who sustains activity, while raw earnings and raw hours are weak or non-significant predictors in both models. Given that access, intrinsic motivation (Törhönen) governs who keeps going. Nowhere in the four continuation/survival studies does *income level* — as opposed to structural/categorical access — carry independent predictive weight, and hours invested (effort) is actively non-significant in the one study that measured it directly against survival (Zhao).

### The exit-survey absence, confirmed

I searched Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex for "former YouTuber," "ex-streamer" exit interviews, content-creator attrition qualitative studies, streamer retirement interviews, and adjacent literatures (gig-driver churn/exit surveys, arts-career/musician dropout studies, freelancer exit surveys) via a dedicated fetcher pass, plus targeted web search for grey literature ("why I quit YouTube" analyzed systematically, creator economy churn qualitative research). The search surfaced one genuine near-miss (Sounds Profitable, discussed above — an exit-inclusive *incidence* sample with no linked reasons) and nothing that both (a) recruits from a population wide enough to include people who have already stopped creating, and (b) asks that specific sub-population why they stopped. No instrument of this kind was found in this literature. This absence is a load-bearing finding for the locus, not a gap in my search effort: the loci-analyst's premise is confirmed rather than merely repeated.

## Committed position

The claim "creators quit primarily because of insufficient income" is not well supported by the evidence in this corpus, and the corpus is unusually well-suited to say so precisely, because it contains the only two designs (Zhao's Cox model and Wollborn's DiD) that model actual continuation behavior rather than self-reported feeling. Both find that a structural/categorical access variable (agency affiliation; platform monetization tier) predicts persistence, while raw income level and raw effort (hours) are weak-to-non-significant — and Törhönen's two studies independently show that intrinsic motivation, not income, predicts stated intention to continue and that income-orientation buys hours and pay but not tenure. The self-report burnout literature's "financial instability is the #1 cause" claim is the finding of exactly one of its four constituent instruments (Censuswide/BDB); the other three (Awin, Vibely, C4MH) either rank a different cause first or don't rank financial causes at all — so even within its own construct (self-attributed severity among survivors), the "money is why we quit" claim does not replicate. The honest reading is not "motivation beats money" as a settled fact either — it is that no instrument anywhere in this literature has asked an actually-departed creator why they left, so the "reasons for quitting" the query asks for do not exist as measured data; what exists is (i) self-reported burnout-severity rankings among people who have not quit, which disagree with each other, and (ii) statistical models of persistence among people who are still active, which say income is not what predicts who keeps going. Reporting BDB's 55%/37% figures as "why creators quit" without this caveat would misrepresent both the construct and the population sampled.

- **Position:** Self-reported financial-insecurity-as-burnout-cause and statistically-modelled income-as-exit-predictor are different constructs measured in different (both survivorship-biased) populations; the modelled-continuation evidence (Zhao, Wollborn, Törhönen ×2) is more probative and finds income level is not a significant driver of persistence, while structural access to monetization infrastructure and intrinsic motivation are.
- **Confidence:** medium-high that income level does not predict actual continuation/exit (four converging designs, two quasi-causal, zero designs finding a strong direct income effect); low on what the true distribution of *reasons for actual exit* looks like, because no instrument measures that population at all.
- **Boundary conditions:** This holds specifically for the "does income predict continuation" question, tested in platform-native livestreaming/video creation (Twitch, YouTube, VTuber) contexts, 2017–2023, majority US/Finland/Japan-East-Asia samples. It does not necessarily extend to creators for whom a specific single revenue stream has collapsed structurally (the Ivors/MU music-royalty case looks different in kind — that is about being priced out of an entire format, not about a psychological "money vs. motivation" tradeoff) or to Southeast Asian creator populations, for which zero instruments in this cluster have any presence.
- **What would change this position:** A study that (1) samples a base population wide enough to include already-departed creators (not recruited from active-creator lists) and (2) asks that specific sub-population, in a survey or interview, why they stopped, with income and non-income reasons coded separately. Absent that, a replication of Zhao's Cox design on a non-VTuber, non-East-Asian creator population with a significant main effect for raw income (not just structural affiliation) would also weaken this position by showing the "threshold, not gradient" finding was VTuber-specific rather than general.
- **Evidence weight:** 2 quasi-causal continuation/survival models (Zhao Cox regression, Wollborn DiD) + 2 SEM/ANOVA studies from the same research team (Törhönen PLS-SEM, HICSS-52 ANOVA) find income does not predict persistence, or predicts only effort/hours; 4 self-report cross-sectional surveys (Censuswide/BDB, Awin, Vibely, C4MH) disagree with each other on whether financial insecurity is even the top-ranked cause of burnout among survivors; 1 behavioral proxy (Ivors/MU) shows income insufficiency changes behavior (outside job) but in a structurally distinct, single-country, single-industry context; 0 instruments measure actual departed creators' stated reasons; 1 near-miss design (Sounds Profitable) demonstrates the right sampling method exists but has not been paired with reason-elicitation.

## Open questions

- Kingsley et al. 2022 (CHI, YouTube demonetization volunteer panel) was not recovered — a genuine access failure, not confirmed absence. Worth another targeted pass (ACM Digital Library direct, or an author's institutional page) if budget allows elsewhere in the pipeline.
- Johnson (2021) "Behind the Streams" full text remains paywalled; the abstract confirms 100+ interviews on off-camera labour but I could not confirm whether any of it addresses stated reasons for quitting among the interview sample.
- Whether Sounds Profitable's underlying report (gated PDF, not the landing page I could access) contains a reasons breakdown specific to the lapsed sub-sample is unconfirmed — I only saw the public landing page and a secondary write-up. This is worth one more fetch attempt if another investigator's budget allows, since if it exists it would be the single most valuable source in this entire locus.
- No Southeast Asian instrument exists anywhere in this cluster on any of the four constructs (burnout prevalence, causes, intention, or modelled continuation). This entire locus's evidence base is US/UK/Finland/North America/East-Asia(VTuber)-only and must be flagged as non-generalizing to SEA in the final report.
