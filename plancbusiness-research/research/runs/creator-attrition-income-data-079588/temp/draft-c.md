# Content Creation as an Occupation: A Boundary-Conditions Map of the Quantitative Record

**Executive summary.** The measurable answer to "what share of content creators earns a living" ranges from 0.043% to 63%, and the range is not a disagreement about the world. It is a disagreement about four things that every figure in this literature silently fixes and rarely states: the **unit of analysis** (person, persona, channel, account, registered profile, or payroll job), the **denominator scope** (everyone who registered, everyone who was ever paid, everyone who self-identifies as full-time), the **income threshold**, and the **observation window**. Two single studies demonstrate this without leaving their own data. Regner's complete Patreon transaction record (n=139,366 registered campaigns, May 2013–October 2015, Patreon, country not reported) identifies roughly 250 creators at a living income and reports them both as the top 1% of the 24,257 campaigns that ever received a pledge and as 0.18% of all registered campaigns — a 5.75-fold swing from the denominator alone [[vol0123456789-3]]. Rieder et al.'s YouTube payout model (36,142,711 channels, 2019 revenue year, global) yields 0.43% of channels above a US minimum-wage-equivalent annual floor and 0.043% above the 2020 US median household income, a tenfold swing from the threshold alone on one table [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]].

On attrition, the strongest measurements are: a median career of 24 months among 1,923 VTubers on YouTube (Feb 2017–Jul 2023, global sample of Japan-origin agencies), with over half stopping entirely inside three years [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]]; roughly 46% three-year exit among an established German YouTube cohort of 500–5,000 subscribers tracked from December 2017 to late 2020 [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]]; and 82.6% of Patreon campaigns never receiving a single pledge, which is failure at entry rather than exit [[vol0123456789-3]]. No platform-scale creator survival curve exists in citable form; investor filings were checked and the closest disclosure is Spotify's earnings-tier retention, not participation retention [[takeaways-loud-and-clear]]. On time-to-income, no calendar-time distribution exists for any platform, and three independent measurements find output volume and threshold-crossing, not elapsed tenure, to be the operative variable. On quit reasons, no instrument anywhere has asked a departed creator why they left. On geography, Southeast Asia is not one condition: a single nine-country instrument fielded in one window records 36% in Thailand and 87% in Laos on the same question [[the-tiktok-effect]].

### B. The three non-creator comparators, and why they belong

Three well-documented cohort curves exist in adjacent populations and are worth holding beside the creator numbers precisely because they show what a real survival curve looks like. Among 11,267 US Uber driver-partners whose first trip fell between January and June 2013, 11% went inactive within one month, 70% were still active at six months and more than half at one year [[an-analysis-of-the-labor-market-for-ubers-driver-partners-in-the]] — noting the authors' disclosed relationship with the platform. English Wikipedia good-faith newcomer survival, measured on 100 randomly sampled newcomers per six-month cohort from 2001 to 2011, fell from 35.7% (first half of 2004) to 11.3% (second half of 2010), with the first-session rejection rate rising from 15.7% to 48.6% between 2005 and 2006 [[researchnewcomer-quality-meta-wiki]]. Of 216 Debian package maintainers active in July 1998, 121 (55.8%) were still contributing in December 2004, implying a cohort half-life near 7.5 years, while the total maintainer population grew from 216 to about 1,237 over the same period [[evolution-of-volunteer-participation-in-libre-software-projects]]. That last pair — steep cohort decay underneath headline population growth — is the single most transferable structural lesson, and it is exactly the confound that platform headcount announcements cannot address.

### C. What is withheld rather than absent

The data required for a creator survival curve exists inside platforms and is not published. YouTube's own documentation operationalizes exit: monetization "may" be switched off on channels that have not uploaded or posted for six months or more, which means monetized-channel counts are continuously pruned of dormant creators by the platform itself [[youtube-partner-program-overview-eligibility-computer-youtube-help]]. Independent reconstruction is bounded by infrastructure: an Internet Archive-based census identifies at least 106 million unique YouTube channels across 1.21 billion snapshots, and no survival model has been run on it [[tube-census]]. Non-English platform telemetry reaches the aggregate level only: Niconico's quarterly posting counts began declining around 2011 while view counts held until 2016, a lag the authors read as creator outflow preceding audience outflow [[why-niconico-in-japan-lost-game-jikkyo-streamers-and-their-audience]].

### B. The non-comparability crosswalk

The following rows must **never** be averaged, ranged, or ranked against one another, and the reason differs in each case:

1. **Rieder's 0.043% and TikTok's 63% are not two estimates of one quantity.** The first divides by every crawled channel including 31.7 million below the monetization gate that receive 0% of revenue [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]]; the second divides by surveyed users who already report using the platform as an income source [[the-tiktok-effect]]. They also use thresholds separated by an order of magnitude — see the crosswalk's fourth item.
2. **Regner's two figures for the same 250 creators may not be presented as a range.** 1.03% and 0.18% describe an identical numerator; only the denominator moves [[vol0123456789-3]].
3. **The Twitch percentiles describe earning accounts, not all accounts, and accounts are not people.** The 896,261 zero-or-negative accounts were excluded before any percentile was computed [[twitch-streamer-earnings-increase-for-top-gamers-data-from-hack-shows-wsj]], and the ranked entities include production companies, publishers and event organisations rather than individuals [[the-unbearable-lightness-of-streaming-superjoost-playlist]]. The leak also discloses no country composition, so any living-wage comparison drawn from it silently assumes a US cost of living for a population of unknown nationality.
4. **Living-wage thresholds are not interchangeable across regions.** The developing-Asia living wage is estimated at US$254.28/month anchored on the absolute poverty line and US$414.88/month on the moderate line, for a family of four at a 48-hour week [[adbi-working-paper-series]]. Western creator research benchmarks against a US federal monthly minimum of $1,160, a $15/hour equivalent, $50,000/year, and a 2020 US median household income of $67,521 [[twitch-streamer-earnings-increase-for-top-gamers-data-from-hack-shows-wsj]]; the UK study benchmarks against £8.21/hour, or £17,076 annualised [[music-creators-earnings-in]]. A "share above a living wage" computed at one of these bars carries no information about the same share at another.
5. **BLS and NEA/ACS are not two readings of the US creative workforce.** The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts employer-reported payroll jobs and structurally excludes the self-employed (61,710 jobs, May 2022) [[independent-artists-writers-and-performers-may-2022-oews-industry-specific-occup]]; ACS-based counts of the same industry group reach 172,376 people, and the roughly 2.8-fold gap **is** the self-employment share [[independent-artists-writers-and-performers-data-usa]].
6. **Strauss et al.'s "YouTube creators earn $43/month" is Patreon income earned by creators whose one linked platform is YouTube.** It is not YouTube ad revenue and must not be read as one [[rich-get-richer-platform-attention-and-earnings-inequality]].

### C. The two legitimate comparison classes

Two kinds of comparison survive the crosswalk. The first is **within-instrument time series holding the denominator constant**: Regner's Patreon active-campaign median of $31/month (2013–2015, floored at >$0) against Strauss et al.'s Patreon medians of $42–$77/month (2018–2024, floored at >$10/month), comparable with that floor difference stated [[vol0123456789-3]] [[rich-get-richer-platform-attention-and-earnings-inequality]]. The second is **dimensionless shape statistics**, which are scale- and currency-invariant:

### D. Two measured biases that push in opposite directions

Platform payout figures **undercount** total creator income, and the only clean measurement of the coverage ratio comes from music: audio streaming royalties are 6% of the average UK music creator's income, all digital sources 14%, and live performance alone 31% (n=708, 2019, UK) [[music-creators-earnings-in]]. Off-platform income also concentrates upward — top-2,000 Twitch channels carry an average 3.7 sponsorship or affiliate links against 2.2 for a comparison sample outside the top 10,000 [[vol0123456789-2]], and 60.6% of YouTube channels above 10,000 subscribers use at least one off-platform monetization strategy [[characterizing-alternative-monetization-strategies-on]]. No individual-level platform-to-total income ratio exists for YouTube, Twitch or Patreon.

Registration denominators **overcount** the population that ever attempted to earn. A genuinely random sample of YouTube videos (n=10,016; 1,000 hand-coded, 989 usable, November 2022, global) finds visible monetization present in 0.21% of videos on coder agreement, with a median posting-channel subscriber count of 4 against a mean of 55,100 [[journal-of-quantitative-description-digital-media-32023-185]]. Partner Program enrolment is roughly 45–50% of eligible channels: 2 million members disclosed in August 2021 [[youtube-says-its-partner-program-now-has-2-million-members-the-verge]] against 4,415,180 channels above 1,000 subscribers in the 2019–2020 census [[mapping-youtube-a-quantitative-exploration-of-a-platformed-media-system]].

The second correction is capped by the platform's own disclosure. When YouTube retroactively expelled partners in February 2018, it stated that 99% of those affected had earned under US$100 in the preceding year and 90% under US$2.50 in the preceding month — and those were creators who had already cleared the older, much looser 10,000-lifetime-view bar [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]]. **Inference, stated as such:** narrowing the denominator from "everyone who registered" to "everyone who cleared a monetization gate" removes fewer near-zero earners than its size suggests, because the 2018 disclosure shows the near-zero mass persists on the far side of a gate. This rests on the YouTube disclosure and on the enrolment gap above, and is not itself a measured quantity.

**Inference, stated as such:** a channel sitting exactly at the eligibility line and earning the modal $5/month would need roughly 20 months of accrual to cross the $100 payment threshold, quite apart from review-queue delay. This is arithmetic over the vidIQ view distribution [[how-much-does-youtube-pay-for-1000-subscribers-in-2026]] and the disclosed payout floor [[meet-youtubes-revenue-thresholds-for-payment-youtube-help]]; no source measures it directly.

### B. Tenure is not the operative variable

Three independent measurements converge. In the 36,336,861-channel census, elite channels had published an average 940 videos against 294 for the next tier down, but average days active since start were "surprisingly constant between tiers"; channel-age correlations with success were 0.082 (subscribers) and 0.101 (views) among the elite, falling to 0.009 and 0.003 across the full population [[mapping-youtube-a-quantitative-exploration-of-a-platformed-media-system]]. On Twitch, newcomer streamers who reached only Affiliate status reverted to pre-lockdown effort within roughly 4–8 weeks while those reaching Partner sustained it, streaming 15.4% less than the Partner group — two cohorts of identical tenure, diverging on threshold status (n=18,467 streamers, 3,329,042 hourly observations, August 2019–July 2020) [[vol0123456789]]. Among 24,257 funded Patreon campaigns, about 20% received exactly one pledge and roughly a third received two, so the modal funded outcome is a handful of pledges rather than a trajectory [[vol0123456789-3]]. No accessible study reports a calendar-time-to-income hazard model for any platform; that absence is a finding rather than a gap in search.

## 4. Documented Reasons Creators Give for Quitting

### A. The population mismatch, stated before any figure

Every burnout instrument in this record recruits from **currently active** creators and asks them to introspect about a hypothetical departure. None samples people who have already stopped. The closest design that reaches a genuinely lapsed population — a census-weighted US survey of 5,034 podcast consumers finding 32% of ever-triers have stopped — never asks that sub-sample why [[the-creators-2025-understanding-the-modern-podcast-creator-landscape-sounds-prof]].

### B. Four instruments, four rankings

| Creators 4 Mental Health / Lupiani | 62% | 69% report financial insecurity (correlate, not ranked cause); 89% lack specialised mental-health resources | 542 | Nov 2025 | North America | [[in-sweeping-study-89-of-creators-claim-to-lack-access-to-specialized-mental-heal]] |
| Vibely | 90%; 71% considered quitting | Algorithm/platform changes, 65%; "need to make a living" second at 59% | 150 | Nov 2021 | Unstated | [[90-of-content-creators-deal-with-burnout-vibely-wants-to-change-that-tubefilter]] |

Only one of the three prevalence instruments ranks money first. Two of them rank a platform-structural cause first or do not rank financial causes at all.

### C. What the models of actual continuation find

In the only Cox proportional-hazards survival model in this literature, affiliation status is the significant predictor of exit (hazard ratio 1.980 for small-agency affiliation, p=0.003), while total stream duration is not significant (p=0.207) and gender is not significant [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]]. In a difference-in-differences panel of 11,371 Patreon creators and 131,006 creator-months spanning the 2017–2018 demonetization shock, the creator population grew rather than shrank and affected creators shifted effort toward paid content on an alternative platform — reallocation rather than exit, among creators who already had structural access to a second channel [[r-a-p-h-a-e-l-a-a-n-d-r-e-s-m-i-c-h-e-l-a-n-g-e-lo-r-oss-i]]. A companion regression-discontinuity design on the same shock finds that losing monetization access cut uploads by 2.816 videos per month against a sample mean of 3.248, roughly an 87% collapse in output, without the aggregate exit pattern following it: exiting creators had **higher** average subscribers and watchtime than stayers, and a smaller proportion of them had lost Partner Program access [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]].

The one non-English platform-scale study that names a departure reason surveys the wrong side of the transaction: among 1,356 lapsed Japanese viewers (screening n=3,328, fielded 24 August–7 September 2021), "creators and streamers moved to other services" was the top or joint-top reason for stopping in every one of six demographic cells, at 29–42% [[why-niconico-in-japan-lost-game-jikkyo-streamers-and-their-audience]]. That measures audience response to creator exit, not creator exit.

### B. Southeast Asia is nine conditions, not one

One instrument, one fielding window (August–September 2022), one question — the percentage of surveyed creators earning more than their country's minimum wage on TikTok — produces: Thailand 36%, Philippines 40%, Cambodia 56%, Indonesia 63%, Singapore 68%, Myanmar 73% (flagged low base, n=26), Malaysia 80%, Vietnam 83%, Laos 87% [[the-tiktok-effect]]. The intra-regional spread of 51 percentage points exceeds the distance between several of these countries and any Western benchmark in this record. Three caveats travel with the whole chart: the sample is self-selected active TikTok users (N=3,412 across nine markets; Indonesia n=540 total), the sub-base of "creators who use TikTok as a source of income" over which the percentage is computed is never disclosed per country, and Indonesia's minimum-wage comparator is a single blended US$216/month figure that erases provincial variation. Separately, the "8 million creators" headcount routinely paired with the 63% figure appears nowhere in the primary report, which contains percentages only; the two numbers come from different sources, years and methods and were merged afterwards [[63-percent-of-tiktok-creators-earn-more-than-minimum-wage]].

### C. The rest of the regional record

Indonesia's most methodologically transparent academic study samples 98 channels drawn by Slovin's formula from a population of 4,561 Social Blade-tracked Indonesian channels (March 2023): views explain income at a path coefficient of 0.968 (p=0.001) while subscribers do not (0.029, p=0.387), R²=0.950, and revenue per 1,000 views ranges IDR 3,682–58,920 — a roughly 650-fold income spread between the top and bottom of an already-elite 98-channel sample [[pengaruh-viewer-dan-subscriber-terhadap-pendapatan-youtuber-indonesia-etnik-jurn]]. Malaysia's only creator-side survey with a disclosed sample (n=489, fielded 11–21 May 2026) measures tax literacy and income composition rather than attrition: 34% have filed income tax, in-kind income is 46% of income sources against 43% for direct cash, and filing rates run from 18% in the under-3,000-follower tier to 83% above 100,000 followers [[nothing-comes-free-study-finds-only-34pc-of-malaysian-content-creators-file-inco]]. The most-cited regional sector report contains no creator-side instrument at all; its only quantified survey is demand-side (n=2,400 consumers across six countries, June–August 2024) [[role-of-influencer-marketing]]. Indonesia's national statistics agency publishes a creative-economy workforce series (27.40 million, 18.70% of the national workforce, 2025) that is a substantially broader category than content creation [[bps-dan-kemenekraf-bersinergi-kreator-lokal-dilibatkan-dalam-sosialisasi-se2026]].

Three structural mechanisms make Western figures non-transferable independently of the threshold problem. Revenue per view differs by audience geography: 1,000 views at 70% inventory fill yield roughly INR 30 (under US$1) from an Indian audience against US$5 from a US audience, a five- to sixfold gap driven by nothing the creator controls [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051241234693]]. Off-platform monetization infrastructure is thinner: among YouTube channels with videos but no links at all, India, Indonesia and Thailand are over-represented, and the authors could find no specialised crowdfunding site serving India [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]]. And "earning money on social media" often means something else entirely: 71% of Indonesian e-commerce participants sell exclusively via social media and chat apps, in a country where 13.2 million of roughly 127 million employed people were engaged in e-commerce in 2019 — and where the two official surveys disagree by more than twofold on that same population in that same year (SAKERNAS 12.2 million against SUSENAS 5.9 million) [[technologies]].

**Inference, stated as such and untested:** if structural access to monetization infrastructure is what predicts persistence [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]] [[vol0123456789]], and Southeast Asian creators face measurably worse structural access on revenue per view [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051241234693]] and off-platform linking [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]], then Southeast Asian attrition should exceed Western attrition. No dataset in this record can test that. It is the highest-value study nobody has run.

## 6. Evidence Quality and Measurement Caveats

### A. Six dimensions, not four

The query's four required fields are necessary but insufficient. Preventing false comparison needs two more — unit of analysis and denominator scope — and a seventh flag wherever income is reported: **gross versus net**. Nearly every figure here is gross of the creator's own costs. Twitch payouts exclude sponsorships, tips and publisher payments [[twitch-streamer-earnings-increase-for-top-gamers-data-from-hack-shows-wsj]]; Superchat totals exclude the agency's cut and, after 2022, are no longer the top revenue line for the largest agencies [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]]; and platform hourly rates exclude the off-camera labour that determines any true hourly figure [[the-unbearable-lightness-of-streaming-superjoost-playlist]].

### B. Sampling frame is the dominant failure mode, and its scale is bounded but not calibrated

The clearest direct measurement of the sampling-frame effect concerns affiliate-link prevalence, not income: 36.7% of How To & Style videos on a popularity-gated dataset of channels above 10,000 subscribers [[characterizing-alternative-monetization-strategies-on]] against 3.49% for the same category on a random-prefix sample of roughly 500,000 videos [[endorsements-on-social-media-an-empirical-study-of]]. That roughly tenfold gap bounds the mechanism's plausible scale. It is **not** a coefficient for income and must not be applied to income figures as one — no study has directly measured the sampling-frame effect on creator earnings. Institutions concede the constraint in their own methodology: the ILO states there was no sampling base from which a random sample of platform workers could be drawn [[ilo-flagship-report]], and the World Bank found self-identified gig workers were the most likely to drop out mid-survey [[the-promise-and-peril]]. Even a Twitch telemetry panel carries a selection floor of one hourly top-500 placement, roughly 21 concurrent viewers [[vol0123456789]].

### C. Reference period and definitional instability

Measured prevalence of platform work swings by a factor of three to nine on reference period alone — from 5.7% of Norway's working-age population over a year to 1.5% over a month [[ilo-brief]]. The same source records that of 133 countries responding to an international review, only 40 have ever measured digital platform employment, most in Europe and Central Asia, with no international statistical standard expected before 2028 [[ilo-brief]]. Threshold changes also break cohorts: Twitch's current Affiliate bar (25 followers, 4 hours, 4 days, 3 concurrent viewers) is materially lower than the historical version, so a creator counted as monetized in 2026 cleared an easier gate than one in 2019 [[joining-the-affiliate-program]], and YouTube's February 2018 rule change severs comparability with earlier cohorts entirely [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]].

### D. Provenance, applied symmetrically

Two headline statistics in this field were traced to their primaries during this work, and the results run opposite to expectation. The Southeast Asian chain, followed back, terminates in a real if thin instrument — a nine-country survey with disclosed fielding dates, a disclosed total sample and a disclosed low-base footnote [[the-tiktok-effect]]. The widely-circulated Western creator-population and professional-share figures terminate in a venture-capital blog estimate carried through an SEO listicle, with the professional count held flat across three years of population growth. Applying one standard uniformly leaves the Western figure in worse condition than the Southeast Asian one, and the precise charge against the latter narrows to a merger: two numbers from different sources, years and methods were welded into one statistic and then repeated as a national figure [[63-percent-of-tiktok-creators-earn-more-than-minimum-wage]]. Two fabricated sources were also identified during this work — a research institute credited with studies that exists nowhere on the record, and a 2025 Indonesian journal article whose entire 24-item reference list is unrelated STEM material. Neither may be cited; their existence is itself a finding about the field's information environment.

### E. What would change the picture

Three instruments now in prospect would resolve much of what this map can only chart. Indonesia's 2026 Economic Census is billed as the first instrument to make content-creator business actors statistically visible, which would be the first nationally-representative creator-specific denominator anywhere in this literature [[bps-dan-kemenekraf-bersinergi-kreator-lokal-dilibatkan-dalam-sosialisasi-se2026]]. The 22nd International Conference of Labour Statisticians in 2028 is the earliest date at which a harmonised definition of platform work could exist, and only 40 of 133 countries have ever attempted the measurement [[ilo-brief]]. And the archival infrastructure for a genuine YouTube survival curve — 106 million unique channels with subscriber histories reaching back to 2006 — now exists publicly and has not yet been used for one [[tube-census]]. Until at least one of those lands, the honest presentation of this evidence is a matrix rather than a number, and the single condition that both would change and would settle it is the same in every section above: one instrument harmonising "active monetized creator" across two or more platforms under one definition. None exists.
