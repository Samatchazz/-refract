# Content Creation as an Occupation: A Map of the Quantitative Record

Four quantities are asked about here — attrition, income distribution, time to first income, and stated reasons for quitting — and the measurable record answers them very unevenly. Attrition has four real measurements that count four different things and no platform-scale survival curve at all. Income distribution is the best-measured of the four, with complete transaction records and platform-disclosed payouts converging on one shape: the mean runs four to twenty-one times the median wherever both statistics have been computed. Time to first income has no measured calendar-time distribution on any platform. Reasons for quitting have never been elicited from anyone who quit. Across the whole record, published shares of creators "earning a living" span from 0.043% to 63%, and that spread is mostly not disagreement about the world — it is four things each study silently fixes and rarely states: the unit of analysis, the denominator scope, the income threshold, and the observation window. This report maps what has been measured, attaches sample size, year, platform and country to every figure, and flags which findings rest on US or Western samples.

## 1. Attrition and Survival Rates of Creators and Channels

Attrition means the rate at which people stop creating. Survival analysis, the standard technique, follows a defined cohort forward from a common start and reports what fraction is still active at each elapsed interval — the design used routinely in medicine and in labour economics. Applying it to creators requires two things: a roster of everyone who started, and a rule for when someone has stopped. Platforms hold both and publish neither. What the public record contains instead is four measurements built on four different units — a persona, a channel, a crowdfunding campaign, and a person — which cannot be laid on one curve.

Two rows are routinely misread. The Kässi activation figures describe online freelancing marketplaces, not content platforms, and rest on directly observed activity for only seven platforms — the authors decline to publish a confidence interval and give an observed range of 2.0% to 36.8% instead [[how-many-online-workers-are-there-in-the-open-research-europe]]. The World Bank's competing 37% mean therefore sits at the top of that range rather than contradicting it, and it finds the distribution bimodal rather than uniformly low [[the-promise-and-peril]]. Regner's 82.6% is failure at entry rather than exit over time, and the Twitch flux figure is churn inside an already-elite population [[vol0123456789-3]] [[vol0123456789-2]].

### B. The absence, and who holds the missing data

No major platform publishes a creator survival curve, and the investor-disclosure channel was checked without finding one. YouTube discloses eligibility rules, revenue splits and aggregate payouts — "over $100 billion to creators, artists, and media companies" across 2022–2025, a pool with no headcount attached — but never a distribution or a count of monetized channels [[youtubes-impact-on-the-creator-economy-how-youtube-works]] [[powered-by-americans]]. The infrastructure reason for the external gap is documented: YouTube's application programming interface exposes only current, never historical, subscriber counts, and the leading commercial alternative lists only the top 5,000 channels with at most three years of history [[tube-census]]. YouTube nonetheless operationalizes exit internally, stating that monetization may be switched off on channels silent for six months or more — a continuously applied definition of creator death whose resulting counts are never published [[youtube-partner-program-overview-eligibility-computer-youtube-help]]. The one genuine platform-disclosed cohort figure is Spotify's, and it measures retention within an earnings tier among the already-successful rather than participation among everyone who started [[takeaways-loud-and-clear]].

One source presents itself as the missing telemetry and is excluded here. A vendor's cross-linked posts describe a single 2019-tracked YouTube cohort and give three different sample sizes for it — 97,601 channels, "105,891 independent YouTube channels" in a post whose own title says 97,601, and "over 50,000" — with no named analyst, no methodology, no error bars, and a product signup at the end of each [[youtube-channel-survival-rates-a-7-year-creator-study-creatormap]]. Its 8.8-year median lifespan is also left-truncated, being the age at death of channels that had already reached 10,000 subscribers.

### C. The closest documented analogues

Two literatures on unpaid contributors have produced exactly the survival methodology this field lacks. English Wikipedia good-faith newcomer survival — at least one edit two to six months after the first session — fell from 35.7% for the first-half-2004 cohort to 11.3% for the second-half-2010 cohort, measured on 100 randomly sampled newcomers per six-month period from 2001 to 2011, with first-session rejection rising from 15.7% to 48.6% between 2005 and 2006 [[researchnewcomer-quality-meta-wiki]]. Of 216 Debian package maintainers active in July 1998, 121 (55.8%) were still contributing in December 2004, implying a cohort half-life near 7.5 years, while the total maintainer population grew from 216 to about 1,237 over the same period [[evolution-of-volunteer-participation-in-libre-software-projects]]. That last pair — steep cohort decay underneath headline population growth — is the most transferable structural lesson available, and it is precisely the confound that platform headcount announcements cannot address. Both populations are unpaid, so exit mechanics differ; these are methodological comparison cases, not creator data.

### D. Concentration is worsening while the top stays stable

Across whole populations, measured inequality has risen. The Gini coefficient — a 0-to-1 index where 0 is perfect equality — for within-topic Patreon creators rose from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.60 in December 2020 on the complete pledge record [[quantifying-the-creator-economy-a-large-scale-analysis-of-patreon]]; VTuber between-creator Gini rose from about 0.60 in 2018 to 0.75 in 2023 [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]]; and Pareto alpha, the exponent describing how fat a distribution's top tail is, fell across YouTube, Instagram and Facebook between 2018 and 2024 [[rich-get-richer-platform-attention-and-earnings-inequality]]. Within the elite tier, composition is nearly frozen: Twitch weekly ranking flux of 0.13–0.20 resembles word-frequency stability in natural language rather than social datasets such as GitHub repositories at 0.65 [[vol0123456789-2]]. The two findings are compatible — incumbency at the top persists while the distance between top and bottom widens.

## 2. Income Distribution Across Creators: Median vs Mean and the Living-Wage Share

Creator income is a right-skewed power law: a small number of very large earners pull the arithmetic mean far above the median, so an average describes almost nobody. Reporting median and mean side by side is therefore not a courtesy but a requirement, and the ratio between them is itself the most portable statistic in this literature because it survives currency and scale changes. The "share earning a living wage" is a different and much harder quantity, because it needs both a distribution and a threshold, and studies disagree about both — and, more consequentially, about who belongs in the denominator.

The three median monthly figures for Patreon-adjacent earnings differ substantially, and the reason is an inclusion floor rather than a measurement dispute. Regner's $31 is the median peak among campaigns that received any money at all, a floor of greater than zero [[vol0123456789-3]]; Strauss and colleagues report $42–77 after excluding everyone earning under $10 a month [[rich-get-richer-platform-attention-and-earnings-inequality]]; Zhao's $127 is a different platform, product and population entirely [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]]. Comparing them requires stating the floors.

Four constraints travel with every figure from the October 2021 Twitch payout leak. The *Wall Street Journal* excluded 896,261 zero- or negative-revenue accounts before computing anything, so its percentiles describe earning accounts [[twitch-streamer-earnings-increase-for-top-gamers-data-from-hack-shows-wsj]]. The unit is the account rather than the person, and production companies sit inside the top ranks [[the-unbearable-lightness-of-streaming-superjoost-playlist]]. It excludes sponsorships, tips and publisher payments, and the US$100 payout floor censors the low tail. And it discloses no country composition, so the widely repeated finding that 25% of the top 10,000 highest-paid streamers do not clear US minimum wage — the 8,000th-ranked account cumulating US$29,396 since 2019, roughly US$14,698 a year against a US$15,080 federal minimum — assumes US costs for a population of unknown nationality [[many-top-twitch-streamers-dont-make-minimum-wage-and-3-new-takeaways-from-the-tw]]. Independent verification is partial at best: one outlet that contacted named streamers found some confirming the figures and others saying they did not line up, and five outlets state five incompatible collection windows for the same files [[twitch-responds-to-leak-revealing-earnings-of-top-streamers-and-more-dexerto]].

The arithmetic inside single studies is what makes the spread legible. Regner's paper reports the same roughly 250 creators as both the top 1% of active campaigns and 0.18% of all registered campaigns — a 5.75-fold swing from the denominator alone, with the numerator untouched [[vol0123456789-3]]. Reading Rieder's published tier table against a US-minimum-wage rather than a US-median-household threshold raises the 0.043% share by roughly an order of magnitude; that recalculation is an arithmetic re-cut of the published tiers by changing the threshold parameter, not a figure the authors state [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]]. Those two levers, both computed on identical underlying data, account for much of the apparent hundredfold telemetry-versus-survey gap without any appeal to measurement error.

Six rows above must never be averaged, ranged or ranked against one another. Rieder's 0.043% and TikTok's 63% are not two estimates of one quantity: the first divides by every crawled channel including 31.7 million below the monetization gate that receive none of the ad revenue, the second by surveyed users who already report using the platform as an income source [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]] [[the-tiktok-effect]]. The Twitch percentiles describe earning accounts, not people. And the thresholds themselves are not interchangeable across regions, a point section 5 takes up. Two comparison classes do survive: within-instrument time series holding the denominator constant, and dimensionless shape statistics — mean/median ratios, Gini, Pareto alpha, hazard ratios — which are scale- and currency-invariant.

One headline sentence is defensible with all its scoping attached: across four telemetry and complete-record datasets covering Patreon and YouTube between 2013 and 2019, under 2% of everyone who registered an account or held a channel reached roughly US$15,000 a year, falling under 0.1% at a US median-household bar — and both bias directions on that figure are known and unquantified for these platforms.

### C. Two measured biases that push in opposite directions

Platform payout figures undercount total creator income, and the only clean measurement of the coverage ratio comes from music: audio streaming royalties are 6% of the average UK music creator's income, all digital sources 14%, and live performance alone 31% (n=708, 2019, UK) [[music-creators-earnings-in]]. Off-platform income also concentrates upward — 60.6% of YouTube channels above 10,000 subscribers use at least one off-platform monetization strategy [[characterizing-alternative-monetization-strategies-on]], and top-2,000 Twitch channels carry 3.7 sponsorship or affiliate links on average against 2.2 outside the top 10,000 [[vol0123456789-2]]. No individual-level platform-to-total income ratio exists for YouTube, Twitch or Patreon.

Registration denominators push the other way, overcounting the population that ever attempted to earn. In a genuinely random YouTube video sample, both coders agreed visible monetization was present in only 0.21% of hand-coded videos, and the median posting channel had four subscribers [[journal-of-quantitative-description-digital-media-32023-185]]. Partner Program enrolment is roughly 45–50% of eligible channels: 2 million members disclosed in August 2021 against 4,415,180 channels above 1,000 subscribers in the 36,336,861-channel 2020 census [[youtube-says-its-partner-program-now-has-2-million-members-the-verge]] [[mapping-youtube-a-quantitative-exploration-of-a-platformed-media-system]]. That second correction is capped by the platform's own disclosure: when YouTube retroactively expelled partners in February 2018, it stated that 99% of those affected had earned under US$100 in the preceding year and 90% under US$2.50 in the preceding month — creators who had already cleared the older, much looser 10,000-lifetime-view bar [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]]. Combining that disclosure with the enrolment gap implies that narrowing the denominator from everyone who registered to everyone past a monetization gate removes fewer near-zero earners than its size suggests. That is an inference from two sourced claims, not a measured quantity.

### D. Where surveys and administrative data land

Two self-selected vendor surveys fielded in the same year on nominally the same quantity report answers three and a half times apart, and both must be labelled as recruitment artifacts rather than population estimates. NeoReach and Influencer Marketing Hub found 43% of creators above US$50,000 a year and 70% full-time, recruited through the two firms' own creator networks [[creator-earnings-breakdown-the-creator-economy-today]]; Linktree found 34% full-time, 12% of full-timers above US$50,000, and 46% of self-identified full-timers under US$1,000 a year (n=9,576 of its own users, fielded 2021, country composition never disclosed) [[pdf-linktree-creatorreport-2022-02f3aa05a27be6fecb3537b13d5ec9depdf]]. Cross-sectional academic surveys of active creators land closer to telemetry: 45.8% of 377 respondents across 30 countries reported any income at all [[fame-and-fortune-or-just-fun]], replicated at 46.1% of 382 in a companion study where reported income standard deviations exceeded group means by 2.6 to 4.6 times [[play-playbour-or-labour-the-relationships-between-perception-of]].

US federal microdata supplies the only nationally representative benchmark. Artists employed part time had median annual earnings of $20,380 in 2023, statistically indistinguishable from all part-time workers, against $70,170 for full-time artists [[artists-and-other-cultural-workers]]; only 59.8% of US artists worked full-year full-time against 66.0% of all workers, falling to 23.5% for actors and 32.6% for musicians [[office-of-research-analysis]]. The wage Gini for independent artists, writers and performers is 0.582 against a US national 0.474 [[independent-artists-writers-and-performers-data-usa]]. The gap between the ACS person count for that industry group and the payroll count of 61,710 is roughly 2.8-fold, and that gap is the self-employment share that payroll instruments structurally cannot see [[independent-artists-writers-and-performers-may-2022-oews-industry-specific-occup]].

## 3. Time from Starting to First Meaningful Income

"Meaningful income" has no standard definition, and the three candidate definitions are separately gated and largely decoupled from each other. A creator must first clear a platform's monetization eligibility rule, which is a behavioural threshold rather than a duration. Money then accrues but does not move until a separate payout floor is crossed. Only past both does any income floor worth calling occupational come into view. No platform publishes a calendar-time distribution for any of the three, and the record suggests elapsed time is the wrong variable in the first place.

A channel sitting at the eligibility line and earning the modal few dollars a month would need on the order of a year and a half of accrual to cross the $100 payment threshold, before any review-queue delay. That is arithmetic over the disclosed payout floor and the vendor view distribution [[meet-youtubes-revenue-thresholds-for-payment-youtube-help]] [[how-much-does-youtube-pay-for-1000-subscribers-in-2026]]; no source measures it directly. Eligibility also does not confer participation: only about half of channels above the subscriber gate actually enrol in the Partner Program [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]].

### B. Elapsed tenure does not separate success from failure

Three independent measurements converge. Across the 36,336,861-channel YouTube census, correlation between channel age and success indicators was 0.082 for subscribers and 0.101 for views within the elite tier, collapsing to 0.009 and 0.003 across the full population; elite channels averaged 940 published videos against 294 for the next tier down, while average days active were "surprisingly constant between tiers" [[mapping-youtube-a-quantitative-exploration-of-a-platformed-media-system]]. On Twitch, a difference-in-differences design — a technique comparing how two groups' outcomes diverge after an event affecting only one — found both newcomer cohorts raising streaming time about 22.7% at lockdown onset, after which newcomers reaching only Affiliate status streamed 15.4% less than new Partners and reverted to pre-pandemic levels within roughly four to eight weeks, while new Partners sustained the increase (n=18,467 streamers, 3,329,042 hourly observations, Aug 2019 – Jul 2020) [[vol0123456789]]. Among Patreon's 24,257 funded campaigns, about 20% received exactly one pledge, so the modal funded outcome is a handful of pledges rather than a trajectory [[vol0123456789-3]].

### C. The circulating timelines have no measured basis

Two widely-mirrored advice sources were inspected directly. One asserts a fast track of one to six months, an average of six months to two years and a slow path beyond two years, illustrated with two survivor anecdotes, while selling watch-hour services [[how-long-does-it-take-to-get-monetized-on-youtube-with-examples]]. The other asserts that "most creators reach this point within 6–12 months of consistent posting" with no sample, cohort, data source or method anywhere in a 4,800-word article [[when-do-you-start-making-money-on-youtube-how-monetization-really-works]]. Two survey-derived quantities do bear on timing and must be read as survivor descriptions rather than cohort outcomes: among Linktree respondents with under 12 months of experience, 59% had not monetized at all, 35% had monetized below a livable income and 6% had earned over $10,000 [[pdf-linktree-creatorreport-2022-02f3aa05a27be6fecb3537b13d5ec9depdf]] [[200-million-people-are-earning-money-from-digital-content-creation-tubefilter]]; among NeoReach respondents, 46% of those online four or more years earn over $20,000 annually, computed only over creators still present to answer [[creator-earnings-breakdown-the-creator-economy-today]].

## 4. Documented Reasons Creators Give for Quitting

Asking why people leave an occupation requires reaching people who left it. Every instrument in this record does the opposite: it recruits currently active creators and asks them about burnout or about a hypothetical future departure. What those instruments measure is prevalence of distress and stated intention among survivors, which is a legitimate quantity but a different one. Set against them are four statistical models of who actually keeps going, and the two literatures do not agree.

Only one of these instruments ranks financial instability first, and it ranks it first by severity while ranking creative fatigue first by frequency — different variables on different denominators [[over-half-of-creators-face-burnout-action-urged-billion-dollar-boy]]. The others place platform and algorithm change ahead of money or do not rank financial causes at all. Money as the leading exit driver is the result of one instrument rather than a replicated finding. The 52% figure itself has been reproduced across at least seven trade outlets, all reporting one Censuswide survey of 1,000 people; only one of those outlets discloses the fielding agency, the July 2025 fieldwork and the 500-per-market split, none of which appears in the commissioning agency's own release [[52-of-content-creators-say-they-have-experienced-burnout]] [[burnout-among-creators-is-reaching-a-crisis-point]] [[the-hidden-cost-of-creator-burnout-and-what-it-means-for-your-influencer-program]]. Awin's two waves cannot be differenced, because the 2024 release restates the 2022 baseline as 87% while Awin's own 2022 write-up headlines 78% [[a-majority-of-content-creators-and-influencers-struggle-with-burnout-as-concerns]]. A comparison outside the creator literature is useful for calibration: in a survey of 1,153 platform workers across 21 Latin American and Caribbean countries, no named risk approaches a majority — financial insecurity 13%, internet connection problems 11%, lack of growth opportunities 10% [[survey-on-workers-on]].

### B. What models of actual continuation find

Four designs model persistence rather than feeling, and none finds income level to be the operative predictor. Zhao's Cox proportional-hazards model — a survival regression estimating how each variable multiplies the instantaneous risk of exit — returns a hazard ratio of 1.980 for small-agency affiliation (p=0.003), while total stream duration (p=0.207) and gender are not significant and Superchat income shows no significant main effect (β=−0.136, p=0.111) [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]]. The Twitch panel finds the Affiliate/Partner status line, not earnings, separating streamers who sustained effort from those who reverted, at identical tenure [[vol0123456789]]. Törhönen's structural model of 377 creators finds intrinsic motivation predicting intention to continue at p=0.000 while extrinsic motivation as a block fails at p=0.052 and no individual extrinsic construct reaches significance; income predicts weekly hours at p=0.046 in a model explaining 2.1% of the variance in hours [[fame-and-fortune-or-just-fun]]. Its companion analysis finds creators who treat creation as work earning the most and working the most hours but showing tenure indistinguishable from play-oriented creators [[play-playbour-or-labour-the-relationships-between-perception-of]].

A quasi-causal test of the income-shock hypothesis points the same way. On the 2017–18 YouTube demonetization shock, a Patreon panel of 11,371 creators and 131,006 creator-months shows the affected population growing rather than shrinking, with multi-homing creators increasing paid content and patron counts relative to Patreon-only controls [[r-a-p-h-a-e-l-a-a-n-d-r-e-s-m-i-c-h-e-l-a-n-g-e-lo-r-oss-i]]. The regression-discontinuity companion — a design comparing units just above and just below a cutoff — finds the complement for creators without an alternate channel: losing Partner Program eligibility cut uploads by 2.816 per month against a sample mean of 3.248, roughly an 87% collapse in output, and keyword diversity by 25.38 against a mean of 41.62 [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]]. In that same German cohort, creators who exited had higher average subscribers and watchtime than those who stayed, and a smaller share had lost Partner Program access. The one behavioural datapoint linking income insufficiency to changed conduct is UK-specific: among 311 members of UK music trade bodies, 43% said insufficient streaming income caused them to take a job outside music — diversification rather than exit [[music-creators-earnings-in]].

Zhao's and Wollborn's results (a structural threshold predicts persistence) combined with the Andres and El-Komboz results (income shocks change allocation and output rather than membership) jointly imply that income determines who can continue while motivation determines who does, among those who can. No source states this; it follows from those four.

### C. The absence, and its two near-misses

Two studies come close enough to make the gap precise. A census-weighted US survey of 5,034 podcast consumers reaches a genuinely lapsed creator population — 6% of the base, roughly half the size of the active creator group — cross-tabulates it demographically, and lists "what specific factors drive creator abandonment" as an open question rather than asking it [[the-creators-2025-understanding-the-modern-podcast-creator-landscape-sounds-prof]]. A CSCW study recruited both current and former YouTube creators for 20 interviews on demonetization and contains no mention of quitting, stopping, leaving or burnout anywhere in 37 pages [[give-everybody-a-little-bit-more-equity-content-creator-perspectives-and-respons]]. The closest exit-reason elicitation anywhere is non-English and samples the wrong side of the transaction: among 1,356 lapsed Japanese Niconico viewers (screening n=3,328, fielded 24 August – 7 September 2021), "creators and streamers moved to other services" was the top or joint-top reason for stopping in every one of six demographic cells, at 29–42% [[why-niconico-in-japan-lost-game-jikkyo-streamers-and-their-audience]]. That measures audience response to creator exit.

## 5. Geographic Scope: Which Findings Are US/Western-Only, and What Is Known for Southeast Asia

Almost every measurement above was taken on a US, UK, Nordic, German or global-platform-with-no-country-split sample. Three things break when those findings are carried to Southeast Asia: the currency levels, the living-wage threshold they are compared against, and the revenue mechanics that generate creator income in the first place. The distribution shapes may well travel; the dollar figures and the shares computed against dollar thresholds do not. This section marks the scope of each load-bearing finding, gives the measured mechanisms of non-transfer, and reports what regional evidence exists.

### B. Four measured mechanisms of non-transfer

The living-wage threshold itself is the largest. The living wage for developing Asia is estimated at US$254.28 a month anchored on the absolute poverty line and US$414.88 on the moderate line, assuming a family of four and a 48-hour week [[adbi-working-paper-series]]. Western creator studies benchmark against a US federal monthly minimum near US$1,160, a US$50,000-a-year bar, or a US$67,521 median household income — a gap of roughly three to twenty-two times. Revenue per thousand views differs by roughly five to six times between core and peripheral audience markets, driven by nothing the creator controls: 1,000 views at 70% inventory fill yield roughly INR 30, under US$1, from an Indian audience against US$5 from a US audience [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051241234693]]. Off-platform monetization infrastructure is thinner, with India, Indonesia and Thailand over-represented among channels carrying no links at all [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]]. And the counterfactual differs: informal employment exceeds 70% of non-agricultural employment in Indonesia and the Philippines, exceeds 60% in Vietnam and is 38% in Thailand [[adbi-working-paper-series]], so creator self-employment is not the departure from a salaried norm that it is in the US. "Earning money on social media" also often means something else entirely — 71% of Indonesian e-commerce participants sell exclusively via social media or chat apps [[technologies]].

The most-cited Southeast Asian influencer sector report contains no creator-side survey at all; its only quantified instrument is a demand-side consumer survey of 2,400 people across six markets [[role-of-influencer-marketing]]. Two Vietnamese-language pieces presented as local creator-income coverage are paraphrases of US survey data benchmarked to US living wages [[báo-cáo-thu-nhập-nhà-sáng-tạo-nội-dung-2025]]. Institutional coverage confirms the vacuum: of 133 countries responding to an ILO review, only 40 have ever measured digital platform employment, most of them European, and no international statistical standard will exist before the 22nd International Conference of Labour Statisticians in 2028 [[ilo-brief]].

Two sourced findings jointly imply a third that no study has tested. Persistence models find structural access to monetization infrastructure to be the significant predictor of continuation [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]] [[vol0123456789]], and regional evidence finds Southeast Asian creators with systematically worse structural access [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051241234693]] [[httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628]]. If both hold, Southeast Asian attrition should exceed Western attrition. No dataset in this record can test that, and the 51-point intra-regional spread suggests it would not hold uniformly. It is offered as a labelled inference and identifies the highest-value unrun study in the field.

## 6. Evidence Quality and Measurement Caveats

The problems in this literature are not mainly arithmetic errors. They are structural: samples drawn from populations that exclude the people the question is about, denominators that shift without notice, and headline statistics whose apparent authority comes from repetition rather than measurement. Four failure modes recur across every section above, and one of them is severe enough that two sources encountered during this work carry entirely fabricated apparatus.

### A. Survivorship bias is the dominant failure mode

Any sample recruited from active creators cannot observe anyone who already left, and every burnout instrument in this record is of that type. Institutions concede the constraint in their own methodology: the ILO states there was no sampling base from which a random sample of platform workers could be drawn [[ilo-flagship-report]], and the World Bank found self-identified gig workers the most likely to drop out mid-survey [[the-promise-and-peril]]. Even a Twitch telemetry panel carries a selection floor of one hourly top-500 placement, roughly 21 concurrent viewers [[vol0123456789]]. The scale of the effect is bounded but not calibrated: the clearest paired measurement concerns affiliate-link prevalence, at 36.7% on a dataset restricted to channels above 10,000 subscribers against 3.49% for the same category on a random-prefix sample of roughly 500,000 videos [[characterizing-alternative-monetization-strategies-on]] [[endorsements-on-social-media-an-empirical-study-of]]. That roughly tenfold gap demonstrates the mechanism is real and large on a monetization-practice metric. It is not a coefficient for income and must not be applied to income figures as one; no study has directly measured the sampling-frame effect on creator earnings.

### B. Denominator and reference-period instability

The Twitch leak's denominator is stated at least six incompatible ways across outlets covering one dataset, and its observation window five ways [[the-unbearable-lightness-of-streaming-superjoost-playlist]] [[many-top-twitch-streamers-dont-make-minimum-wage-and-3-new-takeaways-from-the-tw]]. Reference period alone moves measured platform-work prevalence by a factor of three to nine — from 5.7% of Norway's working-age population over a year to 1.5% over a month [[ilo-brief]]. Threshold changes break cohort comparability outright: YouTube's February 2018 rule change severs comparison with earlier cohorts [[el-komboz-lena-abou-kerkhof-anna-loh-johannes]], and TikTok economics are not comparable across the 16 December 2023 boundary at which the Creativity Program fully replaced the Creator Fund in the US, UK, France and Germany [[unlocking-even-more-opportunities-for-creators-with-the-creativity-program-beta]].

The four metadata fields this report attaches to every figure — sample size, year, platform, country — are necessary but insufficient. Sources here also differ in unit of analysis (person, persona, channel, account, registered profile, payroll job), in denominator scope (all registered, all active, all monetized, self-identified primary occupation, payroll-only), and in whether income is gross or net. Nearly every figure here is gross of the creator's own costs. The ILO's own criteria for a credible platform-earnings figure — gross separated from net, operating costs, unpaid waiting time, working-time intensity, volatility — are met by essentially no published creator income statistic [[ilo-philippines-clarifies-misuse-of-preliminary-platform-work-survey-findings-in]].

Applying one standard to both chains produces an asymmetric result: the Southeast Asian chain has a survey underneath it, and the Western chain does not. Three properties of the Western chain compound this. The professional count is held flat at 2.0 million from 2022 to 2025 while the total grows by 16.6 million, so every net entrant in the model is an amateur by construction. The definition of "professional" changes silently between the 2023 article and the 2025 report. And the companion concentration statistic in wide circulation — "3% of YouTubers make 90% of the wealth" — is a unit transformation of a peer-reviewed finding that on average 85% of *views* accrue to 3% of channels [[opus-4-youtube-channels-uploads-and-views-a-statistical-analysis-of-the-past-10]].

The precise charge against the Indonesian figure narrows accordingly. The 63% rests on a real if thin instrument and is citable with its caveats. The companion "8 million Indonesian creators" headcount appears nowhere in the primary report and nowhere in the vendor's methodology summary [[the-tiktok-effect]] [[tiktoks-socio-economic-impact-in-southeast-asia-a-summary-of-the-report-kadence]]. The failure is the merger: two figures from different sources, years and methods were welded into one statistic at a parliamentary hearing — where the same official, pressed for Indonesia revenue, stated on record that he did not have the data [[anggota-dpr-cecar-omzet-tiktok-mengaku-belum-siap-data-teknologi]] — and then repeated as a national statistic. Indonesia's statistics agency publishes no creator count at all [[bps-dan-kemenekraf-bersinergi-kreator-lokal-dilibatkan-dalam-sosialisasi-se2026]].

### D. Fabricated apparatus, and aggregates without denominators

Two sources encountered here carry invented scholarly furniture, and neither may be cited as evidence. A 2026 trade article attributes a full statistical battery — 62% burnout, 47% considered leaving, n=2,400 full-time creators — to a "Creator Economy Research Institute" that exists in no register, and whose headline figure exactly matches a real n=542 study published three months earlier [[the-creator-burnout-epidemic-why-62-of-full-time-creators-say-theyre-exhausted-t]]. A 2025 Indonesian journal article presents precisely the instrument this question needs — n=60 creators, five cities, a stated fielding window, a full income-distribution table — while its entire 24-item reference list concerns photocatalysis, laser optics, orthopaedic screw fixation and supernova neutrinos, two in-text citations are absent from its own bibliography, and its named case-study creators cannot be found under their stated handles [[pengaruh-ekonomi-kreator-digital-terhadap-pendapatan-generasi-z-di-perkotaan-stu]]. Both pass a metadata-only screen; neither passes a reference-list check. Their existence is a finding about the field's information environment.

Two further categories of figure cannot be converted into per-creator quantities at all. KPMG's estimate that the twelve largest media companies spent US$210 billion on content in 2024 comes from public filings for twelve named firms with no creator count anywhere in the document [[the-future-of]]. And a widely circulated channel-count composite is described by its own author as manual open-source intelligence assembled from Google Ads, SEMrush, Social Blade, Statista and SimilarWeb, because YouTube publishes neither figure [[how-many-youtube-channels-are-there-in-2026-tim-queen]].

### E. What becomes measurable next

Three dated developments would change what this question can be answered with. A YouTube channel census built from 1.21 billion Internet Archive captures, covering at least 106 million unique channels with subscriber histories reaching back to 2006, is already public and has not yet carried a survival model — it is the first dataset capable of producing a genuine channel survival curve, though its coverage bias correlates with subscriber count and would undercount the death of small channels, precisely where attrition is highest [[tube-census]]. Indonesia's 2026 Economic Census is billed as the first national instrument to enumerate content-creator business actors, which would give Southeast Asia's largest creator population the first administrative denominator anywhere in this literature [[bps-dan-kemenekraf-bersinergi-kreator-lokal-dilibatkan-dalam-sosialisasi-se2026]]. And the ILO standard due at the 22nd International Conference of Labour Statisticians in 2028 would make national platform-work counts comparable for the first time, settling centrally whether content creation sits inside or outside "platform work" [[ilo-brief]]. Until at least one lands, the two highest-value studies in this field remain unrun: a cohort survival curve over a full channel population, and an exit survey that asks people who stopped why they stopped. The second is already half-built, since one US survey reaches a lapsed creator population and does not put the question to it [[the-creators-2025-understanding-the-modern-podcast-creator-landscape-sounds-prof]].
