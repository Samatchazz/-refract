- [[characterizing-alternative-monetization-strategies-on]] — Characterizing Alternative Monetization Strategies on — Hua, Horta Ribeiro, Ristenpart, West & Naaman (Cornell Tech / EPFL), 'Characterizing Alternative Monetization Strategies on YouTube', arXiv:2203.10143v2 [cs.CY], 6 Oct 2022 (CSCW). Not an attrition or income-distribution study, but supplies three things this research question needs: a hard number on how many creators monetize off-platform, a rare direct measurement of lifetime Patreon earnings, and an unusually clean demonstration of survivorship/sampling bias in creator datasets.\n\nSAMPLE: the YouNiverse dataset - 71,089,725 videos from 136,091 ENGLISH-SPEAKING YouTube channels, every one of which had at least 10,000 subscribers at collection time (late 2019). Plus 138,538 videos from 291 Alt-lite/Alt-right channels and 63,191 videos from 279 Manosphere channels (497 'problematic' channels total after dedup). The authors flag the popularity bias explicitly: the 10,000-subscriber floor means the dataset systematically excludes creators who never reached that threshold, and the filter is harsher for older cohorts.\n\nADOPTION FINDINGS: 18.4% of videos and 60.6% of channels used at least one alternative (off-platform) monetization strategy. By type - affiliate marketing 11.3% of videos / 51.2% of channels; donations 8.1% / 28.4%; own products 3.0% / 11.3%; cryptocurrency 0.2% / 0.7%. Patreon accounts for 56.3% of donation-link uses; Bitcoin and Ethereum are 68% of crypto addresses. Category variation is large: How To & Style 36.7% of videos / 83.5% of channels use affiliate links; gaming 11.3% / 47.5% affiliate but 18.1% / 49.7% donations. Adoption rises with subscriber decile: 12.5% of top-decile channels (>440k subs) post merchandise links versus 3.6% of the bottom decile (<12.7k subs), and 72% of the most productive decile adopt some alternative monetization versus 30% of the least productive decile (<=29 videos).\n\nSAMPLING-BIAS DEMONSTRATION (directly load-bearing for evaluating other creator statistics): the same quantity measured on an unbiased random-prefix sample by Mathur et al. gives 3.49% of How To & Style videos containing affiliate links, versus 36.7% here. A tenfold difference produced purely by sampling from popular channels instead of the whole platform. Any creator statistic drawn from a threshold-gated or self-selected population should be treated as inflated by roughly this order of magnitude.\n\nMATCHED-PAIR PRODUCTIVITY RESULT: 5,730 pairs of channels matched on content category and on both view-decile and video-decile for three consecutive months before adoption (adoption month between January 2015 and September 2019). In the first month after adoption, adopters posted 6 videos vs 3 for non-adopters; across months 1-12 adopters posted 63 videos vs 44, an increase of over 43%. The authors explicitly refuse a causal reading.\n\nDIRECT EARNINGS MEASUREMENT: using Graphtreon data, of the 497 problematic channels, 97 listed a Patreon link and had a MEDIAN LIFETIME Patreon income of USD 5,540; 58 listed a Bitcoin/Ethereum address with a MEDIAN LIFETIME crypto income of USD 1,155 (to November 2020). 68 of 97 channels earned over USD 1,000 lifetime on Patreon. One outlier, Honey Badger Radio, earned over USD 260,000 on Patreon across six years. Caveat stated by the authors: the linked Patreon or wallet may not belong to the channel owner, so these may be overestimates.\n\nGEOGRAPHY: ENGLISH-LANGUAGE CHANNELS ONLY. The authors note the US is under 20% of YouTube traffic but their dataset is entirely English-speaking, so all figures are Western/anglophone and should NOT be generalised to Southeast Asia. LONG SOURCE, 16,245 words - candidate for source-analyst delegation. `review`
- [[concurrent-streaming-viewer-transfers-and]] — Concurrent Streaming, Viewer Transfers, and — Maxwell Shepherd (Johns Hopkins), 'Concurrent Streaming, Viewer Transfers, and Audience Loyalty in a Creator Ecosystem: A Minute-Level Longitudinal Study', arXiv:2603.23773v1 [cs.SI], 24 March 2026. QUALITY VERDICT: peripheral to this research question. It contains NO income figures and NO attrition or survival rates. Its relevance is that it quantifies audience non-exclusivity and within-ecosystem attention competition - a mechanism behind why creator earnings do not scale with effort.\n\nSAMPLE: 2,935,985 minute-by-minute viewer-count observations across 7,762 livestreams from 18 YouTube channels, all belonging to Hololive English (HoloEN), November 2022 to March 2026 (3.3 years). Median stream duration 181.2 minutes; mean 7,587 viewers per stream; max single-stream 243,897 viewers. These are elite VTubers inside the dominant agency, not a representative creator sample - the author states plainly that focusing on a single organisation 'limits generalizability'.\n\nFINDINGS. (1) Concurrency dilution: mean per-stream audience falls from 14,377 viewers when a creator streams alone to 6,057 when eight ecosystem colleagues stream simultaneously - a 58% raw drop. But after residualising on hour-of-day the within-hour competition correlation is only rho = -0.165 (95% CI [-0.186, -0.145], p<0.001, day-level block bootstrap over 1,209 days, 2,000 iterations), so most of the raw drop is scheduling confound, not audience splitting. (2) Viewer transfers ('raids'): 3,243 candidate transfer events detected; among plausible events (efficiency <=100%) mean spike is 2,552 viewers and MEDIAN transfer efficiency is about 49% (IQR 29-60%), i.e. roughly half a departing audience follows to another ecosystem stream and half leaves entirely. Top 10 channel pairs account for 16% of events. ~5% estimated false positives. (3) Audience exclusivity is a creator-level, not organisation-level, property: Competition Resistance ranges from 0.364 (Gura) to 1.000 (Kiara) among channels in the SAME agency; Post-Peak Retention is uniformly high across channels.\n\nACTIVITY DISPARITY (useful as an effort benchmark): among these 18 elite channels, streaming frequency ranges from 3.1 streams/month (Gura, 90 streams) to 22.8 streams/month (Bijou, 720 streams) - a 7x spread in output within one agency - and observed active periods run 632 days (most recent debut cohort) to 1,220 days for the earliest channels. All 18 are still active, so this yields no exit data.\n\nCAVEATS the author states: viewer counts not identities, so overlap is a proxy; the dilution analysis is observational not causal; transfer detection is heuristic and cannot separate deliberate raids from organic migration; loyalty composite weights (0.30/0.25/0.25/0.20) are subjective.\n\nGEOGRAPHY: Hololive ENGLISH branch only - anglophone audience, Japanese parent agency. Not generalisable to Southeast Asian creators, though Hololive does operate a separate Indonesian branch not covered here. `review`
- [[endorsements-on-social-media-an-empirical-study-of]] — Endorsements on Social Media: An Empirical Study of — Mathur, Narayanan & Chetty (Princeton), 'Endorsements on Social Media: An Empirical Study of Affiliate Marketing Disclosures on YouTube and Pinterest,' Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. 2, CSCW, Art. 119 (Nov 2018); arXiv:1809.00620. THIS IS THE PRIMARY SOURCE of the 3.49% figure that Hua et al. 2022 (Characterizing Alternative Monetization Strategies on YouTube) cite by way of contrast to their own 36.7% figure -- confirming the 10x gap is real and traceable, but resolving what quantity it actually measures.\n\nMETHOD: the authors built an unbiased sample using 'random prefix sampling' -- generating random video/pin IDs directly rather than crawling from popular seed channels -- explicitly to avoid the popularity bias inherent in crawls that start from trending/subscribed content. Final samples: ~500,000 unique YouTube videos and ~2.1 million unique Pinterest pins (collected 2017). This is a genuinely representative, non-popularity-gated sample of platform content, unlike Hua et al.'s YouNiverse dataset which required channels to have 10,000+ subscribers.\n\nFINDING (the traced figure): the authors compiled 57 unique affiliate-URL patterns from 33 affiliate marketing companies (the most comprehensive public list at the time) and searched video/pin descriptions for them. Overall, only 0.67% (3,472) of YouTube videos and 0.85% (18,237) of Pinterest pins contained at least one affiliate URL. Ranking by content category (categories with >=100 affiliate items only), the highest-affiliate categories were: YouTube Science & Technology 3.61%, YouTube Howto & Style 3.49% (THIS is the exact figure Hua et al. cite), Pinterest Women's Fashion 4.62%, Pinterest Hair & Beauty 2.04%. Top affiliate programs by raw link count: Amazon Associates (YouTube 7,308 / Pinterest 7,368 occurrences), AliExpress (YouTube 2,167 / Pinterest 785).\n\nCRITICAL DISAMBIGUATION FOR THE RESEARCH QUESTION: this paper has NOTHING to do with creator income levels, living-wage thresholds, or earnings share. The 3.49%/36.7% pair measures the PREVALENCE OF AFFILIATE-MARKETING LINKS IN VIDEO DESCRIPTIONS within the Howto & Style category -- a disclosure/monetization-practice metric, not an income or living-wage metric. Whatever attributed the 36.7%/3.49% split to a 'living wage' quantity in the contradiction graph is a mischaracterization: the actual comparison is (a) different papers (Mathur et al. 2018 vs. Hua et al. 2022, not two arms of one study), (b) a different platform-content metric (affiliate link presence, not earnings), and (c) specific to one content category (Howto & Style), not creators/income generally. The 10x sampling-bias phenomenon itself is real and well-documented (popularity-gated crawl vs. random-prefix sample), but it cannot be used as direct evidence for a living-wage share divergence without independent verification that a comparable sampling-bias study exists for income specifically.\n\nSecondary finding: disclosure of affiliate relationships was rare across both platforms -- the paper's broader thesis is that endorsement-disclosure norms (per FTC guidelines) are widely violated. `review`
- [[mapping-youtube-a-quantitative-exploration-of-a-platformed-media-system]] — Mapping YouTube: A quantitative exploration of a platformed media system — Rieder, Coromina & Matamoros-Fernandez, 'Mapping YouTube: A quantitative exploration of a platformed media system', First Monday 25(8), 2020, DOI 10.5210/fm.v25i8.10667. DISCOVERED IN PHASE 2 - this is the underlying crawl that supplies the denominator and the view distribution used in the per-tier income table in Rieder et al. 2023. It is the population census against which every creator-income statistic in this corpus should be normalised. Also, unexpectedly, it is one of the only sources in the batch with per-country data covering Indonesia and Thailand.\n\nSAMPLE: a snowball crawl following featured-channel and subscription links, seeded and bounded by a 1,000-subscriber cutoff. It discovered 4,415,180 channels with more than 1,000 subscribers and 36,336,861 channels in total - above then-existing estimates of YouTube's channel population. The authors state a HIERARCHY OF CONFIDENCE: the 100k+ and 10k-100k datasets are near-complete; the 1k-10k dataset is missing an estimated 10-15%; and the roughly 32 million channels below 1,000 subscribers should be treated 'with even more caution'. Crawled 2019-2020.\n\nYOUTUBE'S OWN TIER SYSTEM (important context for every monetization statistic): activating a channel gives 'graphite' status; 1,000 SUBSCRIBERS is the 'opal' threshold and the gate to advertising monetization; 10,000 subscribers ('bronze') gives access to YouTube Space, pop-up events and training; 100,000 ('silver and up') gets a dedicated partner manager and awards, and - per Kumar 2019 - prioritised handling of demonetization appeals. Any creator below 1,000 subscribers earns nothing from advertising by design.\n\nCONCENTRATION: the 153,000 elite channels above 100,000 subscribers are 0.42% of the sample and account for 69.2% of subscribers and 62.4% of views while producing only 8.9% of videos. Going further up, the 15,496 channels above 1 million subscribers (0.04%) take 37% of subscribers and 37.4% of views on 2.3% of videos - which the authors note is 'a much heavier skew than the 80/20 Pareto principle'. Subscriber and view counts correlate at 0.74. Below the 1,000-subscriber monetization threshold the average subscriber count falls to 122. More than half of all videos published by the 4.4 million monetizable channels fail to reach 550 views.\n\nEFFORT VS TIME - directly relevant to 'time from starting to first meaningful income': elite channels have published on average 940 videos versus 294 for the next tier down, but 'this is not simply an effect of having been around for a longer time: if we compare the average number of days active since the starting date, the numbers are surprisingly constant between tiers.' In other words, tenure does not distinguish successful from unsuccessful channels; output volume does. This is the clearest quantitative statement in the corpus that waiting does not produce success.\n\nGEOGRAPHY - unusually useful here. US channels are more than 15% of the elite tier, followed by India, Brazil, Russia, INDONESIA, Great Britain, Mexico, Spain and THAILAND. The US and Great Britain 'punch above their weight', achieving more subscribers and views with fewer videos, which the authors attribute to the English-language advantage. India, South Korea and Egypt publish disproportionate numbers of videos; only Korea converts that into views (K-pop). 'Countries like Brazil and Indonesia stand out for achieving far fewer views than their overall share of channels would suggest.' Thailand and Turkey stand out with HIGH average views per channel. Western channels are on average older; Asian channels (India, South Korea, Pakistan, Vietnam) are younger. Caution: in the full 4.4M monetizable network, 53.6% of channels have NO country set at all and the US accounts for 8.4%, so country-level creator analysis covers under half the platform.\n\nOTHER: Gaming covers 10.7% of all channels; Entertainment and Lifestyle dominate all metrics, Music close behind. Over 25% of published videos are under two minutes. Metadata quality decays going down the tiers, so lower-tier channels are systematically less visible to any metadata-based research method - a bias that compounds survivorship bias in every YouTube study.\n\nLONG SOURCE, 13,334 words - candidate for source-analyst delegation. `review`
- [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2023]] — Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2023 — Hron, Krauth, Jordan, Kilbertus & Dean, 'Modeling Content Creator Incentives on Algorithm-Curated Platforms', ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2206.13102). QUALITY VERDICT: on-topic domain but supplies NO measurable creator statistics - it is a game-theoretic modelling paper, not an empirical one. Formalises creator competition for finite user attention as an 'exposure game' and proves that recommender architecture choices (non-negative vs unconstrained matrix factorization, two-tower depth) change whether Nash equilibria exist. Numerical audits are run on MovieLens and LastFM, i.e. simulated creators over movie/music rating datasets, NOT real creator earnings or exit data. There is no sample of creators, no income figure, no survival rate, no country.\n\nTwo things in it are usable for this research question, both secondhand. (1) A taxonomy of YouTube creator revenue streams (Figure 2, after Ormen & Gregersen 2022): YouTube Partner Programme ad share, YouTube Premium, direct user payment via Super Chat and Super Stickers, memberships, and off-platform webshops, merch, sponsored content, donation services such as PayPal and Patreon, and affiliate marketing. (2) A stated premise, citing Cheng et al. 2008: 'Most creators do not earn significant income, but the majority of traffic is driven by only a few popular and high-earning creators' - offered as an assumption to justify modelling only top creators, with no figures attached.\n\nUSEFUL LEAD: the related-work section points to Mladenov et al. 2020, which explicitly models 'producers who decide whether to stay or leave the platform if their exposure is too low' - the closest thing in this literature to a formal creator-exit model. Also cites Ben-Porat et al. 2019/2020 on repeated creator-platform interaction.\n\nUse this source only for framing the exposure-competition mechanism behind creator exit, never as a numeric citation. `review`
- [[quantifying-the-creator-economy-a-large-scale-analysis-of-patreon]] — Quantifying the Creator Economy: A Large-Scale Analysis of Patreon — El Sanyoura & Anderson (University of Toronto), 'Quantifying the Creator Economy: A Large-Scale Analysis of Patreon', ICWSM 2022, DOI 10.1609/icwsm.v16i1.19338. NOTE ON PROVENANCE: the AAAI OJS direct-download link (the URL in the assigned batch) triggers a browser download handler that the fetcher cannot process; the PDF was retrieved with curl and ingested via a local loopback server. The canonical URL and DOI have been restored in this note's frontmatter. Content is the authentic published paper.\n\nSAMPLE: the COMPLETE set of Patreon pledge transactions from the platform's inception in June 2013 to December 2020 - hundreds of millions of patron-creator transactions totalling over USD 2 billion paid to creators. Each transaction has an anonymised patron ID, creator ID, pledge amount and date; creator metadata includes display name, URL, summary, modality and topic. This is administrative platform data, not survey data, and it is the largest creator-payments dataset in the published literature. At the time of writing Patreon paid over USD 100 million to creators per month.\n\nEARNINGS-DISTRIBUTION FINDINGS. Within-topic creator inequality rose sharply: the Gini coefficient across creators inside a topic climbed from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.60 in December 2020. Across topics, by contrast, inequality FELL slightly, from 0.80 (mid-2015) to 0.72 (2020) - so the platform diversified into more subject areas while concentrating earnings within each one. Top-decile creators receive nearly USD 15 per pledge versus USD 2 for bottom-decile creators, so high earners win on both audience size AND price per patron; mean pledge amounts differ by over USD 12 across content modalities at the top of the distribution. Per-creator pledge Gini (inequality among a single creator's own patrons) is at most 0.45 on average - moderate, and higher for higher-earning creators. Low-earning creators receive virtually all their income from small (<=USD 5) pledges.\n\nAUDIENCE FINDINGS. The audiences of the LARGEST creators pledge to another creator only 40% of the time, versus 90% for the smallest creators - a >50 percentage-point gap. Large creators generate 'one-and-done' patrons who join the platform solely for them, meaning top creators do not feed the ecosystem. Patrons who support a narrow set of creators are more loyal per-creator but churn off the platform more often; patrons pledging to more creators retain pledges better (the difference in churn between highest and lowest activity levels is only 1-2 percentage points, but the loyalty gap is around 10 percentage points). At any one time, all creator size tiers get the majority of pledges from maintained (returning) patrons, and all tiers show positive audience growth - smaller creators actually show HIGHER relative growth, which the authors read as evidence newcomers can still gain prominence.\n\nMETHODOLOGICAL NOTE that matters for this research question: Patreon 'does not offer algorithmic or human-curated recommendations, and there is no notion of a patron-patron social network', so patrons arrive only via search or off-platform links. This makes Patreon a rare algorithm-free control case against which YouTube/Instagram/TikTok recommendation effects can be compared.\n\nWHAT IT DOES NOT CONTAIN: no creator attrition or exit rates, no median creator earnings figure, no time-to-first-income measure, no reasons-for-quitting data, and no country breakdown. Patreon's creator base is heavily US/Western, so all figures should be flagged as Western and not generalised to Southeast Asia. `review`
- [[tube-census]] — TUBE-CENSUS: — Eggleston, Handler & Pacheco (University of Colorado Boulder), arXiv:2605.06999v1 [cs.SI], 7 May 2026. A DATA-INFRASTRUCTURE paper, not an attrition or income study - but it is the best available documentation of WHY creator-attrition statistics do not exist, and it supplies population denominators. The authors mined every Internet Archive Wayback Machine capture of YouTube channel URLs through end-2023 across four URL formats, yielding 1.21 billion channel snapshots and at least 106 million unique channels (username format 372M snapshots / 34.8M channels; channel-ID format 683M / 106M; custom /c/ 62.4M / 5.9M; handle /@ 94.5M / 25.4M), with subscriber counts dating to 2006.\n\nThe measurement-gap finding is directly load-bearing for this research question: 'the official YouTube API does not provide access to the full set of creators or creator metadata on the platform' and exposes only CURRENT, never historical, subscriber counts. The main commercial alternative, Social Blade, 'lists only the top 5,000 channels at a given time, and provides historical metadata spanning at most the past three years'. This means no public dataset can currently answer 'what fraction of YouTube channels stop posting, and how soon' - the paper exists precisely because that class of question was previously unanswerable.\n\nCOVERAGE VALIDATION: stratified sample of 34,000 channels (2,000 each from 17 capture-frequency strata), video counts observed October 2025, gives a weighted mean of 46.3 videos per channel (SE 2.3), implying a 95% CI of 4.39-5.34 billion videos covered, i.e. approximately 30-36% of all YouTube videos. Spearman rank correlation between snapshot count and subscriber count is rho = 0.354 (username, n=684,763), 0.432 (ID, n=577,624), 0.221 (custom, n=284,306), 0.400 (handle, n=90,160) - so the census over-represents large channels, an explicit survivorship/prominence bias. Cohort 1 (2006-2013) covers 2,997,954 captures for 1,047,839 channels and reproduces Social Blade's December 2010/2011/2012 top-500 lists at ~96% overlap.\n\nGROWTH FINDING (Section 7): among the top 75 most-subscribed channels of January 2014, subscriber trajectories follow logistic S-curves - 'grows slowly, then quickly, and then eventually approach an asymptote' - which the authors liken to ecological carrying capacity: a channel captures attention within a niche until it hits a fundamental limit. Two documented deviations: spin-off channels from already-successful creators skip the slow initial phase entirely, and celebrity musicians grow in spurts unrelated to platform dynamics. This is the closest thing in the paper to a 'time to traction' finding and it is drawn only from already-successful channels.\n\nAppendix Table 10 gives median subscribers/videos/views by BERTopic genre for 208,893 English-description channels (e.g. gaming n=12,381 median 7,110 subs; local politics n=1,331 median 53 subs, 25 videos, 11,241 views; South Asian film/music n=1,572 median 300,000 subs) - but these medians apply only to channels captured >=20 times by the Internet Archive, so they are upward-biased and are NOT a population median.\n\nGEOGRAPHY: US-authored, global YouTube data, but the topic-model analysis is restricted to English-language descriptions, so the genre medians exclude nearly all Southeast Asian non-English creators. No income, no earnings, no explicit exit or survival rates. `review`

Three in four creators are male (from the 23,840 who supplied gender). - [[who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income]] — Who Reaps All the Superchats? A Large-Scale Analysis of Income — Zhao, Diep, Pei, Yoon, Jurgens & Zhu (UBC / Stanford / Michigan), arXiv:2503.00825v1 [cs.HC], 2 March 2025, CHI-format. The single most useful source in this batch for the ATTRITION half of the query: it is one of the few papers that reports an actual creator survival analysis with a Cox proportional-hazards model, on observed platform telemetry rather than self-report.\n\nSAMPLE: 1,923 Virtual YouTubers (VTubers) sampled from Virtual YouTuber Wiki (~2,800 listed, >15 languages), whose YouTube livestreams were scraped for public livechat records. Coverage: early 2017 to July 2023 (6.5 years), >1 million streaming hours across ~450,000 sessions, 17 billion total views, 6.75 million unique commenting viewers, 61 currencies converted to USD at daily ECB rates. Breakdown: 1,238 agency-affiliated (Hololive 70, Nijisanji 192, 774 Inc 25, other agencies 951) and 635 independent. GEOGRAPHY: this is a JAPAN/EAST-ASIA-ORIGIN, globally distributed sample - VTubing began in Japan, Hololive operates dedicated Japanese, English and INDONESIAN branches - which makes it the one source here with meaningful non-Western coverage, though the paper does not report country-level breakdowns.\n\nSURVIVAL FINDINGS (Section 7): median lifespan (first stream to last stream) of ALL VTubers is 24 months. By affiliation: 44 months for large agencies (Hololive/Nijisanji), 28 months for independents, 21 months for small agencies - all pairwise differences significant (Large vs Other p=1.3e-28; Large vs Independent p=2.07e-15; Independent vs Other p=2.03e-7). 1,057 of 1,923 (over 50%) had stopped streaming completely within 3 years of their first stream; among those who stopped, median lifespan was about 19 months. Cox model (concordance 0.680, sigma 0.023, 5-fold grouped CV): hazard ratio 1.980 for small/other agency affiliation (p=0.003) and 1.296 for independents (p=0.302, not significant) relative to large-agency baseline; total viewers exp(beta)=0.983 (p=0.001); total members exp(beta)=2.124 (p=1.6e-8, raises initial hazard) with a negative member-x-time interaction 0.976 (p=0.001); Superchat income x time 0.993 (p=0.010). Gender and stream duration were NOT significant. Right-censoring: any VTuber whose last stream fell within 3 months of the July 2023 cutoff is treated as censored.\n\nINCOME FINDINGS: mean monthly Superchat income across all VTubers is ,667 but the MEDIAN is only 27 - a 21x mean/median ratio. 25% of VTubers received zero Superchats in the entire observation window ('only 75% of them had received at least one Superchat'). Between-VTuber Gini rose from ~0.60 (2018) to 0.75 (2023). Within-viewer concentration: of 6.75M unique commenting viewers, 7.96% ever sent a Superchat and 12.65% ever bought a membership; over 80% spent nothing. Per-VTuber Superchat Gini among paying viewers is around and above 0.6, saturating at 0.8-0.9 as the paying base grows. Median share of a VTuber's chatters who are Superchat senders is 5% (14% among loyal viewers, 22% among members). Most Superchats are -0; YouTube caps Superchats at ,000/week. Memberships are short and shallow: median viewer subscribes to 1 VTuber (mean 3.06, Q75 3.00).\n\nAGENCY CONCENTRATION: Hololive + Nijisanji take more than 60% of total views and more Superchat income than all other VTubers combined; the top independent (Kamito) earned one-eighth of the top corporate VTuber (Kiryu Coco) and did not place in the top 100 by income. Note that agencies take a large cut, so VTuber take-home is materially below observed Superchat gross.\n\nCAVEATS: only publicly-visible Superchat and membership revenue is observed - sponsorships, merchandise, concerts and ad revenue are invisible, and Hololive's own financial reports say merchandising overtook Superchats/memberships after 2022, so this UNDERSTATES total top-creator income and probably overstates the relative importance of tipping. Sampling is from a fandom wiki, which favours notable VTubers, so real attrition across all VTubers is very likely worse than the reported 50%-in-3-years. LONG SOURCE, 14,394 words - candidate for source-analyst delegation. `review`
