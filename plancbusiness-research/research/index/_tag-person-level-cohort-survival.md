THE closest open-source analogue to a person-level creator/channel cohort-survival curve found in this search. Unit of analysis: PERSON (individual Debian package maintainer/volunteer), tracked as a literal cohort across time -- not project-level aggregate turnover. Data: Debian's public Sources.gz package-maintainer records across 5 stable releases spanning July 1998 (Debian 2.0) to December 2004 (Debian 3.1 candidate), a 6.5-year window, cross-referenced with the Debian Popularity Contest usage-telemetry system. Core finding (Table III): of the 216 individual maintainers active at Debian 2.0 (Jul 1998), only 207 remained by 2.1 (Mar 1999), 188 by 2.2 (Aug 2000), 147 by 3.0 (Jul 2002), and 121 (55.8%) were still contributing by December 2004 -- a monotonic cohort-decay curve tracked release-over-release for 6.5 years. Because the cohort had not yet fallen to 50% within the observed window, the authors extrapolate a contributor half-life (time for a maintainer cohort to fall to half its initial size) of approximately 7.5 years (90 months); they note this trend held regardless of which release was used as the starting cohort. Total Debian maintainer population grew from 216 (1998) to ~1,237 (2004), roughly 35%/year growth, even as the surviving-cohort count shrank -- i.e., aggregate headline growth co-exists with steep individual attrition, exactly the confound the user's research question is probing. Secondary finding: packages orphaned by departing maintainers are adopted by others >60% of the time across all release pairs, evidencing self-organizing 'regeneration' at the project level even as individual half-life is ~7.5 years. Maintainers who stay tend to accumulate MORE packages over time (5.1 to 6.0 packages/maintainer for the surviving 1998 cohort), i.e. survivors intensify rather than plateau. Caveat: this is Debian-specific (a single, unusually formalized, unpaid-volunteer packaging community), not necessarily generalizable to other OSS projects or to platform creator economies; the paper explicitly flags this as a single-project case study. `review`

Rejection (revert/deletion in first session) for good-faith newcomers jumped from 15.7% to 48.6% between 2005 and 2006. Newcomer quality composition itself stayed roughly flat post-2006 (good-faith ~38.4% and golden ~36.3% of newcomers on average) -- ruling out 'declining newcomer quality' as the cause and pointing instead to rising rejection as the driver of falling survival.
