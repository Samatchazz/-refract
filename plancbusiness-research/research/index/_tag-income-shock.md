- [[platform-design-earnings-transparency-and]] — Platform Design, Earnings Transparency and — Li, Liu & Sundararajan (NYU Stern, arXiv econ.GN 2602.08955, working paper, Feb 2026 v2). A rigorous quasi-experimental design on an INCOME-LEVEL shock in gig work -- exactly the design type Gap 3 asked for -- but the outcome variable is hours/utilization, not exit. In February 2024 Lyft introduced a policy guaranteeing drivers at least 70% of net rider payments weekly ('earning guarantee') plus per-trip earnings-breakdown transparency, rolled out first to 'major markets' (staggered geographically, then nationwide 3 months later), creating a natural experiment. Using proprietary Lyft trip-level data (~47 million rides across one major market, LAX, and two adjacent non-major markets, OCX/SBD, for a 25-week window around the Feb 2024 launch plus the same calendar window in 2022-23) the authors run dynamic staggered difference-in-differences models with a geographic border-discontinuity strategy. Findings: drivers increased weekly working hours (log-hours treatment effect +0.268, group HH i.e. already earning >=70% pretreatment, n=7,232 drivers, 180,800 driver-weeks vs +0.349, group LH i.e. earned <70% at least one pretreatment week, n=47,987 drivers, 1,199,675 driver-weeks -- both significant at p<.001), with the effect strongest for drivers who had the lowest pre-policy earnings and highest earnings uncertainty, and for full-time drivers (+0.378 log-hours, n=32,026) versus part-time (+0.310, n=26,711) and luxury-vehicle drivers (+0.129, n=6,796). Utilization, completed trips and per-hour/per-trip earnings all rose; effects concentrated among drivers with below-median earnings variance pretreatment. CRITICAL LIMITATION FOR GAP 3: despite background citations noting that platform-design changes elsewhere caused documented driver exit/churn (TaskRabbit's 2014 pricing-mechanism change drove provider backlash and exit; a peer-to-peer car-rental platform's pricing shift 'led to increased levels of churn,' citing Filippas et al. 2023), this paper's own dependent variables are hours worked, utilization, take rate, and predictability -- 'Number of drivers' in its tables denotes treatment-group sample sizes, not an entry/exit or retention outcome. The paper does not measure whether the earnings-guarantee shock changed driver retention or exit rates. It is the closest methodologically rigorous income-shock natural experiment found in the adjacent gig-economy literature, and it still does not close Gap 3's ask for a causal income-to-exit estimate -- it measures labor-supply intensity, not survival. `review`
- [[why-niconico-in-japan-lost-game-jikkyo-streamers-and-their-audience]] — Why Niconico in Japan lost “Game Jikkyo” streamers and their audience? — Igarashi, Tanaka & Koyama (DiGRA JAPAN 12th Annual Conference, Feb 2022, peer-reviewed proceedings, Japan). Uses Niconico's own public platform telemetry (Niconico Chart database, quarterly video-post-count and view-count, 2007 Q2-2021 Q2) plus an original online survey of Japanese gaming-video viewers (Dynata-fielded, screening n=3,328, main survey n=1,356, stratified by sex x age-decade 10s/20s/30s, fielded Aug 24-Sep 7 2021) to explain Niconico's decline. Key numbers: Niconico paid-membership peaked at 2.56 million in 2016, then fell by over 1 million by 2021. Video-posting-count began declining from ~2011 but total view-count did not begin declining until 2016 -- a documented multi-year lag the authors read as creator outflow (posting decline) preceding and causing viewer outflow (view-count decline), not the reverse. The authors attribute Niconico's 2011-launched creator-payment program ('Creator Incentive Program') failing to produce YouTube-Partner-Program-scale earners (no HIKAKIN-scale creator emerged) as the proximate driver, alongside under-investment in infrastructure (30-minute live-stream caps, viewer-count limits, forced low-quality 'Economy Mode' for non-paying viewers). Table 2 gives exact reason percentages from the n=1,356 survey for why respondents stopped watching Niconico regularly: 'creators/streamers moved to other services' is the top or joint-top reason in every one of 6 demographic cells (35% men 10s, 35% men 20s, 33% men 30s, 42% women 10s, 29% women 20s, 29% women 30s), ahead of genre decline, quality/latency complaints, app usability, and toxic comments. CAVEATS: this is platform-level aggregate evidence (quarterly posting/view-count time series functioning as a population-level creator-outflow proxy), not individual-creator survival/hazard telemetry -- no per-creator cohort or survival curve is constructed. The reason data in Table 2 surveys VIEWERS about why they stopped watching, not creators about why they stopped creating, so it is adjacent to but does not directly answer 'why creators quit.' It is nonetheless the most quantified Japanese-language academic finding located linking platform-level creator exodus to insufficient monetization (income) -- directly relevant to Gap 3 as a qualitative/correlational counterpoint (not a causal hazard model) to the pipeline's 'income does not predict continuation' position, and relevant to Gap 5 as the closest non-English platform-scale telemetry found, though it measures aggregate posting/view counts rather than individual creator survival. `review`
- [[arxiv250615278v1-cscy-18-jun-2025]] — arXiv:2506.15278v1  [cs.CY]  18 Jun 2025 — Binns, Stein, Datta, Van Kleek & Shadbolt (Oxford, ACM FAccT 2025, peer-reviewed). Longitudinal algorithm audit of Uber's pay in the UK using Data Subject Access Request (DSAR) data: 1.5 million trips from 258 volunteer drivers, 2016-2024, obtained via the non-profit Worker Info Exchange (WIE). Studies the Q1 2023 introduction of 'dynamic pricing' in London -- a genuine before/after income-structure shock (Uber's take rate went from a stable 20-25% fixed cut to a variable, trip-by-trip rate as high as 47-50% on some trips) -- as a natural before/after comparison. Findings: real average pay per hour has been roughly stagnant since 2016 and is LOWER in the year following dynamic pricing even under Uber's own restricted definition of working time (excludes waiting/standby time); Uber's median cut increased; a small minority of drivers earn more per hour post-change but the majority earn less; driver-to-driver pay inequality increased; utilization fell (more unpaid standby time waiting for job allocation); and job-pay predictability decreased (similar trips can no longer be used to predict earnings). CRITICAL LIMITATION FOR GAP 3: this is a genuine, well-identified income-shock event study (before/after a documented platform payout-structure change, with driver-level panel data) but its outcome variables are pay-per-hour, take rate, utilization and predictability -- it contains no attrition, retention, or exit analysis; there is no evidence in this paper on whether drivers who experienced the pay cut left the platform. It is therefore a genuine near-miss for Gap 3's ask: methodologically the type of natural experiment requested (income-level shock, not just tier/status change), but measuring labor-supply/earnings outcomes rather than exit, so it neither confirms nor overturns the pipeline's 'income does not predict continuation' position -- it simply documents that no exit outcome was measured even in the best-available natural experiment on an income shock. `review`
