- [[chinas-livestreaming-industry-platforms-politics-and-precarity]] — CHINA'S LIVESTREAMING INDUSTRY: PLATFORMS, POLITICS AND PRECARITY — Cunningham, Stuart; Craig, David; Lv, Junyi (2019), 'China's livestreaming industry: platforms, politics, and precarity', International Journal of Cultural Studies 22(6). Accepted manuscript obtained from the QUT ePrints institutional repository after the SAGE version was Cloudflare-blocked. GEOGRAPHIC FLAG: CHINA, not Southeast Asia -- a non-Western comparison case only.\n\nTHE KEY QUANTITATIVE FINDING, and the single hardest creator income-distribution figure in this batch: citing a Tencent Research Institute report (via Meng 2017b), the paper reports that among Chinese livestreamers 'only 5 per cent made more than 10,000 yuan ($1500 USD) a month, while ... more than 70 per cent said they earned less than 100 yuan ($15 USD) a month.' The authors set this against a reported population of 3.5 million commercial livestreamers, noting that 5% still translates to hundreds of thousands of successful streamers. PROVENANCE WARNING: this is a second-hand citation of an industry report via a press account -- the underlying Tencent Research Institute methodology, sample size and sampling frame are not stated in this paper, and it should be cited as an industry estimate, not a measured distribution. It is nonetheless the sharpest available statement of the creator income pyramid: a 100-fold gap between the top ventile and the bottom seven deciles, with the modal livestreamer earning roughly USD 15 per month, far below the ADB developing-Asia living wage of USD 254-415 per month.\n\nThe paper's own empirical base is qualitative: mixed methods comprising desk research, content and discourse analysis of Chinese-language media and industry reports, and original interview fieldwork conducted in 2017 in Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou with 22 interviewees -- nearly half livestreamers working full or part time, the remainder platform and MCN executives, investors and media scholars. Sampling was snowball via platform executives and university networks, so the streamer sample is recruited through industry gatekeepers and is likely to over-represent survivors.\n\nOTHER FIGURES WORTH EXTRACTING: market-size estimates for Chinese livestreaming diverge wildly across sources -- USD 5bn (2016, Moshinsky), USD 4.4bn (2017, Lavin), USD 10bn (2018) with USD 19bn predicted for 2022 (iResearch) -- which the authors present explicitly as evidence that the industry's own numbers are unreliable. Composition: per Yang (2018), only 20% of livestreaming is gaming, the rest IRL streaming, of which 70% are women serving audiences that are 75% men. Top game livestreamers can reach earnings over USD 10 million per year (QQ News 2017) and can migrate between platforms taking their audiences with them. WESTERN COMPARISON DATA cited in the same paper: more than 3 million YouTube creators globally received some remuneration; in mid-2018 the top 5,000 YouTubers had at least 1 million subscribers, over 200 had 10 million, and over 100 had a billion lifetime views; the Re-Create Coalition (Shapiro and Aneja 2018) estimated creators earned USD 5.9bn across nine digital and social platforms in the United States in 2016 alone.\n\nThe paper's argument is that Chinese livestreamers face a distinctive precarity in which greater commercial opportunity (multiple competing platforms, integrated e-commerce, no single-platform dependency as in the West) is traded against state regulatory risk, and that gendered performance targeting an estimated 70-million-surplus male population is a structural feature of the market rather than an incidental one. Caveat: 2017 fieldwork, published 2019 -- predates the TikTok/Douyin e-commerce era. `review`
- [[concurrent-streaming-viewer-transfers-and]] — Concurrent Streaming, Viewer Transfers, and — Maxwell Shepherd (Johns Hopkins), 'Concurrent Streaming, Viewer Transfers, and Audience Loyalty in a Creator Ecosystem: A Minute-Level Longitudinal Study', arXiv:2603.23773v1 [cs.SI], 24 March 2026. QUALITY VERDICT: peripheral to this research question. It contains NO income figures and NO attrition or survival rates. Its relevance is that it quantifies audience non-exclusivity and within-ecosystem attention competition - a mechanism behind why creator earnings do not scale with effort.\n\nSAMPLE: 2,935,985 minute-by-minute viewer-count observations across 7,762 livestreams from 18 YouTube channels, all belonging to Hololive English (HoloEN), November 2022 to March 2026 (3.3 years). Median stream duration 181.2 minutes; mean 7,587 viewers per stream; max single-stream 243,897 viewers. These are elite VTubers inside the dominant agency, not a representative creator sample - the author states plainly that focusing on a single organisation 'limits generalizability'.\n\nFINDINGS. (1) Concurrency dilution: mean per-stream audience falls from 14,377 viewers when a creator streams alone to 6,057 when eight ecosystem colleagues stream simultaneously - a 58% raw drop. But after residualising on hour-of-day the within-hour competition correlation is only rho = -0.165 (95% CI [-0.186, -0.145], p<0.001, day-level block bootstrap over 1,209 days, 2,000 iterations), so most of the raw drop is scheduling confound, not audience splitting. (2) Viewer transfers ('raids'): 3,243 candidate transfer events detected; among plausible events (efficiency <=100%) mean spike is 2,552 viewers and MEDIAN transfer efficiency is about 49% (IQR 29-60%), i.e. roughly half a departing audience follows to another ecosystem stream and half leaves entirely. Top 10 channel pairs account for 16% of events. ~5% estimated false positives. (3) Audience exclusivity is a creator-level, not organisation-level, property: Competition Resistance ranges from 0.364 (Gura) to 1.000 (Kiara) among channels in the SAME agency; Post-Peak Retention is uniformly high across channels.\n\nACTIVITY DISPARITY (useful as an effort benchmark): among these 18 elite channels, streaming frequency ranges from 3.1 streams/month (Gura, 90 streams) to 22.8 streams/month (Bijou, 720 streams) - a 7x spread in output within one agency - and observed active periods run 632 days (most recent debut cohort) to 1,220 days for the earliest channels. All 18 are still active, so this yields no exit data.\n\nCAVEATS the author states: viewer counts not identities, so overlap is a proxy; the dilution analysis is observational not causal; transfer detection is heuristic and cannot separate deliberate raids from organic migration; loyalty composite weights (0.30/0.25/0.25/0.20) are subjective.\n\nGEOGRAPHY: Hololive ENGLISH branch only - anglophone audience, Japanese parent agency. Not generalisable to Southeast Asian creators, though Hololive does operate a separate Indonesian branch not covered here. `review`
- [[from-live-to-recording-consumer-demand-and-response-to-price]] — From Live to Recording: Consumer Demand and Response to Price — Cong, Ziwei; Liu, Jia; Manchanda, Puneet, 'From Live to Recording: Consumer Demand and Response to Price Across the Livestreaming Lifecycle', arXiv:2107.01629v3 [stat.ML], version dated 2 June 2026 (originally posted July 2021; part of the first author's dissertation). Georgetown / HKUST / Michigan Ross. GEOGRAPHIC FLAG: this is CHINA (Zhihu), NOT Southeast Asia -- a non-Western comparison case, not SEA evidence.\n\nEVIDENCE GRADE: this is platform-internal administrative telemetry, the highest evidence tier in this corpus. Two proprietary Zhihu datasets: complete transaction records for Live events May 2016-June 2017 (buyer ID, purchase time, unit price, event characteristics, platform promotions, ratings) plus the full Zhihu Q&A user table with registration time and historical activity for every creator and buyer. Estimation sample: 2,299 Live events hosted by 1,165 unique creators, livestreamed November 2016-June 2017 (a window with no structural or policy change), excluding the ~10% of events the platform price-promoted. The outcome window is 30 days before and after each livestream, capturing 85% of total sales.\n\nCREATOR-EARNINGS NUMBERS (the reason this source matters for a creator-income question, though the paper's own topic is pricing). Mean ticket price is USD 2.97 (about 21 RMB) against a platform-permitted range of 0 to 499.9 RMB with a suggested menu of 9.9/19.9/29.9/39.9 RMB. Mean sales are 217.54 tickets in the 30 days before the event and 64.77 in the 30 days after, so a mean event grosses roughly USD 840 in ticket revenue before any platform cut -- for a session that typically runs one to two hours plus preparation. The dispersion is extreme and right-skewed: pre-period sales have a standard deviation of 424.72 against a mean of 217.54, and post-period 146.86 against 64.77, i.e. standard deviations roughly twice the means. This is the classic creator-economy income distribution -- mean far above median, a small number of events carrying the total -- observed here in transaction records rather than self-report. Post-livestream (recorded) sales are 23% of total, a monetization channel creators and platforms largely ignore.\n\nPERSISTENCE / ATTRITION SIGNAL: by December 2020 Zhihu Live had accumulated nearly 10,000 Live events from 5,000 unique creators and 6 million paying users over four and a half years -- an average of about two events per creator across the program's entire life. In the authors' own 8-month estimation window, 1,165 creators hosted 2,299 events, again roughly two each. Low repeat-hosting is consistent with most approved Live creators trying the format once or twice and not continuing, though the paper does not analyse creator survival and does not report a churn rate.\n\nMAIN FINDING (pricing, secondary for this query): price elasticity is approximately -1.2 (p<0.01) pre-livestream versus -0.8 (p<0.01) post-livestream, i.e. demand is LESS price-sensitive for the recording than for the live event, driven by consumer self-selection (post-period buyers are infrequent users with less market knowledge) and by quality uncertainty resolving after the event. Policy simulation: period-specific pricing would set the post-period price 21.93% above the pre-period price and 17.18% above the optimal time-invariant price, raising revenue 2.28% on average, with disproportionately larger gains for less-established creators with weaker reputational signals -- a quantified reputation penalty facing new entrants.\n\nCAVEATS: paid knowledge-livestreaming on a Chinese Q&A platform with expertise vetting at entry (real name, educational background, proof of expertise) -- an unusually credentialed creator population, not representative of open-entry platforms like YouTube or TikTok. Transaction data is from 2016-2017 and is nine to ten years old; Zhihu Live's monetization design has no SEA equivalent. Platform revenue share is not disclosed, so gross ticket revenue overstates creator take-home by an unknown factor. `review`

- [[joining-the-affiliate-program]] — Joining the Affiliate Program — PRIMARY platform documentation - Twitch's own current (retrieved July 2026) requirements for the Affiliate Program, the first monetisation gate a new creator must clear before ANY subscription, Bits, or ad revenue is possible. Four requirements, all of which must be met within the SAME rolling 30-day window: reach 25 followers; stream for 4 hours total; stream on 4 different days; and average 3 concurrent viewers on 4 different days. The 3-viewer test is average CONCURRENT viewers, not total views, and days below 3 average viewers simply do not count rather than counting against the creator. Two-factor authentication is mandatory. Invitation follows automatically, typically 24-48 hours and up to 72 hours after eligibility. Creators aged 13-17 may join with parental consent. Note for cohort comparability: these thresholds are materially LOWER than the widely-cited historical Affiliate requirements (50 followers, 500 total broadcast minutes, 7 unique broadcast days, 3 average concurrent viewers over 30 days), so monetisation-onset statistics from different years are not comparable - a creator counted as 'monetised' in 2026 cleared an easier bar than one in 2019. The Affiliate tier unlocks subscriptions, Bits/Cheering, ad revenue, Channel Points and Extensions. Offboarding is possible on request and re-application is permitted. The page states NO minimum viewer or income requirement to REMAIN an Affiliate and NO payout-threshold figure, and gives no data on what share of accounts ever clear the bar or how long it typically takes - an audited absence: Twitch publishes the gate but not the pass rate. `review`
