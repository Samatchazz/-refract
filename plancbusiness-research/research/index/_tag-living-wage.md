- [[adbi-working-paper-series]] — ADBI Working Paper Series — Khatiwada, S. and Flaminiano, J.P. (2019), 'Prospects for Decent Work in Services', ADBI Working Paper 940, Asian Development Bank Institute, Tokyo (April 2019). NOT about content creators -- this is a multilateral-institution labour economics paper on services employment in developing Asia. It earns its place in a creator-economy corpus as the DENOMINATOR source: it supplies the Asian living-wage thresholds and informality baselines against which any Southeast Asian creator income figure must be judged, and it demonstrates that the US/Western living-wage benchmarks used in creator-economy reporting are off by roughly an order of magnitude for SEA.\n\nThe headline calibration figures: living wage rates for developing Asia are estimated at USD 254.28 per month (Option 1, anchored on the World Bank USD 1.90/day absolute poverty line) and USD 414.88 per month (Option 2, anchored on the USD 3.20/day moderate poverty line). Both are derived by taking the poverty line, inflating it 10% for savings and discretionary income, multiplying by an assumed family of four (two adults, two children), and capping work at 48 hours per week. Average household size across the Asian countries sampled runs 3.8 (Thailand, Viet Nam) to 6.7 (Pakistan); average actual hours worked run 37 (Indonesia) to 47.4 (Pakistan) against statutory 40-48. The implication for creator-economy analysis is direct: a creator earning roughly USD 255-415 per month clears the developing-Asia living wage, whereas US-benchmarked creator studies typically use thresholds several times higher, so 'share of creators earning a living wage' is not comparable across the two contexts without restating the threshold.\n\nSectoral living-wage attainment (authors' calculations on national Labour Force Survey microdata; Bangladesh 2010 HIES, India 2012, Indonesia 2014, Nepal 2008, Pakistan 2013, Philippines 2013, Sri Lanka 2014, Thailand 2010, Viet Nam 2013): only 28% of agricultural wage workers earn at least Option 1 and just 4% earn Option 2; more than half of manufacturing, construction, mining and utilities workers clear Option 1; government services 78% (Option 1) and 63% (Option 2); business services 73% and 53%. Trade services and 'other services' (largely personal services) have markedly lower shares. Services account for almost 40% of total employment.\n\nThe informality baseline matters for interpreting creator work as employment: per ILO statistics (2018 latest available), informal employment as a share of non-agricultural employment reaches 87% in Bangladesh, 85% in India and Cambodia, over 70% in Pakistan, Indonesia and the Philippines, over 60% in Viet Nam and Sri Lanka, with only Thailand (38%) and the PRC (33%) below half. Informal-sector workers in Bangladesh earn on average about one-fifth of formal-sector counterparts; in India and Thailand less than half; and in none of the eight economies examined did informal average wages reach two-thirds of formal wages. Content creation in SEA is therefore informal self-employment inside labour markets where informality is already the majority condition -- meaning the counterfactual for a SEA creator is not a salaried Western job but, in most of the region, another informal job paying well under formal-sector wages.\n\nCaveats: 2019 publication using microdata from 2008-2015, predating the creator economy's growth phase and the TikTok era entirely; living wage figures are anchored to the 2011-PPP USD 1.90/3.20 poverty lines that the World Bank has since revised upward; the paper is a working paper subject to revision and ADBI explicitly disclaims accuracy guarantees. `review`
