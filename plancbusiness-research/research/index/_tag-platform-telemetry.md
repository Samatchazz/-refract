- [[from-live-to-recording-consumer-demand-and-response-to-price]] — From Live to Recording: Consumer Demand and Response to Price — Cong, Ziwei; Liu, Jia; Manchanda, Puneet, 'From Live to Recording: Consumer Demand and Response to Price Across the Livestreaming Lifecycle', arXiv:2107.01629v3 [stat.ML], version dated 2 June 2026 (originally posted July 2021; part of the first author's dissertation). Georgetown / HKUST / Michigan Ross. GEOGRAPHIC FLAG: this is CHINA (Zhihu), NOT Southeast Asia -- a non-Western comparison case, not SEA evidence.\n\nEVIDENCE GRADE: this is platform-internal administrative telemetry, the highest evidence tier in this corpus. Two proprietary Zhihu datasets: complete transaction records for Live events May 2016-June 2017 (buyer ID, purchase time, unit price, event characteristics, platform promotions, ratings) plus the full Zhihu Q&A user table with registration time and historical activity for every creator and buyer. Estimation sample: 2,299 Live events hosted by 1,165 unique creators, livestreamed November 2016-June 2017 (a window with no structural or policy change), excluding the ~10% of events the platform price-promoted. The outcome window is 30 days before and after each livestream, capturing 85% of total sales.\n\nCREATOR-EARNINGS NUMBERS (the reason this source matters for a creator-income question, though the paper's own topic is pricing). Mean ticket price is USD 2.97 (about 21 RMB) against a platform-permitted range of 0 to 499.9 RMB with a suggested menu of 9.9/19.9/29.9/39.9 RMB. Mean sales are 217.54 tickets in the 30 days before the event and 64.77 in the 30 days after, so a mean event grosses roughly USD 840 in ticket revenue before any platform cut -- for a session that typically runs one to two hours plus preparation. The dispersion is extreme and right-skewed: pre-period sales have a standard deviation of 424.72 against a mean of 217.54, and post-period 146.86 against 64.77, i.e. standard deviations roughly twice the means. This is the classic creator-economy income distribution -- mean far above median, a small number of events carrying the total -- observed here in transaction records rather than self-report. Post-livestream (recorded) sales are 23% of total, a monetization channel creators and platforms largely ignore.\n\nPERSISTENCE / ATTRITION SIGNAL: by December 2020 Zhihu Live had accumulated nearly 10,000 Live events from 5,000 unique creators and 6 million paying users over four and a half years -- an average of about two events per creator across the program's entire life. In the authors' own 8-month estimation window, 1,165 creators hosted 2,299 events, again roughly two each. Low repeat-hosting is consistent with most approved Live creators trying the format once or twice and not continuing, though the paper does not analyse creator survival and does not report a churn rate.\n\nMAIN FINDING (pricing, secondary for this query): price elasticity is approximately -1.2 (p<0.01) pre-livestream versus -0.8 (p<0.01) post-livestream, i.e. demand is LESS price-sensitive for the recording than for the live event, driven by consumer self-selection (post-period buyers are infrequent users with less market knowledge) and by quality uncertainty resolving after the event. Policy simulation: period-specific pricing would set the post-period price 21.93% above the pre-period price and 17.18% above the optimal time-invariant price, raising revenue 2.28% on average, with disproportionately larger gains for less-established creators with weaker reputational signals -- a quantified reputation penalty facing new entrants.\n\nCAVEATS: paid knowledge-livestreaming on a Chinese Q&A platform with expertise vetting at entry (real name, educational background, proof of expertise) -- an unusually credentialed creator population, not representative of open-entry platforms like YouTube or TikTok. Transaction data is from 2016-2017 and is nine to ten years old; Zhihu Live's monetization design has no SEA equivalent. Platform revenue share is not disclosed, so gross ticket revenue overstates creator take-home by an unknown factor. `review`
- [[how-much-does-youtube-pay-for-1000-subscribers-in-2026]] — How Much Does YouTube Pay for 1,000 Subscribers in 2026? — vidIQ vendor-telemetry analysis (May 2026, updated 19 May 2026) of 1,027,773 YouTube channels sitting between 1,000 and 1,100 subscribers -- the single most useful measurement AT the monetization threshold rather than above it. Method is stated explicitly in a source box: 'vidIQ analyzed 1,027,773 YouTube channels at 1,000 to 1,100 subscribers in May 2026, segmenting them into three view-volume tiers and applying industry-standard niche CPMs.' CRITICAL METHOD SPLIT: the VIEW distribution is measured telemetry; the DOLLAR figures are modelled from assumed niche CPMs, not observed payouts. Findings: Tier 1, under 5,000 monthly views = 911,166 channels (89%), estimated ad revenue under 20 USD/month and 'for most channels at this view volume the realistic take is under 5 USD'; Tier 2, 5,000-20,000 monthly views = 73,492 channels (7.2%), median around 9,000 monthly views, 5-74 USD/month by niche; Tier 3, 20,000+ monthly views = 43,115 channels (4.2%), 27-503 USD/month by niche. So roughly 89% of channels that have cleared the subscriber half of the YPP gate still earn effectively nothing, and only ~4% reach the 200-700 USD/month figures that advice articles quote. Niche spread within Tier 3: finance/business 251-503 USD at ~60,958 views vs gaming/entertainment 27-106 USD at ~48,264 views. Revenue mechanics disclosed: effective RPM is roughly 25-30% of CPM because only ~50% of views serve ads and YouTube keeps 45% of watch-page ad revenue; average RPM 1.50-3.00 USD for most niches, 4-7 USD finance/tech, under 2 USD gaming. Geographic CPM tiering is directly relevant to the SEA generalization question: Tier 1 markets (US/UK/CA/AU/NO/DK/SE) 8-30+ USD CPM vs Tier 4 (India, Indonesia, Vietnam, Pakistan, Philippines, most of South and Southeast Asia) 0.30-2.50 USD CPM -- roughly a 60-fold spread top to bottom, with an India-specific worked example putting 1k-sub Indian channels under 5 USD/month. CAVEATS: vidIQ sells YouTube growth software, so the sampling frame is presumably its own tracked-channel database rather than a random sample of YouTube; no confidence intervals, no sampling description, no independent replication; the dollar tiers are CPM-model outputs. The article also asserts monetization can be reached 'in as fast as 28 days' with zero supporting method -- treat that as marketing copy, not measurement. `review`

- [[mapping-youtube-a-quantitative-exploration-of-a-platformed-media-system]] — Mapping YouTube: A quantitative exploration of a platformed media system — Rieder, Coromina & Matamoros-Fernandez, 'Mapping YouTube: A quantitative exploration of a platformed media system', First Monday 25(8), 2020, DOI 10.5210/fm.v25i8.10667. DISCOVERED IN PHASE 2 - this is the underlying crawl that supplies the denominator and the view distribution used in the per-tier income table in Rieder et al. 2023. It is the population census against which every creator-income statistic in this corpus should be normalised. Also, unexpectedly, it is one of the only sources in the batch with per-country data covering Indonesia and Thailand.\n\nSAMPLE: a snowball crawl following featured-channel and subscription links, seeded and bounded by a 1,000-subscriber cutoff. It discovered 4,415,180 channels with more than 1,000 subscribers and 36,336,861 channels in total - above then-existing estimates of YouTube's channel population. The authors state a HIERARCHY OF CONFIDENCE: the 100k+ and 10k-100k datasets are near-complete; the 1k-10k dataset is missing an estimated 10-15%; and the roughly 32 million channels below 1,000 subscribers should be treated 'with even more caution'. Crawled 2019-2020.\n\nYOUTUBE'S OWN TIER SYSTEM (important context for every monetization statistic): activating a channel gives 'graphite' status; 1,000 SUBSCRIBERS is the 'opal' threshold and the gate to advertising monetization; 10,000 subscribers ('bronze') gives access to YouTube Space, pop-up events and training; 100,000 ('silver and up') gets a dedicated partner manager and awards, and - per Kumar 2019 - prioritised handling of demonetization appeals. Going further up, the 15,496 channels above 1 million subscribers (0.04%) take 37% of subscribers and 37.4% of views on 2.3% of videos - which the authors note is 'a much heavier skew than the 80/20 Pareto principle'. Subscriber and view counts correlate at 0.74. Below the 1,000-subscriber monetization threshold the average subscriber count falls to 122. More than half of all videos published by the 4.4 million monetizable channels fail to reach 550 views.\n\nEFFORT VS TIME - directly relevant to 'time from starting to first meaningful income': elite channels have published on average 940 videos versus 294 for the next tier down, but 'this is not simply an effect of having been around for a longer time: if we compare the average number of days active since the starting date, the numbers are surprisingly constant between tiers.' In other words, tenure does not distinguish successful from unsuccessful channels; output volume does. This is the clearest quantitative statement in the corpus that waiting does not produce success.\n\nGEOGRAPHY - unusually useful here. US channels are more than 15% of the elite tier, followed by India, Brazil, Russia, INDONESIA, Great Britain, Mexico, Spain and THAILAND. The US and Great Britain 'punch above their weight', achieving more subscribers and views with fewer videos, which the authors attribute to the English-language advantage. India, South Korea and Egypt publish disproportionate numbers of videos; only Korea converts that into views (K-pop). 'Countries like Brazil and Indonesia stand out for achieving far fewer views than their overall share of channels would suggest.' Thailand and Turkey stand out with HIGH average views per channel. Western channels are on average older; Asian channels (India, South Korea, Pakistan, Vietnam) are younger. Caution: in the full 4.4M monetizable network, 53.6% of channels have NO country set at all and the US accounts for 8.4%, so country-level creator analysis covers under half the platform.\n\nOTHER: Gaming covers 10.7% of all channels; Entertainment and Lifestyle dominate all metrics, Music close behind. Over 25% of published videos are under two minutes. Metadata quality decays going down the tiers, so lower-tier channels are systematically less visible to any metadata-based research method - a bias that compounds survivorship bias in every YouTube study.\n\nLONG SOURCE, 13,334 words - candidate for source-analyst delegation. `review`

- [[patreon-subscriber-and-creator-statistics-for-2026]] — Patreon: Subscriber and Creator Statistics for 2026 — Backlinko (Semrush-owned SEO property) Patreon statistics roundup, updated 2026. THE STRONGEST SOURCE IN THIS BATCH and the only one with real provenance: its load-bearing tables cite GRAPHTREON, a census-style scrape of public Patreon pages, plus Patreon company disclosures — measured counts, not a self-selected survey. Two findings here are genuinely new evidence for the query. (1) NET ATTRITION IS NOW VISIBLE: Patreon's paid-creator count peaked at 295,083 in October 2025 and FELL to 286,287 by February 2026 (-8,796, ~3% in four months; the source says ~5% down since June 2025) — the first contraction after nine years of monotonic growth (25,646 in Feb 2017 → 172,176 Feb 2022 → 242,878 Feb 2024 → 279,566 Nov 2024). Caveat: the metric counts creators with at least one PAYING member, so it cannot separate quitting from losing one's last patron. (2) EARNINGS PER CREATOR ARE FALLING: visible monthly payouts have been flat at ~$24M since January 2022 ($24.16M Jan 2022 → $23.97M Jan 2026, -0.72% YoY) while the creator base grew ~66%, so visible payout per paid creator fell from ~$140/mo to ~$84/mo, roughly -40% in four years (derived by analyst). CRITICAL CAVEAT on that: Graphtreon's payout figure EXCLUDES creators with hidden earnings, while the creator count includes everyone — so those per-creator levels are lower bounds, not true means, and the trend holds only if the hidden share is stable. Comparing Graphtreon's ~$24M/month against Patreon's disclosed $2B/year (~$167M/month) implies visible earnings capture only ~14% of payouts. Graphtreon is therefore reliable for creator COUNTS but not earnings LEVELS. Using Patreon's own $2B (2025) over 286,287 creators gives a mean of ~$6,990/yr (~$582/mo) gross of Patreon's 5-12% cut — a MEAN on a power-law distribution, with no median published anywhere. Concentration is measured: only 31 creators of 286,287 (~0.011%, 1 in 9,200) have over 20,000 paying members; top disclosed monthly earnings are $273,836 (the yard), $200,697 (TrueAnon), $198,278 (Chapo Trap House). Demand side has plateaued too: 10M monthly paying members flat 2024-2025 against 60M+ free memberships (~14% paid conversion), and Patreon's valuation fell ~70% from $4B (April 2021) to a $1.5B internal mark. Podcasting, the 2nd-biggest category, had 40,000+ creators earning $472M in 2024 from 6.7M memberships (~$11,800 mean per podcaster, ~$70 per membership/yr). COUNTRY SCOPE: no geographic breakdown of creators or members is published anywhere; Patreon is US-headquartered and its top creators are overwhelmingly US/English-language, so none of this generalizes to Southeast Asia. Minor hygiene error: the revenue table lists 2022 twice and dates the $228M figure to 2022 while the body says 2024. `review`
- [[quantifying-the-creator-economy-a-large-scale-analysis-of-patreon]] — Quantifying the Creator Economy: A Large-Scale Analysis of Patreon — El Sanyoura & Anderson (University of Toronto), 'Quantifying the Creator Economy: A Large-Scale Analysis of Patreon', ICWSM 2022, DOI 10.1609/icwsm.v16i1.19338. NOTE ON PROVENANCE: the AAAI OJS direct-download link (the URL in the assigned batch) triggers a browser download handler that the fetcher cannot process; the PDF was retrieved with curl and ingested via a local loopback server. The canonical URL and DOI have been restored in this note's frontmatter. Content is the authentic published paper.\n\nSAMPLE: the COMPLETE set of Patreon pledge transactions from the platform's inception in June 2013 to December 2020 - hundreds of millions of patron-creator transactions totalling over USD 2 billion paid to creators. Each transaction has an anonymised patron ID, creator ID, pledge amount and date; creator metadata includes display name, URL, summary, modality and topic. This is administrative platform data, not survey data, and it is the largest creator-payments dataset in the published literature. At the time of writing Patreon paid over USD 100 million to creators per month.\n\nEARNINGS-DISTRIBUTION FINDINGS. Within-topic creator inequality rose sharply: the Gini coefficient across creators inside a topic climbed from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.60 in December 2020. Across topics, by contrast, inequality FELL slightly, from 0.80 (mid-2015) to 0.72 (2020) - so the platform diversified into more subject areas while concentrating earnings within each one. Top-decile creators receive nearly USD 15 per pledge versus USD 2 for bottom-decile creators, so high earners win on both audience size AND price per patron; mean pledge amounts differ by over USD 12 across content modalities at the top of the distribution. Per-creator pledge Gini (inequality among a single creator's own patrons) is at most 0.45 on average - moderate, and higher for higher-earning creators. Low-earning creators receive virtually all their income from small (<=USD 5) pledges.\n\nAUDIENCE FINDINGS. The audiences of the LARGEST creators pledge to another creator only 40% of the time, versus 90% for the smallest creators - a >50 percentage-point gap. Large creators generate 'one-and-done' patrons who join the platform solely for them, meaning top creators do not feed the ecosystem. Patrons who support a narrow set of creators are more loyal per-creator but churn off the platform more often; patrons pledging to more creators retain pledges better (the difference in churn between highest and lowest activity levels is only 1-2 percentage points, but the loyalty gap is around 10 percentage points). At any one time, all creator size tiers get the majority of pledges from maintained (returning) patrons, and all tiers show positive audience growth - smaller creators actually show HIGHER relative growth, which the authors read as evidence newcomers can still gain prominence.\n\nMETHODOLOGICAL NOTE that matters for this research question: Patreon 'does not offer algorithmic or human-curated recommendations, and there is no notion of a patron-patron social network', so patrons arrive only via search or off-platform links. This makes Patreon a rare algorithm-free control case against which YouTube/Instagram/TikTok recommendation effects can be compared.\n\nWHAT IT DOES NOT CONTAIN: no creator attrition or exit rates, no median creator earnings figure, no time-to-first-income measure, no reasons-for-quitting data, and no country breakdown. Patreon's creator base is heavily US/Western, so all figures should be flagged as Western and not generalised to Southeast Asia. `review`
- [[tube-census]] — TUBE-CENSUS: — Eggleston, Handler & Pacheco (University of Colorado Boulder), arXiv:2605.06999v1 [cs.SI], 7 May 2026. A DATA-INFRASTRUCTURE paper, not an attrition or income study - but it is the best available documentation of WHY creator-attrition statistics do not exist, and it supplies population denominators. The authors mined every Internet Archive Wayback Machine capture of YouTube channel URLs through end-2023 across four URL formats, yielding 1.21 billion channel snapshots and at least 106 million unique channels (username format 372M snapshots / 34.8M channels; channel-ID format 683M / 106M; custom /c/ 62.4M / 5.9M; handle /@ 94.5M / 25.4M), with subscriber counts dating to 2006.\n\nThe measurement-gap finding is directly load-bearing for this research question: 'the official YouTube API does not provide access to the full set of creators or creator metadata on the platform' and exposes only CURRENT, never historical, subscriber counts. The main commercial alternative, Social Blade, 'lists only the top 5,000 channels at a given time, and provides historical metadata spanning at most the past three years'. This means no public dataset can currently answer 'what fraction of YouTube channels stop posting, and how soon' - the paper exists precisely because that class of question was previously unanswerable.\n\nCOVERAGE VALIDATION: stratified sample of 34,000 channels (2,000 each from 17 capture-frequency strata), video counts observed October 2025, gives a weighted mean of 46.3 videos per channel (SE 2.3), implying a 95% CI of 4.39-5.34 billion videos covered, i.e. approximately 30-36% of all YouTube videos. Spearman rank correlation between snapshot count and subscriber count is rho = 0.354 (username, n=684,763), 0.432 (ID, n=577,624), 0.221 (custom, n=284,306), 0.400 (handle, n=90,160) - so the census over-represents large channels, an explicit survivorship/prominence bias. Cohort 1 (2006-2013) covers 2,997,954 captures for 1,047,839 channels and reproduces Social Blade's December 2010/2011/2012 top-500 lists at ~96% overlap.\n\nGROWTH FINDING (Section 7): among the top 75 most-subscribed channels of January 2014, subscriber trajectories follow logistic S-curves - 'grows slowly, then quickly, and then eventually approach an asymptote' - which the authors liken to ecological carrying capacity: a channel captures attention within a niche until it hits a fundamental limit. Two documented deviations: spin-off channels from already-successful creators skip the slow initial phase entirely, and celebrity musicians grow in spurts unrelated to platform dynamics. This is the closest thing in the paper to a 'time to traction' finding and it is drawn only from already-successful channels.\n\nAppendix Table 10 gives median subscribers/videos/views by BERTopic genre for 208,893 English-description channels (e.g. gaming n=12,381 median 7,110 subs; local politics n=1,331 median 53 subs, 25 videos, 11,241 views; South Asian film/music n=1,572 median 300,000 subs) - but these medians apply only to channels captured >=20 times by the Internet Archive, so they are upward-biased and are NOT a population median.\n\nGEOGRAPHY: US-authored, global YouTube data, but the topic-model analysis is restricted to English-language descriptions, so the genre medians exclude nearly all Southeast Asian non-English creators. No income, no earnings, no explicit exit or survival rates. `review`
