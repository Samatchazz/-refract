A DATA-INFRASTRUCTURE paper, not an attrition or income study - but it is the best available documentation of WHY creator-attrition statistics do not exist, and it supplies population denominators. The authors mined every Internet Archive Wayback Machine capture of YouTube channel URLs through end-2023 across four URL formats, yielding 1.21 billion channel snapshots and at least 106 million unique channels (username format 372M snapshots / 34.8M channels; channel-ID format 683M / 106M; custom /c/ 62.4M / 5.9M; handle /@ 94.5M / 25.4M), with subscriber counts dating to 2006.\n\nThe measurement-gap finding is directly load-bearing for this research question: 'the official YouTube API does not provide access to the full set of creators or creator metadata on the platform' and exposes only CURRENT, never historical, subscriber counts. The main commercial alternative, Social Blade, 'lists only the top 5,000 channels at a given time, and provides historical metadata spanning at most the past three years'. This means no public dataset can currently answer 'what fraction of YouTube channels stop posting, and how soon' - the paper exists precisely because that class of question was previously unanswerable.\n\nCOVERAGE VALIDATION: stratified sample of 34,000 channels (2,000 each from 17 capture-frequency strata), video counts observed October 2025, gives a weighted mean of 46.3 videos per channel (SE 2.3), implying a 95% CI of 4.39-5.34 billion videos covered, i.e. approximately 30-36% of all YouTube videos. Spearman rank correlation between snapshot count and subscriber count is rho = 0.354 (username, n=684,763), 0.432 (ID, n=577,624), 0.221 (custom, n=284,306), 0.400 (handle, n=90,160) - so the census over-represents large channels, an explicit survivorship/prominence bias. Cohort 1 (2006-2013) covers 2,997,954 captures for 1,047,839 channels and reproduces Social Blade's December 2010/2011/2012 top-500 lists at ~96% overlap.\n\nGROWTH FINDING (Section 7): among the top 75 most-subscribed channels of January 2014, subscriber trajectories follow logistic S-curves - 'grows slowly, then quickly, and then eventually approach an asymptote' - which the authors liken to ecological carrying capacity: a channel captures attention within a niche until it hits a fundamental limit. Two documented deviations: spin-off channels from already-successful creators skip the slow initial phase entirely, and celebrity musicians grow in spurts unrelated to platform dynamics. This is the closest thing in the paper to a 'time to traction' finding and it is drawn only from already-successful channels.\n\nAppendix Table 10 gives median subscribers/videos/views by BERTopic genre for 208,893 English-description channels (e.g. gaming n=12,381 median 7,110 subs; local politics n=1,331 median 53 subs, 25 videos, 11,241 views; South Asian film/music n=1,572 median 300,000 subs) - but these medians apply only to channels captured >=20 times by the Internet Archive, so they are upward-biased and are NOT a population median.\n\nGEOGRAPHY: US-authored, global YouTube data, but the topic-model analysis is restricted to English-language descriptions, so the genre medians exclude nearly all Southeast Asian non-English creators.
