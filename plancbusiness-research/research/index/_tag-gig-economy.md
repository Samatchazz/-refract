- [[an-analysis-of-the-labor-market-for-ubers-driver-partners-in-the]] — An Analysis of the Labor Market for Uber’s Driver-Partners in the — Hall (Uber employee) & Krueger (Princeton, consulted for Uber Dec 2014-Jan 2015), NBER Working Paper w22843 / published version ILR Review 2018. Descriptive (non-causal) analysis of Uber's US driver-partner labor market using Uber's own administrative trip data plus a Benenson Strategy Group survey. Provides the closest available gig-economy tenure/attrition analogue to a creator-survival curve: among 11,267 US UberBLACK/uberX driver-partners who made their first trip between January and June 2013 and had made at least 4 trips, 11% became inactive (no trip recorded) within one month of starting; 70% were still active after 6 months; more than 50% remained active a full year after starting. Inactivity is operationalized as not recording any trip for 6+ months. Earnings-per-hour show a quadratic relationship with tenure, peaking around 14 months. The paper explicitly selects October 2014 -- 'a month after Uber's summer fare cuts' -- as its earnings-table snapshot to represent 'normal market conditions,' but does NOT analyze the fare cut itself as a treatment or measure its effect on driver attrition; the fare-cut reference is incidental context for sample-period selection, not a natural experiment. CAVEATS: (1) this is a driver population, not a content-creator population, and ride-hailing exit mechanics (opportunity cost of alternate driving gigs, vehicle costs, licensing) differ from creator exit mechanics -- flagged per Gap 3 instructions as a population difference; (2) descriptive only, not causal -- no identification strategy links pay level to continuation; (3) authorship conflict of interest is substantial: Hall was an Uber employee/shareholder while writing it and Krueger was a paid Uber consultant during the initial draft, which a companion 2019 critique ('Too Good to Be True? A Comment on Hall and Krueger's Analysis,' Berg & Johnston, ILR Review) argues leads to selective framing of driver satisfaction and pay figures. Useful only as an attrition-shape comparator (11%/1-month, 70%/6-month, >50%/1-year, n=11,267) alongside the corpus's existing Wikipedia (35.7%->11.3% newcomer survival 2004-2010) and Debian (55.8% maintainer retention at 6.5 years) analogues -- not as evidence bearing on Gap 3's causal income-shock question, which this paper does not test. `review`
- [[dependence-and-precarity-in-the-platform-economy-theory-and-society-springer-nat]] — Dependence and precarity in the platform economy | Theory and Society | Springer Nature Link — ABSTRACT + NOTES + REFERENCES ONLY — Springer paywall; body not retrieved. Schor, Attwood-Charles, Cansoy, Ladegaard & Wengronowitz (2020), Theory and Society 49:833-861, published 7 Aug 2020. ADJACENT, NOT CREATOR-SPECIFIC: 112 in-depth interviews with workers on seven US platforms — Airbnb, TaskRabbit, Turo, Uber, Lyft, Postmates, Favor — recruited mainly in the Boston area with $30-40 interview payments and a >=5-transaction screen. NO content creators in the sample, so it carries no direct evidence on creator attrition, creator income distribution, or time-to-first-income. Its relevance is the analytic finding that the dominant explanatory variable for platform-worker outcomes is DEPENDENCE: workers who need platform income for basic expenses report lower satisfaction, less autonomy AND lower earnings than supplemental earners, which the authors read as platforms free-riding on conventional employers. That dependence/supplemental split is the framework a creator-income analysis would need to import, and it predicts that survey samples dominated by hobbyist supplemental earners will look systematically rosier than the full-time-dependent tail. Also records a Pew (2016) finding that 42% of US gig-platform workers were aged 18-29 and 39% aged 30-49. Explicitly contrasts with Ravenelle (2019) 'Hustle and Gig' (Airbnb/Uber/TaskRabbit/Kitchensurfing, New York City), whose success-stories/strivers/strugglers typology is more negative but reports no numerical breakdown. US-ONLY; generalizes poorly to Southeast Asia. `review`
- [[how-many-online-workers-are-there-in-the-open-research-europe]] — How many online workers are there in the ... | Open Research Europe — Kassi, O., Lehdonvirta, V. & Stephany, F. (2021), 'How many online workers are there in the world? A data-driven assessment', Open Research Europe 1:53, DOI 10.12688/openreseurope.13639.4 (v1 20 May 2021, latest v4 15 Oct 2021). Peer-reviewed and open-peer-reviewed (Richard Heeks was a reviewer). THIS IS THE PRIMARY SOURCE behind the 163 million / 8.6 percent / 2 percent statistic that the ILO Online Labour Observatory, ILO Working Paper 164 and the World Bank all cite second-hand. ERC-funded (iLABOUR, GIGSTATS).

EXACT HEADLINE NUMBERS: 163,000,000 registered freelancer profiles = 140,000,000 directly observed plus 23,000,000 predicted by an XGBoost model (95% bootstrap band 12-65 million on the predicted component). Of these, approximately 14 million (8.6%) have obtained work through a platform at least once, and 3.3 million (2.0%) have completed at least 10 projects OR earned at least US$1,000 — the exact provenance of the '2 per cent' figure. Adjusting for multi-homing (dividing by 1.83 platforms per worker) reduces these to 7.7 million and 1.8 million; further adjusting upward for multi-working (x1.21, since 21% of surveyed workers reported account-sharing) gives 9.3 million and 2.2 million. Upper bounds under the widest error bands: 205 million registered, 75 million ever-worked, 21 million significantly-worked.

THE METHODOLOGICAL CAVEAT THAT MATTERS MOST: the activity shares rest on a tiny sample. 'We were able to observe the number of workers who had ever worked on the platform for only 7 platforms. For 6 platforms we were able to observe the number of workers who had worked significantly.' The authors decline to compute a confidence interval for this reason and instead publish the observed min-max as an error band: ever-worked share [2.0%, 36.8%], significantly-worked share [0.1%, 10.0%]. This RECONCILES the apparent conflict with the World Bank's 37% mean active share — 36.8% is the highest single platform value Kassi et al. observed, so the World Bank's traffic-proxy estimate sits at the extreme upper edge of the directly-observed range rather than contradicting it outright.

Other model detail: 159 training observations split 128 validation / 31 training; XGBoost with Poisson objective; prediction RMSE 577,000; correlation between predicted and observed in the validation data only 0.27, which the authors call 'more moderate... but still clearly positive'. Multi-homing parameter from ILO (2021) and Wood et al. (2019a) surveys: 48% of respondents worked on a single platform, average 1.83 platforms.

AUTHORS' OWN INTERPRETATION, verbatim: 'the fact that only a small minority have completed any projects, let alone a substantial number of projects suggest that digital platform work is a viable way to make a living only to a small minority of registered workers.' Also: 'the strikingly small share of workers, who have worked significantly, indicates that there is vast oversupply of workers on the platforms we observe.' Compared with Kuek et al. (2015)'s 50 million registered / 10% active, they note growth in registrations but 'much less growth in amount of work completed'.

SCOPE WARNING: online FREELANCING platforms (Upwork, Freelancer, Amazon Mechanical Turk and similar), 2020 data. This is NOT content creation and the population is not creators. Its value to this report is as the best-measured analogue of creator funnel attrition, and as the traceable origin of a figure that circulates in institutional literature without its 7-platform sample size attached. `evergreen`
- [[ilo-flagship-report]] — ILO Flagship Report — ILO (2021) flagship report, 'World Employment and Social Outlook 2021: The role of digital labour platforms in transforming the world of work'. 131,000 words — FLAG FOR SOURCE-ANALYST DELEGATION. This is the primary source behind the platform-earnings figures that the UNDP 2025 Asia-Pacific report and most institutional literature repeat without their sample sizes.

SAMPLES (ILO's own surveys, 2017-2020). Online web-based platforms: global surveys of microtask workers (2017) and freelance/competitive-programming workers (2019-20), about 2,900 respondents from 100 countries; plus country surveys in China (1,107 respondents) and Ukraine (761), giving n = 4,009 for the pooled online-earnings tables. Location-based: about 5,000 respondents in app-based taxi (9 countries) and delivery (11 countries) 2019-20, plus over 2,200 in traditional taxi (9 countries) and delivery (4 countries). CRITICAL CAVEAT stated by the ILO: 'there was no sampling base from which a random sample could be drawn' — these are non-probability convenience samples of ACTIVE workers, so they are survivorship-biased by construction.

EARNINGS (the numbers everyone else cites). Average hourly earnings on online web-based platforms US$3.4 including unpaid time, with half of workers earning less than US$2.1/hour and 66 per cent earning below the average. Paid-time-only averages are much higher — US$11.2 on freelance platforms and US$4.4 on microtask — but fall to US$7.6 and US$3.3 once unpaid search, bidding and profile-building time is counted; 64 per cent (freelance) and 63 per cent (microtask) earn below their platform average. Workers in developing countries earn 60 per cent less on freelance platforms than developed-country workers even after controlling for characteristics and task type. Microtask workers earn 64 per cent less in India and 81 per cent less in the United States than comparable traditional-labour-market work, with the gap wider for women. On the gender gap the ILO is more equivocal than its citers: at the global level, after controlling for education and experience, there is NO difference in hourly earnings on freelance platforms, though significant country-level gaps exist.

DOCUMENTED CONSTRAINTS AND INVOLUNTARY EXIT. About 45 per cent of online platform workers who want more work say the binding constraint is lack of available work; 18 per cent on microtask platforms cite inability to find well-paid tasks and 41 per cent on freelance platforms cite difficulty finding clients. Involuntary attrition is measured directly: 19 per cent of app-based taxi workers and 15 per cent of app-based delivery workers reported account deactivation; among those, deactivation lasted up to seven days for 73 per cent (taxi) and 69 per cent (delivery) but was PERMANENT for 7 and 15 per cent respectively. About 65 per cent considered the deactivation unjustified; 69 and 83 per cent appealed, and 48 and 59 per cent were dissatisfied with the outcome. A footnote makes the survivorship problem explicit: 'Workers whose accounts had been permanently deactivated on one platform were using another platform at the time of the survey' — permanently exited workers are structurally invisible to this instrument.

SCOPE: microtask, freelance, competitive-programming, taxi and delivery platforms. NOT content creation; no creator, influencer or YouTube/TikTok data. Includes an Indonesian Gojek respondent quote but no Southeast Asian earnings tables. `evergreen`
- [[platform-design-earnings-transparency-and]] — Platform Design, Earnings Transparency and — Li, Liu & Sundararajan (NYU Stern, arXiv econ.GN 2602.08955, working paper, Feb 2026 v2). A rigorous quasi-experimental design on an INCOME-LEVEL shock in gig work -- exactly the design type Gap 3 asked for -- but the outcome variable is hours/utilization, not exit. In February 2024 Lyft introduced a policy guaranteeing drivers at least 70% of net rider payments weekly ('earning guarantee') plus per-trip earnings-breakdown transparency, rolled out first to 'major markets' (staggered geographically, then nationwide 3 months later), creating a natural experiment. Using proprietary Lyft trip-level data (~47 million rides across one major market, LAX, and two adjacent non-major markets, OCX/SBD, for a 25-week window around the Feb 2024 launch plus the same calendar window in 2022-23) the authors run dynamic staggered difference-in-differences models with a geographic border-discontinuity strategy. Findings: drivers increased weekly working hours (log-hours treatment effect +0.268, group HH i.e. already earning >=70% pretreatment, n=7,232 drivers, 180,800 driver-weeks vs +0.349, group LH i.e. earned <70% at least one pretreatment week, n=47,987 drivers, 1,199,675 driver-weeks -- both significant at p<.001), with the effect strongest for drivers who had the lowest pre-policy earnings and highest earnings uncertainty, and for full-time drivers (+0.378 log-hours, n=32,026) versus part-time (+0.310, n=26,711) and luxury-vehicle drivers (+0.129, n=6,796). Utilization, completed trips and per-hour/per-trip earnings all rose; effects concentrated among drivers with below-median earnings variance pretreatment. CRITICAL LIMITATION FOR GAP 3: despite background citations noting that platform-design changes elsewhere caused documented driver exit/churn (TaskRabbit's 2014 pricing-mechanism change drove provider backlash and exit; a peer-to-peer car-rental platform's pricing shift 'led to increased levels of churn,' citing Filippas et al. 2023), this paper's own dependent variables are hours worked, utilization, take rate, and predictability -- 'Number of drivers' in its tables denotes treatment-group sample sizes, not an entry/exit or retention outcome. The paper does not measure whether the earnings-guarantee shock changed driver retention or exit rates. It is the closest methodologically rigorous income-shock natural experiment found in the adjacent gig-economy literature, and it still does not close Gap 3's ask for a causal income-to-exit estimate -- it measures labor-supply intensity, not survival. `review`

- [[arxiv250615278v1-cscy-18-jun-2025]] — arXiv:2506.15278v1  [cs.CY]  18 Jun 2025 — Binns, Stein, Datta, Van Kleek & Shadbolt (Oxford, ACM FAccT 2025, peer-reviewed). Longitudinal algorithm audit of Uber's pay in the UK using Data Subject Access Request (DSAR) data: 1.5 million trips from 258 volunteer drivers, 2016-2024, obtained via the non-profit Worker Info Exchange (WIE). Studies the Q1 2023 introduction of 'dynamic pricing' in London -- a genuine before/after income-structure shock (Uber's take rate went from a stable 20-25% fixed cut to a variable, trip-by-trip rate as high as 47-50% on some trips) -- as a natural before/after comparison. Findings: real average pay per hour has been roughly stagnant since 2016 and is LOWER in the year following dynamic pricing even under Uber's own restricted definition of working time (excludes waiting/standby time); Uber's median cut increased; a small minority of drivers earn more per hour post-change but the majority earn less; driver-to-driver pay inequality increased; utilization fell (more unpaid standby time waiting for job allocation); and job-pay predictability decreased (similar trips can no longer be used to predict earnings). CRITICAL LIMITATION FOR GAP 3: this is a genuine, well-identified income-shock event study (before/after a documented platform payout-structure change, with driver-level panel data) but its outcome variables are pay-per-hour, take rate, utilization and predictability -- it contains no attrition, retention, or exit analysis; there is no evidence in this paper on whether drivers who experienced the pay cut left the platform. It is therefore a genuine near-miss for Gap 3's ask: methodologically the type of natural experiment requested (income-level shock, not just tier/status change), but measuring labor-supply/earnings outcomes rather than exit, so it neither confirms nor overturns the pipeline's 'income does not predict continuation' position -- it simply documents that no exit outcome was measured even in the best-available natural experiment on an income shock. `review`
