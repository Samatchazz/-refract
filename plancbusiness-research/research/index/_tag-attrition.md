GEOGRAPHIC FLAG: CHINA, not Southeast Asia -- a non-Western comparison case only.\n\nTHE KEY QUANTITATIVE FINDING, and the single hardest creator income-distribution figure in this batch: citing a Tencent Research Institute report (via Meng 2017b), the paper reports that among Chinese livestreamers 'only 5 per cent made more than 10,000 yuan ($1500 USD) a month, while ... more than 70 per cent said they earned less than 100 yuan ($15 USD) a month.' - [[how-many-online-workers-are-there-in-the-open-research-europe]] — How many online workers are there in the ... | Open Research Europe — Kassi, O., Lehdonvirta, V. & Stephany, F. (2021), 'How many online workers are there in the world? A data-driven assessment', Open Research Europe 1:53, DOI 10.12688/openreseurope.13639.4 (v1 20 May 2021, latest v4 15 Oct 2021). Peer-reviewed and open-peer-reviewed (Richard Heeks was a reviewer). THIS IS THE PRIMARY SOURCE behind the 163 million / 8.6 percent / 2 percent statistic that the ILO Online Labour Observatory, ILO Working Paper 164 and the World Bank all cite second-hand. ERC-funded (iLABOUR, GIGSTATS).

EXACT HEADLINE NUMBERS: 163,000,000 registered freelancer profiles = 140,000,000 directly observed plus 23,000,000 predicted by an XGBoost model (95% bootstrap band 12-65 million on the predicted component). Of these, approximately 14 million (8.6%) have obtained work through a platform at least once, and 3.3 million (2.0%) have completed at least 10 projects OR earned at least US$1,000 — the exact provenance of the '2 per cent' figure. Adjusting for multi-homing (dividing by 1.83 platforms per worker) reduces these to 7.7 million and 1.8 million; further adjusting upward for multi-working (x1.21, since 21% of surveyed workers reported account-sharing) gives 9.3 million and 2.2 million. Upper bounds under the widest error bands: 205 million registered, 75 million ever-worked, 21 million significantly-worked.

THE METHODOLOGICAL CAVEAT THAT MATTERS MOST: the activity shares rest on a tiny sample. 'We were able to observe the number of workers who had ever worked on the platform for only 7 platforms. For 6 platforms we were able to observe the number of workers who had worked significantly.' The authors decline to compute a confidence interval for this reason and instead publish the observed min-max as an error band: ever-worked share [2.0%, 36.8%], significantly-worked share [0.1%, 10.0%]. This RECONCILES the apparent conflict with the World Bank's 37% mean active share — 36.8% is the highest single platform value Kassi et al. observed, so the World Bank's traffic-proxy estimate sits at the extreme upper edge of the directly-observed range rather than contradicting it outright.

Other model detail: 159 training observations split 128 validation / 31 training; XGBoost with Poisson objective; prediction RMSE 577,000; correlation between predicted and observed in the validation data only 0.27, which the authors call 'more moderate... but still clearly positive'. Multi-homing parameter from ILO (2021) and Wood et al. (2019a) surveys: 48% of respondents worked on a single platform, average 1.83 platforms.

AUTHORS' OWN INTERPRETATION, verbatim: 'the fact that only a small minority have completed any projects, let alone a substantial number of projects suggest that digital platform work is a viable way to make a living only to a small minority of registered workers.' Also: 'the strikingly small share of workers, who have worked significantly, indicates that there is vast oversupply of workers on the platforms we observe.' Compared with Kuek et al. (2015)'s 50 million registered / 10% active, they note growth in registrations but 'much less growth in amount of work completed'.

SCOPE WARNING: online FREELANCING platforms (Upwork, Freelancer, Amazon Mechanical Turk and similar), 2020 data. This is NOT content creation and the population is not creators. Its value to this report is as the best-measured analogue of creator funnel attrition, and as the traceable origin of a figure that circulates in institutional literature without its 7-platform sample size attached. `evergreen`
- [[ilo-flagship-report]] — ILO Flagship Report — ILO (2021) flagship report, 'World Employment and Social Outlook 2021: The role of digital labour platforms in transforming the world of work'. 131,000 words — FLAG FOR SOURCE-ANALYST DELEGATION. This is the primary source behind the platform-earnings figures that the UNDP 2025 Asia-Pacific report and most institutional literature repeat without their sample sizes.

SAMPLES (ILO's own surveys, 2017-2020). Online web-based platforms: global surveys of microtask workers (2017) and freelance/competitive-programming workers (2019-20), about 2,900 respondents from 100 countries; plus country surveys in China (1,107 respondents) and Ukraine (761), giving n = 4,009 for the pooled online-earnings tables. Location-based: about 5,000 respondents in app-based taxi (9 countries) and delivery (11 countries) 2019-20, plus over 2,200 in traditional taxi (9 countries) and delivery (4 countries). CRITICAL CAVEAT stated by the ILO: 'there was no sampling base from which a random sample could be drawn' — these are non-probability convenience samples of ACTIVE workers, so they are survivorship-biased by construction.

EARNINGS (the numbers everyone else cites). Average hourly earnings on online web-based platforms US$3.4 including unpaid time, with half of workers earning less than US$2.1/hour and 66 per cent earning below the average. Paid-time-only averages are much higher — US$11.2 on freelance platforms and US$4.4 on microtask — but fall to US$7.6 and US$3.3 once unpaid search, bidding and profile-building time is counted; 64 per cent (freelance) and 63 per cent (microtask) earn below their platform average. Workers in developing countries earn 60 per cent less on freelance platforms than developed-country workers even after controlling for characteristics and task type. Microtask workers earn 64 per cent less in India and 81 per cent less in the United States than comparable traditional-labour-market work, with the gap wider for women. On the gender gap the ILO is more equivocal than its citers: at the global level, after controlling for education and experience, there is NO difference in hourly earnings on freelance platforms, though significant country-level gaps exist.

DOCUMENTED CONSTRAINTS AND INVOLUNTARY EXIT. About 45 per cent of online platform workers who want more work say the binding constraint is lack of available work; 18 per cent on microtask platforms cite inability to find well-paid tasks and 41 per cent on freelance platforms cite difficulty finding clients. Involuntary attrition is measured directly: 19 per cent of app-based taxi workers and 15 per cent of app-based delivery workers reported account deactivation; among those, deactivation lasted up to seven days for 73 per cent (taxi) and 69 per cent (delivery) but was PERMANENT for 7 and 15 per cent respectively. About 65 per cent considered the deactivation unjustified; 69 and 83 per cent appealed, and 48 and 59 per cent were dissatisfied with the outcome. A footnote makes the survivorship problem explicit: 'Workers whose accounts had been permanently deactivated on one platform were using another platform at the time of the survey' — permanently exited workers are structurally invisible to this instrument.

SCOPE: microtask, freelance, competitive-programming, taxi and delivery platforms. NOT content creation; no creator, influencer or YouTube/TikTok data. Includes an Indonesian Gojek respondent quote but no Southeast Asian earnings tables. `evergreen`

THE STRONGEST INCOME-DISTRIBUTION SOURCE IN THIS BATCH — measured platform revenue data, not survey. Built on the October 2021 Twitch data leak covering the revenue Amazon paid the top 10,000 streamers from August 2019 to October 2021; the authors used third-party-compiled public rankings derived from the leak rather than the raw dump, and matched 9,523 of 10,000 streamers. Enriched with Twitch API, TwitchTracker, StreamCharts and Twitchstats data collected January-April 2022. HEADLINE NUMBERS: revenue across the top 10,000 ranged from USD 9,500,000 down to USD 23,520 over the ~26-month window. Gini index for that top-10,000 revenue distribution is 0.57, with the distribution fitting a power law of exponent -2.13. Extrapolating via a parabolic fractal fit to ALL 9.2 million streamers on the platform gives a Gini of approximately 0.93 — near-total concentration, and far above any national income Gini. Subscriber-count Gini is 0.63 for the top 1,000, about 0.6 extended to the top 10,000. CONVERSION IS REGRESSIVE AT SCALE: smaller streamers convert followers to paying subscribers better (0.017 subscribers per follower in the lowest revenue quintile versus 0.008 in the highest), revenue scales sub-linearly with subscribers (power-law exponent about 0.8), and revenue per hour watched falls as revenue rises — so top streamers earn more despite worse per-unit monetization, by sheer volume and by revenue diversification (top 2,000 channels carry an average 3.7 sponsorship/affiliate links versus 2.2 for a random sample outside the top 10,000). SURVIVAL AND CHURN AT THE TOP: using Iniguez et al.'s ranking-dynamics model, the weekly probability of dropping out of the ranking is about 0.2 for the top 100 and about 0.135 for the top 1,000; more than 50% of streamers in the list were present from the very start of the observation window. Twitch's ranking flux (0.13-0.20) resembles the stability of word-usage frequency in natural languages (English 0.13, French/Russian 0.18) rather than social datasets such as GitHub repositories (0.65) or Guardian readers (0.80) — i.e. the creator elite is close to frozen, and lower ranks churn much faster than higher ones. Platform scale context: Twitch average concurrent viewers rose from 1.26 million (2019) to 2.78 million (2021). CAVEATS THE AUTHORS STATE: leaked figures were never confirmed by Amazon (though corroborated by multiple streamers); they cover ONLY Amazon-paid revenue and exclude sponsorships and external income; pseudonym drift forced discarding some records; the 9.2M-streamer Gini is an extrapolation from a fitted curve, not a measurement. GEOGRAPHY: global Twitch, no country breakdown — cannot be disaggregated for Southeast Asia. `evergreen`
