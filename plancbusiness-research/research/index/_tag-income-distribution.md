- [[artists-and-other-cultural-workers]] — Artists and Other Cultural Workers — US National Endowment for the Arts, NASERC indicator series, Indicator A.7 'What Are the Earnings for Artists and Other Cultural Workers?' (report dated May 2025). Source is the US Census American Community Survey for 2015, 2019 and 2023, all figures in constant 2023 dollars, differences reported only at p<.05. This is the FRESHEST nationally-representative US administrative earnings data for creative occupations in this batch and supersedes the 2015-2019 NEA brief on recency. Crucially, unlike BLS OEWS it 'captures earnings regardless of class of worker (private or wage-salary, government, or self-employed)', so it does include independent creators — earnings are wage-salary plus NET self-employment income before deductions.

THE LOAD-BEARING NUMBER FOR CREATOR-INCOME COMPARISON IS THE PART-TIME MEDIAN. Full-time employed artists had median annual earnings of $70,170 in 2023, versus $61,170 for all full-time workers (+15%) and $56,040 for non-artist cultural workers (+25%). But artists employed PART TIME (under 35 hrs/week and/or under 50 weeks/year) had a median of only $20,380 — 3.4 times lower than the full-time artist median, and statistically indistinguishable from all part-time workers ($19,330). Non-artist cultural workers part-time: $14,830. Since most people who make content do so part-time, $20,380 is the far more relevant administrative benchmark than the $70,170 that headlines usually quote. The NEA warns that part-time earnings trends should be read cautiously given variability.

TREND: full-time artist median earnings rose 5 per cent between 2015 and 2023 ($66,910 to $70,170), SLOWER than the 8 per cent rise for all full-time workers ($56,860 to $61,170). Non-artist cultural workers showed no measurable net change: +10 per cent 2015-2019 ($54,370 to $59,760) then -6 per cent 2019-2023 (to $56,040). Earnings fell in 2023 for three artist occupations: dancers, producers and architects.

BY OCCUPATION (2023, full-time): dancers $39,230 at the bottom to architects $99,630 at the top, with producers $77,250 second. Non-artist cultural workers span $35,200 (ushers and lobby attendants) to $81,070 (photographic process workers). By education: artists with less than high school earned $46,030 versus $38,720 for all similarly-educated full-time workers; at every college level artists out-earned non-artist cultural workers, but artists with a bachelor's degree or higher earned LESS than the general workforce with the same credential.

KEY CAVEAT the NEA states first: 'Individuals reporting primary employment as an artist or other cultural worker were less likely to be working full time than are workers in general.' Occupation is self-reported PRIMARY occupation only — the ACS asks about the job with the most usual hours — so anyone whose creative work is a secondary activity is invisible in these counts. US-ONLY; does not generalize to Southeast Asia. `evergreen`

- [[ilo-flagship-report]] — ILO Flagship Report — ILO (2021) flagship report, 'World Employment and Social Outlook 2021: The role of digital labour platforms in transforming the world of work'. 131,000 words — FLAG FOR SOURCE-ANALYST DELEGATION. This is the primary source behind the platform-earnings figures that the UNDP 2025 Asia-Pacific report and most institutional literature repeat without their sample sizes.

SAMPLES (ILO's own surveys, 2017-2020). Online web-based platforms: global surveys of microtask workers (2017) and freelance/competitive-programming workers (2019-20), about 2,900 respondents from 100 countries; plus country surveys in China (1,107 respondents) and Ukraine (761), giving n = 4,009 for the pooled online-earnings tables. Location-based: about 5,000 respondents in app-based taxi (9 countries) and delivery (11 countries) 2019-20, plus over 2,200 in traditional taxi (9 countries) and delivery (4 countries). CRITICAL CAVEAT stated by the ILO: 'there was no sampling base from which a random sample could be drawn' — these are non-probability convenience samples of ACTIVE workers, so they are survivorship-biased by construction.

EARNINGS (the numbers everyone else cites). Average hourly earnings on online web-based platforms US$3.4 including unpaid time, with half of workers earning less than US$2.1/hour and 66 per cent earning below the average. Paid-time-only averages are much higher — US$11.2 on freelance platforms and US$4.4 on microtask — but fall to US$7.6 and US$3.3 once unpaid search, bidding and profile-building time is counted; 64 per cent (freelance) and 63 per cent (microtask) earn below their platform average. Workers in developing countries earn 60 per cent less on freelance platforms than developed-country workers even after controlling for characteristics and task type. Microtask workers earn 64 per cent less in India and 81 per cent less in the United States than comparable traditional-labour-market work, with the gap wider for women. On the gender gap the ILO is more equivocal than its citers: at the global level, after controlling for education and experience, there is NO difference in hourly earnings on freelance platforms, though significant country-level gaps exist.

DOCUMENTED CONSTRAINTS AND INVOLUNTARY EXIT. About 45 per cent of online platform workers who want more work say the binding constraint is lack of available work; 18 per cent on microtask platforms cite inability to find well-paid tasks and 41 per cent on freelance platforms cite difficulty finding clients. Involuntary attrition is measured directly: 19 per cent of app-based taxi workers and 15 per cent of app-based delivery workers reported account deactivation; among those, deactivation lasted up to seven days for 73 per cent (taxi) and 69 per cent (delivery) but was PERMANENT for 7 and 15 per cent respectively. About 65 per cent considered the deactivation unjustified; 69 and 83 per cent appealed, and 48 and 59 per cent were dissatisfied with the outcome. A footnote makes the survivorship problem explicit: 'Workers whose accounts had been permanently deactivated on one platform were using another platform at the time of the survey' — permanently exited workers are structurally invisible to this instrument.

SCOPE: microtask, freelance, competitive-programming, taxi and delivery platforms. NOT content creation; no creator, influencer or YouTube/TikTok data. Includes an Indonesian Gojek respondent quote but no Southeast Asian earnings tables. `evergreen`
