# Interim report: living-wage-share-magnitude

**Locus question:** Survey and telemetry evidence disagree by two to three orders of magnitude on what share of creators earns a living wage; establish which figure the report prints and why.
**Flavor:** dialectical

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep had already assembled both sides of this fight and, unusually, had already diagnosed the mechanism. The contradiction graph (`living-wage-share` cluster) laid out Side A (self-selected surveys: Linktree 12% of full-time creators >$50k, NeoReach 43% >$50k, Goldman Sachs "~4% professional," TikTok-Indonesia 63% above provincial minimum wage) against Side B (telemetry: Regner's Patreon transaction record ~0.18% of all registered campaigns, Rieder et al.'s YouTube payout model ~0.043% of channels above US median wage, the Twitch leak's ~0.1%, UK IPO's ~1.6% of ONS-counted musicians) and flagged that Hua et al. 2022 measured a comparable sampling-frame inflation at "only about 10x," leaving a genuine residual unexplained. The redundancy audit (`temp/redundancy-audit.md`) additionally traced the Goldman Sachs "4% professional" figure to a SignalFire venture-capital blog estimate laundered through a Zippia listicle — i.e., not an independent measurement at all — and traced the "3% capture 90%" statistic to a unit-transformation error (Bärtl measured 85% of *views*, not revenue). The `survey-vs-telemetry-regime` cluster separately established, with a measured coefficient, that self-selected creator surveys cannot see the zero-earning majority that telemetry shows dominates every platform (Regner: 82.6% of Patreon campaigns never pledged; Rieder et al.: 31.7M of 36.1M YouTube channels earn 0% of ad revenue; Zhao et al.: 25% of VTubers received zero Superchats). The consensus-claims file already states as high-confidence, 5-instrument-corroborated: "Living-wage thresholds used in Western creator research are roughly an order of magnitude above Southeast Asian equivalents, so 'share earning a living wage' is not a transferable quantity." My job was to go from this diagnosis to an actual quantitative decomposition and a printable number.

## What the new sources say

I did not need many new fetches — the corpus's cited primary sources (`vol0123456789-3`, `httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628`, `music-creators-earnings-in`, `many-top-twitch-streamers-dont-make-minimum-wage-and-3-new-takeaways-from-the-tw`, `the-unbearable-lightness-of-streaming-superjoost-playlist`) contained load-bearing numbers the width summary hadn't fully extracted, and several already-vaulted-but-unread notes (`less-than-2-of-content-creators-on-patreon-earn-monthly-minimum-wage`, `no-one-makes-a-living-on-patreon-the-outline`, `pdf-linktree-creatorreport-2022-...`, `with-this-report-we-assess-the-current-state-of-the-creator-economy-including`, `rich-get-richer-platform-attention-and-earnings-inequality`, `who-reaps-all-the-superchats-a-large-scale-analysis-of-income`, `adbi-working-paper-series`, `tiktok-buka-data-63-persen-kreator-di-indonesia-berpenghasilan-tinggi-merdekacom`, `ada-8-juta-kreator-konten-tapi-baru-14-persen-yang-cuan`) supplied the rest. I additionally dispatched a fetcher for two items not resolvable from the vault: the original "Hua et al. 2022" sampling-bias paper the contradiction graph cites without a note ID, and the Wall Street Journal's original percentile analysis of the Twitch leak. That fetch was still in progress when this note was finalized; see Open Questions.

**Regner 2021 (`vol0123456789-3`), Patreon complete transaction data 2013–2015, n=139,366 registered campaigns.** "Most of the registered campaigns (115,183 out of 139,366) did not receive any pledges" — 82.6% zero-earning. Of the 24,257 "active" campaigns (received ≥1 pledge, i.e. 17.4% of all registered), "the top 5%, about 1200 creators, receive a substantial income and around 250 creators (the top 1%) are funded to an extent that can be considered an income sufficient for making a living" (≥$2,500/month). This gives two exact, computable shares from one dataset: **250/139,366 = 0.18% of all registered campaigns**, and **250/24,257 = 1.03% of active (pledge-receiving) campaigns**. The gap between those two numbers *is* the denominator effect, quantified: 5.75x.

**Digital Music News / Graphtreon (`less-than-2-of-content-creators-on-patreon-earn-monthly-minimum-wage`), Oct 2017, n=79,420 tracked Patreon creators (US, global platform).** Independent of Regner (different year, different data source — Graphtreon aggregates creators' public earnings pages, not transaction-level access): "only 1,393 creators (under 2%) earned the federal monthly minimum wage of $1,160 a month" — **1.75%**. "If changed to $15 per hour, only 0.8%, or 635 creators, made that amount" — a **2.2x swing from threshold choice alone**, on the identical population. Graphtreon's denominator (79,420 "active" tracked creators) is structurally the same kind of already-attempting population as Regner's "active" subset, and at a comparable ~$2,500-2,600/month bar the two studies — two years apart, different methodology — converge on roughly 1%: Regner 1.03%, Graphtreon 0.8%. The primary source for this analysis, `no-one-makes-a-living-on-patreon-the-outline` (Brent Knepper, Dec 2017), confirms the same figures and adds first-person texture: creators earning $120–$500/month after months of full-time effort.

**Rieder et al. 2023 (`httpsdoiorg10117720563051231180628`), "Making a Living in the Creator Economy," Social Media + Society, YouTube channel sample derived from Rieder et al. 2020's 36M-channel dataset, applying Google's disclosed 2019 ad-revenue split ($15B total, $8.25B distributed to channels).** Their Table 1 gives channel counts and average annual ad revenue per subscriber tier: Diamond (10M+ subs) 517 channels, avg $1,707,446/yr; Gold (1M-10M) 14,969, avg $165,892; Silver (100k-1M) 138,284, avg $16,346; Bronze (10k-100k) 769,472, avg $2,058; Opal (1k-10k) 3,491,938, avg $295; <1k subscribers 31,727,531 channels, 0% of revenue. Total channels: 36,142,711. The paper's own framing: "only a small fraction of channels can expect to earn a living wage from advertising income... a steep income pyramid where the lower tiers are basically working for free." I computed the exact shares at two thresholds from their own table: at the **US federal minimum-wage-equivalent annual floor (~$15,080)**, Silver-tier-and-above clears it — (517+14,969+138,284)/36,142,711 = **0.43%**. At the **2020 US median household income (~$67,521)**, only Diamond+Gold clear it — (517+14,969)/36,142,711 = **0.043%** — exactly the figure the loci-analyst cited. Same dataset, same denominator, **10x difference purely from threshold choice**.

**Washington Post (`many-top-twitch-streamers-dont-make-minimum-wage-and-3-new-takeaways-from-the-tw`), Oct 2021, analysis of the Twitch payout leak (~2019–2021 window), "top 10,000 highest paid streamers."** "25 percent of the top 10,000 highest paid Twitch streamers don't make minimum wage" — meaning 75% of that top-10k slice do, but "there are around 9 million streamers on Twitch, meaning that 10,000 is a vanishingly small drop in the bucket — just 0.1 percent of all streamers." Combining: at most ~7,500 streamers clear US minimum wage from Twitch earnings alone, against a base of ~9 million — **≈0.083% of all Twitch streamers**, consistent in order of magnitude with the Rieder/Regner "all registered" figures. Van Dreunen's contemporaneous analysis (`the-unbearable-lightness-of-streaming-superjoost-playlist`) independently states "8.1 million active streamers on Twitch in September [2021]" — close corroboration of WaPo's ~9M denominator — and adds that streamers receive only ~9% of income from ads and 56% from subscriptions, and that even the #1-#10 average channel nets "only" $1,209/hour of on-air time once the enormous off-camera labor (marketing, visual identity, networking, admin) required is considered — a caution against treating even leaked payout totals as pure profit.

**UK IPO, "Music Creators' Earnings in the Digital Era" (`music-creators-earnings-in`), 2021, two instruments in one report.** (1) A **census, not a survey**: Official Charts Company streaming-count data for all UK-charting artists. 1,723 artists achieved ≥1 million UK streams in October 2020 — "that constitutes 0.41% of all artists whose music achieved at least one stream in October in the UK." The report explicitly labels its ≥1M-streams/month living-wage threshold "speculation," not a derived income figure. Applying BPI's 41.8% UK-artist share to that 1,723 yields an estimated 720 UK-based artists, which against the ONS's independent count of 45,000 working UK musicians gives **720/45,000 = 1.6%** — this is the largest of the telemetry-side figures because its denominator (ONS-counted *working* musicians) is already filtered to people plausibly practicing music professionally, not "everyone who ever uploaded a track." (2) A **709-respondent self-selected survey**: 72% of all respondents and 64% of those earning entirely from music made **£30,000 or less** (against the ONS full-time median of £31,461); of the 197 respondents for whom music was their *sole* income, 43% earned **≤£20,000/year**, against a national-minimum-wage-equivalent full-time salary of £17,076 — i.e., a large fraction of even self-selected survey respondents who say music is their only income sit at or near minimum wage. Notably, this self-selected sample *also* fails to show a living-wage majority — it is not just telemetry that finds low rates; the corpus's own most-detailed Western survey does too, once the "only source of income" filter is applied honestly.

**Strauss, Yang & Mazzucato (`rich-get-richer-platform-attention-and-earnings-inequality`), official Patreon monthly earnings data, n=104,719, March 2018/2021/2024 cross-sections, creators segmented by which social platform they link Patreon from.** Median *monthly* Patreon earnings by linking platform: Facebook $47, Instagram $59, Patreon-only $57, Twitch $46, Twitter/X $72, YouTube $47. Even restricting the denominator to creators who have *already* set up a monetization account linked from an established social profile — a substantially pre-selected, monetization-attempting population — the median is $46–$72/month (**$552–$864/year**), nowhere near any living-wage threshold at any country's rate. Mean earnings ($149–$373/month) are 3-6x the median on every platform, confirming extreme right-skew: platform-level "average creator earnings" statistics (the kind platforms disclose in aggregate) systematically overstate the typical creator's experience.

**Linktree Creator Report 2022 (`pdf-linktree-creatorreport-2022-...`), n=9,576 Linktree users, fielded 2021, country composition undisclosed, self-selected/vendor survey.** The primary source for the widely-cited "12% of full-time creators make over $50K a year" figure. Critically, the report's own data show the "full-time" label is not itself an income-verified category: **46% of self-identified full-time creators make under $1,000/year**, and only 6% of "beginner" creators (under 12 months) have earned over $10K. "Full-time" here is a self-description, not a monetization or income threshold — which means Side A's own denominator is internally inconsistent with the plain-English claim being made about it.

**Goldman Sachs, "Creator Economy" research note (`with-this-report-we-assess-the-current-state-of-the-creator-economy-including`), 26 March 2025.** I verified the redundancy audit's finding directly: the report's own table footnote states "'Professional' and 'Amateur' based on **self-definitions** (e.g. full-time vs. part-time)" with **no income threshold specified at all**, sourced to "Zippia, Influencer Marketing Hub, Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research." The oft-repeated "4% of creators are professional/earn a living" framing therefore has **no underlying income measurement in the 2025 source** — it is a self-identification split, not a living-wage-share estimate, and should not be cited in the report as an income statistic at all.

**Southeast Asia: TikTok Indonesia self-disclosure (`tiktok-buka-data-63-persen-kreator-di-indonesia-berpenghasilan-tinggi-merdekacom`), 15 Jul 2025, statement by TikTok's Head of Public Policy to DPR Komisi I (Indonesian parliament).** "8 juta kreator berpenghasilan melalui fitur monetisasi TikTok. 63 Persen di antaranya berpenghasilan lebih dari upah minimum" — 8 million *already-monetized* creators, 63% of whom earn above (unspecified, presumably provincial) minimum wage — against "125 Juta pengguna aktif" (125 million monthly active Indonesian TikTok users). This is a single-source, unaudited, no-methodology corporate disclosure to a legislature — no raw data, no definition of which regional UMR was used (Indonesia's UMR ranges roughly Rp2.0–2.1M/month in the poorest provinces to Rp5.4M/month in Jakarta, i.e., roughly USD $130–$350/month), no sampling frame. Recomputed on TikTok's *own* full-MAU denominator rather than its self-selected "already monetized" denominator: 8M/125M = 6.4% of all Indonesian TikTok users monetize at all; 63% of that 6.4% clears UMR = **≈4.0% of all Indonesian TikTok MAU**. That recalculated 4% figure is within roughly one order of magnitude of the Western telemetry figures (0.04–1.6%) — not two to three — once the SAME "all accounts" denominator convention is applied. The remaining gap is well explained by threshold: Indonesia's UMR (~$130-350/month, close to ADBI's *developing-Asia living wage* estimate of $254.28–$414.88/month, `adbi-working-paper-series`) is roughly 4–10x lower than the US federal minimum-wage-equivalent ($1,257/month) and roughly 16-44x lower than the US median household income equivalent ($5,627/month) used in the Western telemetry studies. A separate, incompatible Indonesian claim (`ada-8-juta-kreator-konten-tapi-baru-14-persen-yang-cuan`, Aug 2023, IMF-cited population figure via a TipTip PR statement) claims only 14% of Indonesia's ~8 million *potential* creators "cuan" (profit) — but tracing its derivation shows it is a garbled application of Linktree's *global* self-selected 59%-haven't-monetized-yet statistic to the Indonesian population, not an independent Indonesian measurement. Indonesia therefore reproduces, at smaller scale, the exact same evidentiary pathology documented for the West in the redundancy audit (SignalFire→Zippia→Goldman): single-source, no-methodology figures citation-laundered into "data."

**ADBI Working Paper 940 (Khatiwada & Flaminiano) (`adbi-working-paper-series`).** Not creator-specific — a general developing-Asia labor economics paper — but it is the only rigorously derived quantitative living-wage benchmark for the region in the corpus: "Living wage rates for developing Asia were estimated at USD 254.28 per month for Option 1 (anchored on the absolute poverty line) and USD 414.88 per month for Option 2 (anchored on the moderate poverty line)," derived from the World Bank's $1.90/$3.20-per-day thresholds inflated 10% for savings/discretionary income. This is the correct SEA-appropriate denominator-independent threshold the report should use instead of importing a US dollar figure wholesale.

## Evidence synthesis

Decomposing the 100–1000x gap into its three candidate components, using only measurements the corpus (or my new reading of it) actually quantifies:

**(1) Denominator choice** is the single largest, most cleanly quantified lever. Regner's own paper gives both cuts on identical data: 0.18% of all registered Patreon campaigns vs. 1.03% of campaigns that received at least one pledge — a **5.75x multiplier** from denominator alone, on the same population, same year, same threshold. Rieder et al.'s YouTube data shows the same structural pattern even more starkly: 31.7 million of 36.1 million channels (87.8%) sit in the <1,000-subscriber tier and capture literally 0% of ad revenue. Any published "share of creators" statistic that filters its base to "full-time," "active," "already monetized," or "self-identified creator" is, by construction, excluding a population that is 80-90%+ of the true universe and that earns ~$0. This is why Side A's surveys (which recruit *from* self-identified creator populations, e.g. Linktree's 9,576 Linktree users, NeoReach's undisclosed panel) and Side B's telemetry (which starts from the full registered/channel population) are not measuring the same denominator, and a large share of the headline gap evaporates once this is made explicit.

**(2) Threshold choice** is a second, independently-sized lever, best demonstrated within a single dataset: Rieder et al.'s own numbers move from 0.43% to 0.043% — exactly 10x — solely by swapping the bar from "US federal minimum wage annualized" to "US median household income." Graphtreon's Patreon data shows a smaller but real 2.2x swing (1.75% to 0.8%) moving from $1,160/month to $15/hour (~$2,600/month). At the international level, the threshold lever is larger still: ADBI's developing-Asia living wage ($254–415/month) is 3–5x below the US federal-minimum-wage-equivalent ($1,257/month) and 13.5–22x below the US median household income equivalent ($5,627/month). Applying a Western income threshold to a Southeast Asian creator population, or vice versa, is not measuring "the same thing at a different rate" — it is answering a different question, and the report must never print a bare percentage without stating both the currency-denominated threshold and the country it is anchored to.

**(3) Sampling/self-selection bias**, the component the loci-analyst flagged Hua et al. as measuring at ~10x, is real but I can only partially independently corroborate its magnitude from primary sources I read directly (a follow-on fetch for the specific Hua et al. 2022 paper was in progress at write-time; see Open Questions). What I *can* verify directly: the UK IPO's own 709-respondent self-selected survey, despite being drawn from a population that opted into answering a professional-creator survey, still shows 72% of respondents at or below the UK median salary and 43% of "music-only-income" respondents at or below a minimum-wage-equivalent bar — i.e., even the "generous" self-selected instrument does not show a living-wage majority once its own most-relevant subgroup filter is applied honestly. Conversely, Side A's most-cited figures (Linktree, NeoReach, Goldman) diverge wildly from each other under nominally the same methodology-type and denominator (self-identified full-time/professional creators, same approximate year): 12% (Linktree) vs. 43% (NeoReach) vs. an income-undefined 4% self-identification split (Goldman) — a >3.5x internal spread among instruments that are supposedly all measuring "full-time creators clearing $50K." That internal instability is itself strong evidence that self-selected vendor surveys carry a wide, non-trivial noise/bias band on this specific quantity, independent of whatever Hua et al.'s exact coefficient turns out to be.

**(4) The residual.** Multiplying even conservative estimates of the three levers — denominator (~5-6x, per Regner), threshold (~10x within-country per Rieder, larger cross-country), and sampling bias (~5-10x, per the corpus's own Hua-et-al citation and the Linktree/NeoReach internal spread) — yields a combined multiplier in the 250x-600x range, which is the same order of magnitude as the observed headline gap between Side A's 4-63% and Side B's 0.04-1.6%. This means the honest conclusion is that **there is little to no unexplained residual**: nearly all of the apparent two-to-three-order-of-magnitude disagreement is attributable to denominator, threshold, and sampling-frame artifacts that are individually measurable in the corpus's own primary sources, not to a genuine substantive dispute about the underlying world. Side A and Side B are not competing estimates of the same quantity; they are accurate-in-their-own-terms answers to two different questions ("of people who already call themselves full-time/successful creators, what fraction clear a high bar?" vs. "of everyone who ever tried, what fraction clears a subsistence bar?").

### Position A: A meaningful minority (roughly one in ten to one in two) of creators earns a living wage

The strongest version of this case rests on the Linktree and UK IPO survey data specifically, not on Goldman (which makes no income claim) or NeoReach (undisclosed methodology, unreproduced). Linktree's n=9,576 survey, despite its limits, is a real fielded instrument with a 95% completion rate, and its 12%-of-full-time-creators->$50K figure is at least a *self-consistent* statistic about a *defined* subpopulation (creators who call themselves full-time). The UK IPO's 709-respondent survey is methodologically the most serious instrument on Side A: government-commissioned, professionally fielded, with published banded microdata, and it does not oversell — 72% of respondents make £30,000 or less, which is a modest, credible, non-cherry-picked number. If the report's question is "among people who have already established themselves as working creators, what fraction reach a comfortable income," these instruments are legitimate and the answer is a real, non-trivial minority, on the order of 15-40% depending on threshold.

### Position B: A tiny fraction (well under 2%, often under 0.1%) of creators earns a living wage

The strongest version of this case rests on Regner, Rieder et al., the Twitch leak, and Graphtreon — four independent measurements, different platforms, different years (2015, 2017, 2019-2021, 2019), different research groups, converging on the same order of magnitude once the denominator is "everyone who registered / everyone with a channel / everyone who streams," which is the only denominator that answers the query's actual question ("content creation as an occupation" — i.e., what happens to a representative entrant, not a self-selected survivor). These are transaction-level or platform-disclosed records, not surveys, so they are not subject to self-selection or recall bias, only to the (much smaller and well-understood) biases of the specific dataset (e.g., Rieder's average-revenue-per-tier is an estimate, not individual-level data; the Twitch leak covers a ~2-year window that may understate annualized income for newer streamers). If the report's question is "what is the true occupational prospect for someone entering content creation," this is the correct answer, and it is under 2% at a US subsistence-level threshold and under 0.1% at a US median-income threshold.

## Committed position

The report should print a **matrix, not a single number** — denominator × threshold × platform × year × country — because the decomposition above shows the "single figure" question is not resolvable without erasing exactly the information (scope) that explains the apparent disagreement. But if the report needs one headline sentence, it should be built on Position B's denominator (full population, not self-identified survivors), because that is the denominator that actually answers "content creation as an occupation": **across four independent telemetry sources spanning three platforms and two research traditions (Regner's Patreon transactions 2013-15, Graphtreon's Patreon aggregation Oct 2017, Rieder et al.'s YouTube payout model for 2019, and the Twitch payout leak analyzed by the Washington Post for 2019-2021), somewhere between 0.04% and 1.75% of all people who register an account or attempt monetization on a major creator platform reach a US subsistence-level income (~$15,000/year), and this share falls by roughly a further order of magnitude (to under 0.05%) at a US median-household-income bar.** Survey-based figures in the 4-63% range (Linktree, NeoReach, Goldman, TikTok Indonesia) are not wrong on their own terms, but they answer a narrower question — "of people who already identify as full-time/monetized creators, what fraction clear a bar" — and conflating the two is the report's single biggest risk of misleading a reader. The load-bearing reason: three of the four gap components (denominator, threshold, sampling bias) are independently quantifiable in the corpus's own primary sources at magnitudes (5-6x, 10x, 5-10x respectively) whose product accounts for essentially the entire observed 100-1000x headline gap, leaving little or no genuine residual disagreement about the underlying world.

- **Position:** The living-wage-share of content creators is a matrix-valued quantity, not a scalar; the correct headline figure for "content creation as an occupation" (full-population denominator, subsistence threshold) is under 2%, converging toward ~1% for a ~$2,500/month bar and under 0.1% for a US median-income bar, and the widely-cited 4-63% survey figures describe a different, narrower population (self-identified full-time/monetized creators) rather than a competing estimate of the same thing.
- **Confidence:** medium-high. High confidence in the denominator (5.75x, Regner) and threshold (10x, Rieder) decomposition components, since both are exact arithmetic on primary transaction/telemetry data I read directly. Medium confidence in the sampling-bias component and in the claim that the residual is fully explained, since I could not independently verify the Hua et al. 2022 paper's exact 36.7%/3.49% figures before writing this note (fetch was in progress; the corpus's contradiction-graph and consensus-claims treat it as high-confidence with 5 corroborating instruments, which I did partially corroborate via the UK IPO survey's own low-earning majority and the Linktree/NeoReach internal 3.5x spread, but this is corroboration of direction, not of the exact multiplier).
- **Boundary conditions:** This position holds for platforms with either complete transaction records (Patreon/Regner, Graphtreon) or platform-disclosed aggregate payout data with a channel-count breakdown (YouTube/Rieder) or a large-scale leak (Twitch). It holds least well for Southeast Asia, where no analogous complete-population telemetry study exists — the only regional figures (TikTok's 63%-of-8M self-disclosure, the IMF/TipTip 14% figure) are single-source, unaudited, and in the TikTok case already pre-filtered to an "already monetized" denominator; recomputing TikTok's own figure on its full-MAU denominator (≈4%) brings it within one order of magnitude of the Western figures, suggesting the underlying dynamic likely holds regionally too, but this is an inference from one platform's self-report, not an independent measurement, and should be flagged as such.
- **What would change this position:** (a) If the pending Hua et al. verification returns a paper that is NOT about this quantity, or returns a multiplier far below 5x, the "little to no residual" conclusion would weaken and the report would need to allow for a larger genuine (non-artifactual) disagreement between survey and telemetry regimes. (b) If a complete-transaction-record study analogous to Regner's ever became available for a Southeast Asian platform (e.g., an audited TikTok Indonesia payout dataset), and it showed a living-wage share above ~5% even on a full-MAU denominator at a US-equivalent threshold, that would falsify the "SEA reproduces the same dynamics at a lower threshold" inference and suggest a genuine regional difference in platform economics, not just a threshold artifact. (c) A large-N, randomly-sampled (not self-selected) creator income survey with a published sampling frame, if it ever independently reproduced NeoReach's 43% figure, would meaningfully strengthen Position A.
- **Evidence weight:** 4 independent telemetry/administrative instruments (Regner, Graphtreon, Rieder et al., Twitch leak/WaPo) support the low (sub-2%) figure; 1 rigorous government-commissioned survey with published microdata (UK IPO) is ambiguous — its raw numbers actually support the low figure once the "sole income" filter is applied, despite being classified as Side A evidence in the corpus; 3 self-selected vendor surveys/citation chains (Linktree, NeoReach, Goldman) support the high figure but disagree with each other by >3.5x and, in Goldman's case, do not measure income at all; 1 unaudited single-source platform disclosure to a foreign legislature (TikTok Indonesia) supports a high regional figure but collapses toward the low figures under denominator-normalization.

## Open questions

- Could not independently verify "Hua et al. 2022" — the paper the corpus's contradiction graph cites for a direct ~10x sampling-frame measurement (36.7% vs. 3.49% for an unspecified matched quantity). A fetcher was dispatched via academic-API and web search but had not returned results before this note was finalized. If it surfaces, the report should cite it directly rather than via the contradiction-graph's secondhand framing, and should confirm exactly what quantity (income-any, monetization-status, or something else) the 36.7%/3.49% pair refers to.
- The original Wall Street Journal analysis of the Twitch leak (the reported original source for widely-recirculated $120/$28/0.06% percentile figures) was also queued for fetching and had not returned; if recovered, it would let the report cite a primary rather than derivative source for those specific numbers and would let me check whether the WSJ's percentile denominator matches or differs from WaPo's ~9M/10,000 framing used here.
- No complete-transaction-record or platform-audited payout dataset exists for any Southeast Asian platform in this corpus. The TikTok Indonesia figure is the best available regional data point and it is a single, unaudited corporate disclosure. A genuinely load-bearing SEA figure would require either a leaked/audited payout dataset (as happened for Twitch) or a government-commissioned survey with the rigor of the UK IPO study, fielded specifically for Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, or Vietnam.
- I did not attempt to verify NeoReach/Influencer Marketing Hub's methodology directly (sample size, fielding date, country composition are undisclosed in the corpus); given its 43% figure is 3.5x Linktree's for nominally the same quantity and same year, and no primary methodology could be located in the corpus, I recommend the report either drop this figure or cite it explicitly as methodologically opaque.
