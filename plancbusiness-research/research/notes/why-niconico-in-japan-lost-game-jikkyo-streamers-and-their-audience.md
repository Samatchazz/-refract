summary: 'Igarashi, Tanaka & Koyama (DiGRA JAPAN 12th Annual Conference, Feb 2022,
  peer-reviewed proceedings, Japan). Uses Niconico''s own public platform telemetry
  (Niconico Chart database, quarterly video-post-count and view-count, 2007 Q2-2021
  Q2) plus an original online survey of Japanese gaming-video viewers (Dynata-fielded,
  screening n=3,328, main survey n=1,356, stratified by sex x age-decade 10s/20s/30s,
  fielded Aug 24-Sep 7 2021) to explain Niconico''s decline. Key numbers: Niconico
  paid-membership peaked at 2.56 million in 2016, then fell by over 1 million by 2021.
  Video-posting-count began declining from ~2011 but total view-count did not begin
  declining until 2016 -- a documented multi-year lag the authors read as creator
  outflow (posting decline) preceding and causing viewer outflow (view-count decline),
  not the reverse. The authors attribute Niconico''s 2011-launched creator-payment
  program (''Creator Incentive Program'') failing to produce YouTube-Partner-Program-scale
  earners (no HIKAKIN-scale creator emerged) as the proximate driver, alongside under-investment
  in infrastructure (30-minute live-stream caps, viewer-count limits, forced low-quality
  ''Economy Mode'' for non-paying viewers). Table 2 gives exact reason percentages
  from the n=1,356 survey for why respondents stopped watching Niconico regularly:
  ''creators/streamers moved to other services'' is the top or joint-top reason in
  every one of 6 demographic cells (35% men 10s, 35% men 20s, 33% men 30s, 42% women
  10s, 29% women 20s, 29% women 30s), ahead of genre decline, quality/latency complaints,
  app usability, and toxic comments. CAVEATS: this is platform-level aggregate evidence
  (quarterly posting/view-count time series functioning as a population-level creator-outflow
  proxy), not individual-creator survival/hazard telemetry -- no per-creator cohort
  or survival curve is constructed. The reason data in Table 2 surveys VIEWERS about
  why they stopped watching, not creators about why they stopped creating, so it is
  adjacent to but does not directly answer ''why creators quit.'' It is nonetheless
  the most quantified Japanese-language academic finding located linking platform-level
  creator exodus to insufficient monetization (income) -- directly relevant to Gap
  3 as a qualitative/correlational counterpoint (not a causal hazard model) to the
  pipeline''s ''income does not predict continuation'' position, and relevant to Gap
  5 as the closest non-English platform-scale telemetry found, though it measures
  aggregate posting/view counts rather than individual creator survival.'

Abstract
This study focuses on the expansion of "Game Jikkyo" on online video platforms. This study examines interplatform competition in the gaming video content market in Japan and the U.S. through the intensifying competition among major U.S. platformers and the stagnation of the Japanese online video platform, NicoNico. In conclusion, the stagnation of the NicoNico was caused by the outflow of the gaming video content creators due to the lack of revenue gained, as well as the migration of viewers.
