Across Asia, digital platforms are changing the way that work is organized and executed. Platforms are digital 
applications and marketplaces that connect and facilitate an exchange between suppliers and consumers. 
They are growing quickly, with one estimate placing their gross revenues at $455 billion as of 2023.1 One of the 
most common types of exchanges they factilitate is the supply of services or labour. Platforms that facilitate 
these types of exchanges are often called digital labour platforms. These platforms have faced criticism, 
especially in the more formalized labour markets of Europe and North America, that they are helping to create 
a gig economy and shift away from the employer–employee relationship. The starting point for many Asian 
economies is different and often more informal, but these countries will also experience many of the same 
opportunities and challenges brought about by digital labour platforms elsewhere. 
This report reviews some of the major legal and regulatory challenges related to platform work and examines 
how employer and business membership organizations (EBMOs) can shape this framework while expanding 
their organizations to provide more effective policy advocacy and services for the platform economy. The 
report starts by briefly defining key concepts, including the platform economy. It then moves to a review of 
recent global developments in labour laws and regulatory frameworks that govern platforms, in areas including 
employment relationships, dismissal or termination, earnings, workers’ organization and representation and 
other issues. It then looks at recent developments in Southeast Asian countries. The report closes by reviewing 
how the rise of labour platforms may affect both the legal and regulatory framework as well as EBMOs in 
Southeast Asia. It reviews relevant issues and makes suggestions on potential policy options for which EBMOs 
could advocate as well as membership strategies and institutional considerations that they could adopt. 
An overview of digital platforms
Digital platforms are intermediaries “that bring together groups of users to facilitate economic or social 
exchange”.2 Digital platforms are often characterized by network effects, which means that the value of the 
product, service or platform increases with the number of users. Platforms have a range of business models. 
Some facilitate the sale of physical goods while others facilitate the exchange of services. Both types of 
platforms have grown considerably in recent years, but they have done so more quickly in some countries 
and sectors than others. Although it is difficult to get precise information on the number of platforms and 
users around the world, the International Labour Organization (ILO) estimated the presence of 777 active 
online web-based platforms (such as microtask, freelance and competitive programming) and location-based 
platforms in the taxi and delivery sector as of in January 2021.3
Policy issues in the platform economy
The increasing prominence of digital platforms, especially due to the COVID-19 pandemic, has drawn attention 
to several important yet unresolved issues in platform work. Perhaps no issue is as important and contentious 
as how to classify the people who work through digital platforms. The vast majority of platforms classify their 
workers as “independent contractors”. In recent years, various jurisdictions have found that they should be

classified as employees, independent contractors and even other types of classifications, such as “dependent 
contractors” or “workers”. Numerous courts, including in Argentina, the Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland, 
Taiwan (China) and Uruguay, have ruled that platform workers should be classified as employees, although 
some of the rulings were case-specific. Jurisdictions, including the European Union, Spain and the American 
state of California, are considering or have taken legislative steps to make it much more difficult to classify 
platform workers as independent contractors. In other countries, including Australia, Belgium, Brazil and New 
Zealand, courts, tribunals or ombudsmen have ruled that platform workers are not employees of a certain digital 
platform. The United Kingdom’s Supreme Court found that Uber drivers are “dependent contractors”, as have 
courts in Canada and Italy. Germany and Spain are considering introducing this type of worker classification. 
Another important policy issue and one that directly relates to worker classification, is dismissal and termination. 
Many platforms include language in their terms and conditions that allows them to terminate the accounts of 
platform workers, sometimes for any reason at all or without explanation. In some jurisdictions, regulations 
have been established or are under consideration to govern termination. For example, a new regulation in 
Ontario, Canada, requires that platforms provide a worker with a written explanation and a two-week notice 
period, unless the account closure is due to wilful misconduct of the worker. The European Commission is 
considering rules that will prevent platforms from terminating workers simply for exercising their labour rights. 
Earnings have long been one of the most important questions for platform workers, with some jurisdictions, 
including China and India, requiring platforms to ensure that workers earn at least the minimum wage. The 
government of Ontario, Canada, has gone further by also giving platform workers the right to recurring pay 
periods and pay days and prohibiting deductions or withholdings. 
The terms and conditions of platform work vary significantly between different locations and sectors. In 
some jurisdictions, digital platforms are required to pay for some types of insurance. For example, China’s 
DiDi Chuxing is required to offer workers a medical insurance plan and pays some of the contributions, while 
in France, platforms are required to reimburse occupational accident and illness insurance costs. There are 
isolated cases of platforms offering other benefits, for example, Deliveroo provides sick pay in France. But 
these examples are limited. 
Workers’ organizations and representation in the platform economy has been an expanding area of focus. 
Many jurisdictions prohibit self-employed workers from bargaining collectively out of fear that they could 
coordinate and affect prices. Both France and Ireland have made legal changes to permit platform workers 
to bargain collectively and, in the case of France, join a trade union. There have also been several collective 
agreements reached on behalf of platform workers. For example, the delivery platform Just Eat and the the 
Workers’ General Union (Unión General de Trabajadores) and Workers’ Commissions (Comisiones Obreras) 
trade unions in Spain reached an agreement covering working time, working conditions and wages. Elsewhere, 
platforms have engaged in voluntary dialogue with workers and workers’ representatives who were not 
formally unionized.
Platform economy 
Digital platforms have been growing in Southeast Asian countries for years – a trend that accelerated 
significantly due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The growth in the size of these platforms and the number of 
people working through them has increased attention on the quality of work they provide. In some countries, 
such as Indonesia, platform workers earn more than other informal workers. One study found that they earned 
6 per cent more, after accounting for differences in hours worked.4 Another study by the ILO, which focused

For the most contentious issue in platform work – the classification of the working relationship – the majority of 
Southeast Asian countries consider these workers as independent contractors. Some countries are considering 
changes to this approach. In December 2021, Malaysia’s Deputy Human Resources Minister stated that the 
Government planned to amend the Employment Act 1955 (Act 265) to allow drivers whose work was arranged 
through digital platforms to be recognized as workers.6 Most other countries continue to use a range of tests 
to determine the appropriate classification of the working relationship. 
Several Southeast Asian countries are considering reforms of the terms and conditions for platform workers. In 
Singapore, for example, the Government is studying changes that may include requiring platform companies 
to make Central Provident Fund contributions and provide coverage against work injuries, likely under the 
Work Injury Compensation Act.7 Thailand recently changed its national social insurance scheme to include self-
employed workers who are not already covered while also subsidizing their coverage. Indonesia, Philippines 
and Viet Nam already subsidize health insurance schemes for certain population groups, including freelancers. 
Some Southeast Asian countries are considering, or have already adopted, measures to regulate platform 
workers’ earnings. For example, Indonesia established minimum per-kilometre fares for some transportation 
platforms. 
And a few Southeast Asian countries are also reviewing the legal and regulatory framework on platform 
workers’ organization and representation. Thailand is reviewing its Labour Relations Act, with freedom of 
association and protection for the right to organize under consideration. As of March 2022, Singapore was 
studying changes to allow platform workers’ representation. 
EBMOs and the changing reality of platform work
Economies and labour markets in Southeast Asia are being reshaped by changes in technology, new ways of 
doing business and even the COVID-19 pandemic. Digital platforms present a unique challenge for the region’s 
EBMOs as well as important opportunities to expand their membership base and continue to shape the legal 
and regulatory framework regarding work. One of the biggest changes brought on by platforms is that they 
are creating a new way for people to be engaged for their labour. As the ILO noted in its World Employment 
and Social Outlook 2021 report, platforms are “transforming the world of work”.8 This presents an important 
challenge for EBMOs, whose membership packages and service offerings typically revolve around traditional 
employment relations. If platforms continue to drive a shift away from traditional employment and collective 
bargaining and undermine the competitiveness of these institutions, it could significantly undermine the 
appeal of a labour-focused EBMO. 
Many EBMOs have faced challenges in recruiting platforms to join their organization. Observers have noted 
that there appears to be a lack of interest among platforms globally in joining some types of EBMOs, especially 
those focused on employment-related issues. The lack of platform membership in EBMOs may undermine 
their representativity. It could also result in new types of dialogue about employment-focused issues that 
exclude the EBMO, affecting national-level social dialogue.

With platform work issues growing in importance, it is essential for EBMOs to develop a clear policy position that 
reflects the views and interests of their members. On the question of employment classification, many EBMOs 
prefer to continue advocating for independent contractor status. The current direction of policy discussions 
suggests that there is increasing risk associated with advocating a blanket independent contractor status approach 
for all platform workers. EBMOs may want to consider changing their approach and call for some workers to 
continue being classified as independent contractors while others are classified as a dependent contractor. 
EBMOs could continue to advocate against classifying these workers as employees, citing: 
	
X the free entry and unlimited number of workers who can sign up to work through a digital platform; 
	
X control over the time, location and duration of work; and
	
X the ability to work for competing platforms. 
EBMOs may get more traction if they combine opposition to the employee classification with support for 
increased access to and financial support for the social protection of platform workers. EBMOs should consider 
calling for platforms to pay for work injury insurance and liability insurance for workplace incidents. They 
should also advocate for platform workers to have access to medical insurance and/or a public health insurance 
scheme, pension or private retirement scheme and paid leave. EBMOs may also put forward a position that 
these benefits should be funded by either the workers or a combination of workers and platform, depending 
on the working relationship. 
EBMOs could also advocate for changes in taxation and competition rules to ensure fair competition between 
platforms and other businesses. This does not mean that taxation policies for platform and non-platform 
workers must be the same. It could mean introducing reasonable requirements for platforms to ensure tax 
compliance of workers who use them. EBMOs could call for:
	
X select tax-related data disclosures from platforms;
	
X fair tax treatment of independent contractors who work through platforms and offline independent 
contractors who provide the same service; and 
	
X an obligation for platforms with dependent workers to withhold money due for taxes and remit this to 
the government, similar to the requirements applied to traditional enterprises. 
EBMOs should seek to reinforce existing tripartite structures and the role of their organization in all labour and 
social matters. EBMOs should push back against direct negotiations between governments and platforms over 
the organization or regulation of work when these negotiations do not involve trade unions and employers’ 
representatives. This undermines tripartite structures and risks creating multiple, uncoordinated regulatory 
frameworks for work. They should also encourage union partners not to exclude EBMOs from dialogue with 
digital platforms about national-level policies on the regulation of work. EBMOs could consider developing 
membership packages for digital platforms as well as such services as legal advisory on compliance with the 
country’s worker classification framework or representation of platforms in labour-related disputes.

Introduction
Across Asia, digital platforms are changing the way that work is organized and executed. Platforms are digital 
applications and marketplaces that allow suppliers and consumers to connect and engage in economic exchange. 
These platforms are growing in size quickly, with one estimate putting gross revenues at around $455 billion 
as of 2023.9 Some platforms, such as Amazon and Alibaba, connect suppliers of goods with consumers who are 
seeking those goods. Other platforms connect suppliers of services (or labour) with businesses or consumers 
who are seeking those services. The growth of platforms is increasing the impacts of their disruptive business 
model and raising many questions about platform-based labour as well as the future of businesses, business 
organizations and the traditional employer–employee relationship. 
Platforms are already having significant implications for the organization of work and employment in Asia. 
Digital labour platforms have faced criticism that they are helping to create a gig economy and shift away from 
the employer–employee relationship. This criticism has been especially strong in Europe and North America, 
which have well-developed labour markets where the majority of workers are employed by businesses. This 
is not the case in many countries in Asia, where a considerable share of the labour force works informally. 
For these workers, platforms present fewer risks and more potential benefits. The challenges associated with 
platform work remain and have become increasingly prominent due to the economic disruptions brought 
by the COVID-19 pandemic.

Governments in Southeast Asia are just beginning to consider their legal and regulatory framework to platform 
work. Social partners have an important place in shaping this framework, and it is essential for employer and 
business membership organizations (EBMOs) to take an active and well-informed role in this. Several issues 
will be addressed by doing so: One of the most prominent is whether or under what conditions an individual 
working through a platform is an “employee” or is “self-employed”. Many platforms have blurred the line 
between employee, self-employed and entrepreneur, and many countries are considering reforms to these 
classifications that to make them more suitable for platform work. A related challenge, which is also heavily 
contested, is the legal and regulatory framework governing earnings, including the question of whether 
workers in the platform economy should be protected by minimum wages legislation or regulations. Countries 
are also considering whether and how to regulate dismissal or unilateral termination of platform workers and 
the representation and organization of individuals or businesses that primarily depend on digital platforms. 
This report provides an overview of recent developments in the changes to the legal and regulatory frameworks 
governing platform work worldwide. This includes both changes to legislation as well as court decisions that 
have clarified how existing legal and regulatory frameworks apply to work through digital platforms. It then 
looks at the situation in Southeast Asian countries, first by providing an overview of the development of the 
platform economy in the region. It then looks at changes and potential changes in the legal and regulatory 
frameworks. The report concludes with a review of the implications for EBMOs from the rise of digital platforms 
and the corresponding changes to the legal and regulatory framework. It reviews relevant issues and makes 
suggestions on potential policy options for which EBMOs could advocate as well as membership strategies 
and institutional questions for them to consider adopting.

Research approach and 
methodology
Although digital platforms are a relatively new phenomenon, they have grown quickly and become an 
important part of many economies in Southeast Asia and around the world. Because of this, the amount of 
research on platforms and their effects has grown exponentially in recent years, as has the amount of focus 
that policymakers have directed towards digital platforms. Although the research on platforms has provided 
a host of new insights, the literature is still developing. Further work is needed to understand the diversity of 
platform business models and country contexts in which they operate. The research that has been pursued 
has been useful for what has been a dynamic time for the legal and regulatory framework for platforms as 
well as for the business models of platforms, which can change either slightly or significantly, with some 
degree of regularity.
This guide is based on a thorough review of the global literature on digital platforms. It includes reviews of the 
platforms as well as the experiences and outcomes of people working through digital platforms. The report 
also reviews sources that detail changes in the legal and regulatory framework in jurisdictions around the 
world, which are covered in more detail in Southeast Asia. 
The research sought information from EBMOs on their responses to the changes brought about by digital 
platforms. Few EBMOs have done much study on the topic, and few organizations have published either reports 
or policy positions about labour regulations for platforms. As part of the research for this guide, EBMOs in 
the Southeast Asia were asked to complete a questionnaire about digital platforms. Their responses were 
sometimes limited, however. This may be due to the current context in the region, in which many governments 
and other stakeholders are just starting to consider legal and regulatory changes for the platform economy.

The platform economy: 
Overview
What is a digital platform? 
Digital platforms are an important but distinct part of the broader digital economy and have many characteristics 
that distinguish them from other parts of that economy. “Digital economy” is a more general term, referring 
to the “broad range of economic activities that use digitized information and knowledge as integral factors 
of production”.10 
Digital platforms have been defined as intermediaries “that bring together groups of users to facilitate economic 
or social exchange”.11 The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development views platforms as entities 
that facilitate “interactions between two or more distinct but interdependent sets of users (whether firms or 
individuals) who interact through the service via the Internet”.12 As these definitions distinguish, platforms do 
4


---

not buy or sell their own goods or services as a merchant or service provider would. Instead, they provide a 
marketplace where buyers and sellers can interact. Platforms, unlike suppliers of goods and services, create 
“value primarily by enabling direct interactions between two (or more) distinct types of affiliated customers”.13 
This definition of platforms, which centres on the interaction of two or more human parties, is also different 
from a technology platform, which is a software-based application that can help individuals interact with 
businesses, improve productivity at work, etc. 
Platforms are often referred to as either two-sided or multi-sided markets because they bring together 
multiple and different types of users. Two-sided markets are “markets in which one or several platforms 
enable interactions between end users and try to get the two (or multiple) sides ‘on board’ by appropriately 
charging each side. That is, platforms court each side while attempting to make, or at least not lose, money 
overall.”14Another important feature of digital platforms is the presence of network effects. Network effects 
exist when “the value of a product, service or platform depends on the number of buyers, sellers or users 
who leverage it”.15 As more users engage with a platform, it becomes more attractive to other potential new 
users. This creates a cycle of self-reinforcing positive growth.16 This effect is evidenced in transportation apps, 
such as Grab and Gojek. An increase in the number of riders will attract more drivers, and this in turn benefits 
all riders through reduced wait times and other benefits. 
The work undertaken on digital platforms is also commonly referred to as “platform work” or “gig work”.17 
However, these terms are not synonymous. Platform work is done by individuals who use a digital platform 
to mediate work arrangements. Gig work is characterized by a variety of arrangements “that have previously 
been denoted as precarious, flexible and contingent”.18 Gig workers can use digital platforms but can also be 
non-platform workers, such as casual wage workers and own-account workers in the conventional economy. 
The conflation of these terms has sometimes been used to highlight that platform work is often not based 
on a full-time, permanent employment relationship or to suggest that platform work is contributing to the 
erosion of the “standard employment relationship”.19 This is not the case with all platform workers, such 
as the drivers for Just Eat Takeaway.com, most of whom are direct or indirect employees of the platform.20 
Except when directly quoting other sources that use the “gig work” term, this report uses “platform work” 
and “platform workers”, given its focus on platform-mediated work. 
Taxonomy of digital platforms 
Even though digital platforms share some common characteristics, the platform model has been applied to a 
variety of markets. Digital platforms are used to facilitate interactions in which a range of goods and services 
are exchanged. Classifying these into an orderly structure is challenging because they span such a range of 
activities. Many platforms engage in the sale of physical goods. Some of these facilitate business-to-consumer 
transactions (Amazon) while others facilitate business-to-business transactions (Alibaba). Some platforms 
facilitate the rental of assets, such as accommodations, vehicles or even tools. Some platforms facilitate 
financial transactions between two parties or provide other business-related services.

Size and growth of digital platforms
Digital platforms are an increasingly important part of economies around the world. Despite their significant 
growth in recent years, platforms remain just a limited part of the vast majority of economies. For several 
reasons, they are also a difficult part of the economy to accurately measure because they are new, span a 
range of economic sectors and activities and often work across international borders. A 2019 estimate placed 
the size of the global gig economy at some $204 billion in gross revenue.21 The same source estimated that the 
global gig economy could be worth some $455 billion as of 2023, although this estimate has almost certainly 
been exceeded, given the growth of platforms due to the COVID-19 pandemic.22 
Digital platforms are not affecting all parts of economies equally. Instead, they have been heavily focused on 
some sectors and have been adopted much more quickly in some countries than in others. Platforms have 
been more transformative in such sectors as tourism, transport and logistics. This has created challenges 
for actors in these sectors as well as the traditional forms of employment they use.23 Of all sectors, digital 
platforms probably have been most transformative in transportation. An estimated 58 per cent of gross 
revenue generated by digital platforms in 2018 were from transportation-based services.24
The size and diversity of digital platforms, as well as their model of facilitating other parties to engage in 
exchange, makes it difficult to collect information on the number of platforms and their users. The International 
Labour Organization (ILO) estimated that, as of January 2021, there were 777 active online web-based 
platforms (such as microtask, freelance and competitive programming) and location-based platforms in the 
taxi and delivery sector globally.25 Data on the number of platform users is even more difficult to obtain, but 
limited surveys indicate that millions of individuals have worked through digital platforms. Surveys from the 
European Union found that 9.7 per cent of adults had worked on a digital platform at some point in their life. 
This varied significantly from one country to the next. For example, a survey in Switzerland found that only 
0.4 per cent of adults had worked on a digital platform in the past year, while in the United Kingdom, that 
prevalence was 4 per cent.26
Benefits and challenges of digital platforms 
Digital platforms create value and deliver benefits for workers and users in numerous ways. One of the most 
important ways in which digital platforms add value is by reducing transaction costs, including the costs 
of searching for a counterparty to a transaction and the costs of completing that transaction. Platforms 
assist in matching buyers and sellers and often do so in a way that is much more efficient than existing systems. 
Platforms often facilitate transactions between “individuals and organizations that would otherwise have 
difficulty finding each other”.27 This is one of the main ways in which platforms create value: They consummate 
the match.28 Real-world evidence has measured this effect. Some platforms can also facilitate payments or, 
at a minimum, communicate information on how and when the payment will be made.

Platforms also help create, analyse and distribute information. They provide price information, either by 
setting it directly or communicating price offers between prospective sellers and buyers. Often, price information

Some platforms can also help both workers and businesses access labour markets that they previously were 
not able to use. Workers who engage through online platforms can engage in work for businesses around 
the world when previously they would have been limited to working for businesses in their local geographical 
area. For some workers, especially those with skill sets that are globally in demand, this can help them to

markets that they previously could not. This can help them access workers with particular skills who may not 
be available locally, a greater number of workers than may be available locally and workers at a lower cost 
than may be available locally. 
Even though platforms create a host of benefits and add value in some ways, they also create a range of 
challenges. They facilitate cross-border economic activity that previously had not crossed international 
boundaries, raising new questions about taxation and jurisdiction for labour laws. They create new types of 
engagement between workers and platforms, which sometimes have characteristics of both employment and 
independent contracting. These new types of engagement take place in a legal and regulatory framework 
that is not well designed for regulating these new business models. The next section explores some of these 
challenges, with a focus on those that relate to labour organized through digital platforms.

Digital platforms have had an increasingly important role in many economies for a decade or more. The use 
of platforms and the policy focus on them has reached new heights in the past few years, in part due to the

when many individuals joined platforms to earn an income – driven in part by pandemic-related job losses. 
The increasing prominence of platforms has drawn attention to both their strengths and weaknesses. Some 
weaknesses have been further highlighted by an increasing number of disputes between platforms and 
platform workers. While these disputes cover a range of issues, earnings by far have been the most common 
driver of disputes, followed by employment status, health and safety and then regulation.29

The employment relationship
Perhaps one of the most important regulatory questions for digital platforms is how to classify the people who 
use them to find work. For many platforms, the default position is to classify those workers as independent 
contractors. This classification may or may not be accurate, depending on the national laws and regulations 
and the specific characteristics of the relationship between the platform and platform worker, among other 
things. In recent years, various jurisdictions have found that platform workers should be classified as employees, 
independent contractors or a third category of workers – “dependent contractors”, “limb workers” or similar. 
Classification as employee
In some jurisdictions, workers for digital platforms have been classified as employees either through decisions 
by courts or new regulations by governments. Notably, most of the high-profile decisions or regulations 
that classify platform workers as employees have come in the transportation sector (including both mobility 
and delivery applications). Examples of decisions that have classified workers of these types of platforms as 
employees include:
	
X Switzerland (2020): Uber was classified as an “employer” by the courts. 
	
X Spain (2020): The Spanish Supreme Court ruled that riders for food delivery app Glovo were employees 
and not independent contractors. The Court found that Glovo fixed the “conditions for the provision of 
its services and owns the assets essential to carrying out its services” while riders “fulfil their roles within 
the employer’s professional organization”.32
	
X Spain (2021): A new regulation (Riders’ Law) was introduced that classified all workers on food delivery 
platforms as employees.33 
	
X Argentina (2021): The First Instance Argentine Labour Court found that a driver on the platform Cabify 
was misclassified as a contractor and was instead an unregistered employee of the company.34
	
X Netherlands (2021): The Amsterdam District Court ruled that Uber drivers are employees and not 
contractors, thus entitling them to a range of greater labour rights.35
	
X Taiwan, China (2022): The Taipei Administrative High Court found that an employment relationship 
existed between a digital delivery platform and a delivery person, based on examination of four criteria: 
the existence of personal subordination; the existence of economic subordination; the existence of 
organizational subordination; and the performance by the individual in person. However, the decision 
was qualified to be valid only for the case in question.36
	
X Uruguay (2022): The twentieth Labour Court of Montevideo found that there was an employment 
relationship between Uber and a driver.37

The implications of worker classification 
Worker classification is an issue of major concern for both platform workers and digital platforms. For platforms, 
the concern is partly financial. In California, for example, the additional cost of hiring all drivers under an

million a year and its competitor Lyft $290 million a year.59 Platforms spent $200 million to pass Proposition 22, 
a ballot measure that would have excluded platform workers from the regulations under Assembly Bill 5.60 In 
other jurisdictions, decisions that require platforms to classify workers as employees have had considerable 
consequences. For example, after passage of the Spanish law, Deliveroo announced its intention to leave Spain.61

law, despite its attempt to improve the working conditions of app-based workers. 
Dismissal or termination 
Another important policy area is the dismissal of platform workers. Often, digital platforms include language 
in their terms of use that govern dismissals, generally by giving them the unilateral ability to terminate 
contracts. The ILO found that it is not uncommon for a platform’s terms of use to “include language that 
allows platforms to reject user accounts for any reason at all, without having to notify users of the reason for 
rejection”.63 For example, Section 11 of the terms of use from the digital platform CrowdFlower notes that the 
platform reserves the right to “suspend, limit or cancel your access to the CrowdFlower website at any time 
for any reason”.64 When an account is closed, platform terms of use often state that workers do not have the 
right to access the platform any longer. Other dismissal-related questions around disputing the closure, right 
to data and work history are “not always clearly regulated”.65 This stands in contrast to many employment 
laws, which provide protections and potentially compensation to employees in the case of dismissal.

Terms and conditions of work
Terms and conditions of work are an important aspect of the experiences of platform workers. The requirements 
that platforms face to provide certain terms and conditions at work affects their costs and profitability and 
therefore the fairness of competition between platforms and traditional enterprises. The ability of platforms 
to influence terms and conditions of work varies significantly between countries and also depending upon the 
specific nature of an individual platform or sector. Many jurisdictions have implemented a legal and regulatory 
framework that seeks to promote occupational safety and health (OSH) broadly and not just in the context of 
an employer–employee relationship. Regulations around working hours and leave, however, are much more 
closely connected to the status of the relationship between the worker and employer. This section reviews 
how a range of countries have addressed the terms and conditions for platform workers related to working 
hours, leave, social protection, accident insurance and OSH. 
Terms and conditions of work sometimes are made clear to platform workers through apps or the terms 
of use statement. In other cases, the algorithms that underlie the apps contain code that shapes the terms 
and conditions of work. This is not always disclosed to workers. Some jurisdictions are starting to create 
requirements that platform companies disclose algorithms. In Spain, for example, a new section was added 
to the Workers’ Statue that requires platform companies to disclose algorithms to the employees’ legal 
representatives. This allows them to understand the “parameters, rules and instructions used as a basis for 
the algorithms or artificial intelligence systems that affect how decisions that could affect working conditions 
and access to and retention of employment are made”.75

Workers’ organization and representation
Most workers in the platform economy are not part of a traditional trade union. There are several reasons 
for this. In some jurisdictions, platform economy workers are self-employed and “many jurisdictions do not 
permit workers who are not in an employment relationship to bargain collectively”.85 Instead, these self-
employed workers often fall under competition laws, which prohibit them from engaging in coordination and 
negotiation and which could constitute a cartel. For these platform workers, this is problematic because even 
though they are genuinely self-employed, they exhibit a similar degree of economic dependence on platforms 
as employees do on their employers.86
In places where platform economy workers can organize, there are other challenges. Some of these are 
logistical, including the dispersed nature of the workers and the barriers unions face obtaining information 
about workers, such as a list of worker contacts. Another challenge lies in the legal and regulatory framework. 
Many countries require that a bargaining unit or representative union be identified in order to engage in 
collective bargaining. This concept is “difficult to apply in the context of online web-based platforms, since 
the workforce is diffused across geographical and industry boundaries”.87 
A number of jurisdictions have made changes to specifically allow platform workers to organize and to provide 
their representatives with certain rights. Examples include: 
	
X France (2016): The Labour Code gives platform workers the right to form and join a trade union and 
assert collective interest through their unions.88 It also contains provisions giving self-employed platform 
workers the right to collective action that is protected from retaliatory actions by the platform.89
	
X Ireland (2017): The Competition Act excludes certain categories of workers from prohibitions on 
organization to thus allow them to bargain collectively. 
	
X France (2021): France issued a temporary executive order to give platform workers the right to have 
representatives who can negotiate collective bargaining agreements and engage in social dialogue on 
their behalf.90
Despite the challenges associated with organizing platform workers, many trade unions have reached 
agreements with platforms regarding the terms and conditions of work. Examples of agreements include: 
	
X Spain (2021): Delivery platform Just Eat and the Workers’ General Union (Unión General de Trabajadores) 
and Workers’ Commissions (Comisiones Obreras) trade union confederation signed a collective agreement 
for delivery workers in the food sector that covers working time, wages and working conditions.91
	
X United Kingdom (2022): The General, Municipal, Boilermakers’ and Allied Trade Union (referred to as 
GMB) and Deliveroo signed a union recognition deal covering the platform’s self-employed riders. The 
partnership deal gives GBM the right to bargain collectively on behalf of the riders, although it recognizes 
that they are self-employed. It ensures a pay floor for Deliveroo riders and representation on issues, 
including riders’ health, safety and well-being.92

EBMOs and the changing 
reality of platform work
Economies and labour markets are ever evolving, shaped by changes in technology, ways of doing business 
and even the COVID-19 pandemic. EBMOs must adapt to these changes by continually modifying their services 
and policy advocacy to best serve members with dynamic needs. Digital platforms present a unique challenge 
for EBMOs but also an important opportunity to expand their membership base and continue to shape the 
legal and regulatory framework regarding work. 
Challenges and opportunities
Digital platforms present a unique challenge for EBMOs. Unlike the vast majority of other businesses, platforms 
do not sell a good or service. Instead, they provide a marketplace for buyers and sellers to interact. Some of 
these platforms give buyers and sellers almost total discretion in all aspects of the transaction. Other platforms 
work much differently and operate in a way that controls many aspects of the transaction. Platforms that 
focus on services are especially creating a new way for people to be engaged for their labour. This means 
that platforms, as the ILO noted in its 2021 World Employment and Social Report, “are also changing the very

organization of work”.137 This presents an important long-run challenge for EBMOs, whose membership packages 
and service offerings revolve in part around traditional employment relations. If platforms continue to drive 
a shift away from traditional employment and collective bargaining and undermine the competitiveness of 
these institutions, the appeal of an EBMO that is more narrowly focused on these areas could be significantly 
impacted. 
This presents an important challenge not just to EBMOs but to the social partners who long have shaped the 
organization of work through social dialogue and representative organizations. At the global level, many social 
partners are finding that “attracting new members is becoming a considerable challenge generally and even 
more so in the platform economy context”.138 Some social partners globally have taken steps to address this, 
including by expanding outreach and increasing their presence in digital tools and social media.139 
Many EBMOs have faced challenges in recruiting platforms as members. Observers have noted that there 
appears to be a lack of interest among platforms globally in joining some types of EBMOs, especially those 
focused on employment-related issues, though there have been exceptions in Denmark, Germany and 
Slovakia.140 Generally, platforms are more likely to join a broadly focused business association or chamber 
of commerce. This is probably because platforms do not consider themselves as employers and are often 
vehemently opposed to having platform workers classified as employees. The lack of platform participation 
in employers’ organizations creates challenges for EBMOs, which may be perceived as less representative if 
they do not have platforms among their members. It could also lead to policy positions that lack input from 
platforms. Perhaps more problematic is that it can result in new types of dialogue about employment-focused 
issues (such as labour markets and labour law) that exclude EBMOs. For example, trade unions seeking to 
engage with platforms as a group often cannot approach EBMOs because they do not have many platforms 
in their membership. This can make social dialogue regarding workers on platforms difficult and one-sided.141 
The separation of platforms and EBMOs is not universal. The Malaysian Employers Federation, for example, 
reported that it has members that are platforms and that the growth of the platform economy has created 
“good potential for them to increase overall membership”. Developments elsewhere in the world show that 
EBMOs face a number of risks. For instance, they risk being excluded from important national-level dialogues on 
labour matters in which they have traditionally had influence and which are of importance for their members. 
They also are at risk of the gradual erosion of employment relationships as the means for organizing work, 
which could undermine the case for enterprises to be members of an EBMO.

X Box 4. Direct negotiations between platforms and unions on national standards
In Australia, the Transport Workers Union began negotiating directly with numerous platforms, including

2.	 Facilitate a cost-effective and efficient mechanism to resolve disputes, such as deactivation of relevant

3.	 Ensure that the rights of platform workers to join and be represented by the relevant registered 
organization (or union) are respected and that platform workers have an effective collective voice.
4.	 Ensure that appropriate enforcement exists to meet these standards and objectives.

importance is less common. Instead, these issues traditionally were raised with national or sectoral EBMOs.

Employment classification 
Most EBMOs either did not take a position on the classification of platform workers or argued that they 
should be classified as independent contractors. Advocating for an independent contractor classification 
continues to be a reasonable approach for some platform workers, such as skilled tradespeople or computer 
programmers. It is not clear that this approach makes sense for all platform workers, nor is it clear that it will 
be successful. In short, there is increasing risk associated with advocating a blanket independent contractor 
status approach for all platform workers. This is evident not just by the reclassifications taking place in Europe 
but also developments in Southeast Asian countries, including Malaysia and the Philippines. EBMOs want a 
more nuanced approach to classifying platform workers. 
EBMOs should not try to apply one classification to all platform workers. They should instead recognize the 
range of business models and types of engagement with platform workers. Some platforms, such as Airbnb, 
are used primarily to rent accommodations, and although some labour is involved, few policymakers would 
classify these workers as employees. Other workers, especially those who work through location-based labour 
platforms, have more characteristics of their work in common with employees. Some aspects of location-based 
platform work are difficult to reconcile with the classification of independent contractor in some jurisdictions. 
EBMOs could adopt a policy approach that argues for different types of employment classification based on 
the work characteristics of platform workers. This approach would include calling for the following:
	
X Some platform workers should continue to be classified as independent contractors. This argument is 
most salient when the characteristics of the work more closely resemble that of an independent contractor 
(for example, they have the capacity to set their own prices or reject work without consequences). Many 
platforms already operate in this way. But they could also provide guidance to platforms to modify their 
business models to ensure that they are compliant with regulations regarding independent contractor 
status.
	
X Other platform workers should be classified using a third type of status, which could be a 
“dependent contractor” or “worker”. This classification could trigger some additional legal protections 
for workers and requirements of platforms. It is important for EBMOs to clearly distinguish the legal 
requirements for this status compared to that of an independent contractor. These distinctions should 
be clearly elaborated in the legal and regulatory framework. Characteristics of this type of status could 
include when the platform: 
	
f exercises control over the remuneration of the contractor (by setting prices, as opposed to allowing 
the contractor to set their own prices);
	
f requires the contractor to abide by certain work rules, such as appearance, conduct or performance 
of the work; and
	
f supervises the work or verifies the quality of the results.
EBMOs should be aware that other business associations have adopted support for the use of a third 
classification for platform workers. For example, the Chamber of Progress, a collection of large, mostly United 
States-based platform businesses, has called for a “new category of employment to ensure flexible workers 
receive the pay they deserve and keep the hours they enjoy”.142
EBMOs could continue to advocate against using the employee classification for location-based labour platforms. 
This position is not out of the global mainstream, with courts and governments around the world finding that 
location-based labour platform workers are independent contractors. Some countries in Southeast Asia have 
already begun discussing the classification of some platform workers as employees. EBMOs could emphasize

EBMOs could advocate for a set of rules around earnings to ensure that platform work pay is transparent

working with the platform. It could also include requirements that pay must be delivered within a certain 
number of days after the service was performed and that all deductions must be clearly disclosed and itemized.

EBMO institutional approach 
Ensuring tripartite structures and the role of EBMOs
EBMOs are an important actor for representing the voice of the private sector in national, sectoral and 
regional policy discussions and social dialogue. This is in part because they provide a representative private 
sector viewpoint on how work should be organized and regulated, among other things. But digital platforms 
present a challenge to this traditional EBMO function because they create another avenue through which 
work is organized. They do this in a way that both falls outside of many aspects of the legal and regulatory 
framework and also falls outside of long-established consultation and social dialogue mechanisms. 
For EBMOs, it is important to respond to these changing dynamics by reinforcing the importance of tripartism 
and the central role of representative organizations of businesses. It is important for EBMOs to: 
	
X reinforce with both government and trade unions that EBMOs are the primary voice of business for all 
labour and social matters; and 
	
X recognize that direct negotiations between governments and platforms about the organization or 
regulation of work in the absence of trade union and employer representatives undermines the tripartite 
structures and risks creating multiple, uncoordinated regulatory frameworks for work. 
In some countries, unions have also been having dialogue with digital platforms about the organization and 
regulation of work. In some instances, these negotiations cover matters similar to what is covered during 
enterprise-level collective bargaining. Because this type of collective bargaining is commonplace, it does 
not present any challenges for EBMOs. In fact, it may present opportunities if EBMOs can put together a 
service offering to assist platforms in their negotiations with trade unions. When unions dialogue with 
platforms about national-level policy, EBMOs should note the importance of including representatives of the 
broader private sector. This would help ensure that there are not unfair standards that disadvantage some 
enterprises while benefiting platforms.

Membership and services for digital platforms
For EBMOs, one of the most important agenda items regarding the platform economy is to increase the 
share of platforms as members. To date, EBMOs globally have struggled to attract digital platforms to their 
membership ranks, though this trend is more pronounced among employment-focused organizations than 
it is among chambers of commerce. With the increasing attention on the labour-related aspects of platforms, 
especially location-based labour platforms, EBMOs have an opportunity to parlay their labour-related expertise 
into a more attractive value-for-money proposition for platforms. 
Some platforms may not have an interest in paying a membership fee that EBMOs use in part to fund labour-
related services. To increase the appeal of membership to platforms, EBMOs can adopt other approaches, 
such as: 
	
X Develop and implement a special membership package or tier for digital platforms. This package 
could include access to non-labour-related services and involve platforms in the development of policy 
agendas but provide no labour-related advisory or support services. 
	
X Start a partner or subsidiary association affiliated with the national EBMO that caters only to 
digital platforms. The benefit of this approach is that it would help address the hesitations of platforms 
in joining an organization with a traditional focus on employer–employee relations.
EBMOs can offer a range of services that may appeal to digital platforms. These services draw on the EBMO’s 
existing expertise in labour-related areas. The potential services that EBMOs could offer to platforms include: 
	
X labour law advisory to help platforms ensure that they are properly classifying existing workers;
	
X strategic advisory to platforms to design business models to meet legal standards for a certain type of 
worker classification;
	
X assistance in negotiations with trade unions, in a similar way that EBMOs provide assistance to an 
enterprise or group of enterprises during their collective bargaining with a workers’ representative; and 
	
X inputs into the development of policy for the EBMO’s advocacy agenda.
EBMOs could conduct consultations with digital platforms to determine what opportunities exist and also 
which types of platforms are most likely to join them. While the largest platforms may have the resources to 
address many of these challenges in-house, EBMOs may find that there are more opportunities to provide 
services to newer and smaller platforms that cannot afford to have legal and labour expertise in-house. 
Platform-related services for existing members
For EBMOs, the needs of existing enterprises regarding labour and employment will remain similar in the 
years to come. Platforms are changing some aspects of the competitive environment for enterprises and often 
present enterprises with new opportunities for how to organize their business. EBMOs could conduct research 
to review how enterprises are using platforms and provide advisory and training to help them incorporate 
digital platforms into their operations in a way that increases competitiveness.

Social protection systems in Asean: A strategy guide for employers
Economies and labour markets are ever evolving, shaped by changes in technology, ways of doing business 
and the increasing prominence of digital platforms during the COVID-19 pandemic. Employer and business 
membership organizations (EBMOs) must adapt to these changes by continually modifying their services and 
policy advocacy to best serve members with dynamic needs. Digital platforms present a unique challenge for 
EBMOs but also an important opportunity to expand their membership base and continue to shape the legal 
and regulatory framework regarding work.
