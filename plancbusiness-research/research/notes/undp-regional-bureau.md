⊲	Gig work is structural. It is now part of how the labour market functions 
and must be governed as such.  
⊲	Opportunity is real, but insecurity is widespread. Flexibility and income 
access often come with volatility, weak voice, and thin protections.  
⊲	Digital informality is the core risk. Platforms can replicate the 
vulnerabilities of informal work while scaling them faster.  
⊲	Protection gaps are the critical failure point. Social security and labour 
standards often do not cover multi-job, task-based livelihoods.  
⊲	Algorithmic governance needs accountability. Pricing, allocation, ratings, 
and deactivation systems should be transparent, contestable, and 
subject to oversight.  
⊲	Gig economy also suffers from inequality. Digital divides shape who 
benefits and who remains in low-value work.  
⊲	As platform work scales, so do fraud, fake job offers, identity theft, and 
exploitative intermediaries, making trust, verification, and digital safety 
part of the labour policy agenda.  
⊲	The future of gig work will be reshaped by big transitions. AI/automation, 
cross-border online work, and the green transition will change what gigs 
exist, who benefits, and what protections are needed. 
⊲	Effective governance is essential for a productive gig economy. The 
aim is to guide it towards decent and productive work through coherent 
governance that integrates labour policy, social protection, digital 
regulation, fiscal policy, and competition rules.

Harnessing the Gig Economy
The Asia-Pacific region is at the epicentre of the global gig 
economy boom that offers unprecedented flexibility and 
income to millions (WEF, 2025)1. The gig economy refers 
to a labour market characterised by short-term, flexible 
work arrangements, often facilitated by digital platforms 
(ILO, 2025).2 Gig workers are now a non-negligible part of 
the global labour force, with about 154 million to 435 mil­
lion people doing online gig jobs, which is around 4.4% to 
12.5% of the total (Datta et al, 2023).3 The number would be 
much higher if offline gig work were included. This policy 
paper speaks to both.
In China, over 200 million individuals, about 40% of the 
urban labour force, depend on various flexible work (The 
Economist, 2025)4 Indonesia’s gig workforce is estimated at 
between 430,000 and 2.3 million, comprising various types 
of gig workers (WEF, 2025). India is projected to have 23.5 
million gig workers by 2029-30, making up 40% of the glob­
al freelance market. Bangladesh has over 650,000 online 
freelancers (The Daily Star, 2024)5, one of the largest such 
pools in the world, and Pakistan, the Philippines, and Viet­
nam are also emerging hubs for online and offline gig work. 
Across the Asia-Pacific region, significant demographic and 
economic shifts are reshaping labour markets and driving 
the growth of the gig economy. Many countries are experi­
encing a youth bulge, with large groups of young workers 
entering the job market while formal job creation remains 
slow (ILO, 2024).6 This shortage of formal employment op­
portunities contributes to persistently high levels of informal 
work, making gig work an accessible alternative. Addition­
ally, rapid urbanisation has increased the demand for plat­
form-based services. A sweeping digital transformation, 
marked by the widespread use of smartphones and expand­
ing internet access, supports the growth of the gig economy 
by enabling digital platforms to scale quickly. Furthermore, 
the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption and ex­
pansion of remote work.
However, this rapid rise in gig work also exposes workers 
to “digital informality”: irregular earnings, economic insta­
bility, algorithmic control and a lack of protections. These 
challenges mirror long-standing issues in the Asia-Pacific’s 
informal economy. The gig economy is not simply a new 
form of work- it is a complex system that is rapidly expand­
ing due to structural labour market gaps, demographic 
change, digital transitions, and shocks. It is now a semi-per­

Harnessing the Gig Economy
Originally coined in 1915 and now widely associated with 
platform-based labour, “Gig Work” refers to short-term, 
flexible, task-specific employment arrangements that de­
viate from the conventional parameters of standard em­
ployment. When these types of work arrangements be­
come prevalent in an economy, it is referred to as a “Gig 
Economy.”  Instead of traditional open-ended employment 
contracts, gig work involves on-demand tasks or projects 
completed for payment on a per-task or per-service basis. 
This model spans a wide array of sectors and job types, 
from driving for a ride-hailing app or delivering food to free­
lancing online as a graphic designer or coder. 
Broadly, gig work falls into two categories. At one end of 
the spectrum are the location-based gigs that require phys­
ical presence (e.g. rideshare driving, delivery couriers, do­
mestic services, care workers, warehouse gig workers). At 
the other end of the spectrum are the online “cloud” gigs 
that can be done remotely via the internet (e.g. digital free­
lancing in design, programming, writing, data entry, etc.). 
What unites these diverse gigs is their reliance on apps 
and digital platforms to connect workers with customers, 
handle payments, and, often, algorithmically manage the 
work. Workers need access to the internet and to inter­
net-enabled devices, as well as some level of digital liter­
acy. The platforms utilise algorithms to connect workers 
with tasks, create an environment for interactions between 
supply and demand, and set guidelines for the work being 
performed. 
Gig opportunities vary significantly by country and re­
gion, depending on internet access, regulations in force, 
economic factors and level of skills specialisation. Gig 
work also intersects with traditional employment, as many 
workers combine both. For example, a nurse could offer 
telehealth consultations outside regular shifts; a full-time 
IT professional might take on freelance projects on week­
ends; a cook might sell homemade meals or host virtual 
cooking classes. 
Gig workers are typically classified as self-employed or in­
dependent contractors, meaning they usually lack access 
to minimum wages, paid leave, health insurance, or pen­
sion plans that formal employees enjoy (ILO, 2025). Rather

The rise of gig work is no longer a fleeting or marginal trend. 
The preferences of businesses and consumers, as well as 
broader structural shifts in the economy, including growth in 
the manufacturing and services sectors, rapid urbanisation, 
large-scale shocks, and demographic shifts in the region, 
have consolidated its position and practice. Technological 
advancements have played a pivotal role in facilitating this 
evolution. Worker motivation to work on digital labour plat­
forms varies across platform types and by gender (ILO, 2021)7. 
Datta et al. (2023) has identified flexibility, additional income 
opportunities, and addressing the livelihood income gap as 
key reasons for the growing adoption of gig work (Figure 1).

Demographic and labour market shifts
In Asia and the Pacific, structural employment gaps, rapid 
labour force growth, particularly among youth, outpace for­
mal job creation. Many countries face a youth bulge, with 
millions entering the workforce each year, yet formal em­
ployment opportunities remain scarce or inaccessible. At 
the same time, a large share of the region’s workforce has 
long operated in informal settings with limited job security, 
wages, or protections. In this context, gig work, whether 
through ride-sharing, delivery services, or online freelanc­
ing, offers an accessible income stream. For many, plat­
form-based work is not a choice but a necessity in such a 
constrained labour market. 
Beyond necessity, a significant number of people are also 
attracted by the flexibility and independence that gig work 
promises. Students, caregivers, and others who need a 
flexible work schedule find gig opportunities appealing be­
cause they can choose when and how much to work. On 
freelance platforms, the primary motivator seems to be the 
desire for job flexibility or to work from home, while on mi­
crotask platforms, supplementing income is key. A survey 
of 1,500 Malaysian gig workers found that flexibility, acces­
sibility, and the opportunity to earn supplementary income 
were the three primary reasons individuals joined gig work. 
The need for flexibility is especially significant for women in 
both developing and developed countries, as in most cas­
es, they are the primary caregivers in the family and must 
balance paid work with family responsibilities.

avoiding long-term contracts or fixed payrolls. This model 
is particularly attractive for startups and small businesses, 
while larger corporations use online freelancing platforms 
to outsource specialised tasks.
Shock accelerators
The COVID-19 pandemic catalysed platform adoption. 
During the lockdown, many formal jobs disappeared over­
night, while demand for delivery, logistics, and remote 
digital services surged, which embedded gig work more 
deeply in national labour markets. For instance, demand 
for grocery and medicine delivery via gig couriers surged 
across Asian cities during lockdowns. Also, businesses, 
having gone remote, became more open to hiring free­
lancers online. For some businesses, the motivation for 
hiring freelancers was not only for flexibility, but also to 
externalise costs by sourcing labour from lower-cost mar­
kets in Asia and the Pacific, particularly in creative, trans­
lation, data, and content-moderation work. The COVID-19 
period effectively normalised gig work as a larger part of 
labour market dynamics and introduced new users (both 
workers and customers) to these platforms. As economies 
reopened, gig work had cemented a larger role and was 
advancing rapidly in most countries in the region especially 
in countries such as India and the Philippines, where glob­
al demand for remote services has historically intersected 
with large, skilled, and cost-competitive labour pools (Datta 
et al, 2023).
However, post-shock trajectories have been uneven: while 
some workers have transitioned back into formal employ­
ment, many have remained in gig work due to persistent 
shortages of quality jobs or the flexibility it offers, often 
continuing to face income volatility and limited protec­
tions (ILO, 2021). These demographic, technological, and 
shock-related drivers explain why gig work has become a 
structural feature of labour markets rather than a temporary 
phenomenon, underscoring the need for proactive policies 
that integrate gig work into social protection systems, skills 
strategies, and governance frameworks rather than treat­
ing it as a marginal or informal activity.

While the gig economy has offered job opportunities, it has 
exposed workers to multiple vulnerabilities and challeng­
es, many of which echo broader socio-economic issues 
such as inequalities across gender, geography, and socio­
economic status. This section addresses key challenges, 
while section 6 explores policy considerations to leverage 
gig economy development for human development.
Precarity and informality
Gig workers often face precarious conditions such as low 
and volatile earnings, long and irregular hours, and limit­
ed safety and social protections. They are typically ex­
cluded from formal labour regulations. The nature and 
degree of precarity differ across digital labour platforms. 
Location-based workers, such as ride-hailing drivers and 
delivery couriers, are more exposed to physical risks, long 
and unpredictable hours, algorithmic management, and 
must often cover work-related costs like fuel and mainte­
nance. In contrast, online web-based workers, including 
freelancers and microworkers, face less physical risk but 
experience significant income insecurity due to global 
competition, unpaid waiting time, opaque task allocation, 
and limited bargaining power.
Most gig workers are classified as self-employed contrac­
tors, which means they lack access to health insurance, 
injury compensation, pensions, or grievance mechanisms. 
NITI Aayog (2022)8 highlights that the vast majority of gig 
and platform workers in India remain excluded from formal 
social security coverage under current schemes, leaving 
them economically vulnerable in case of work-related ac­
cidents or illness. 
Income volatility and pay gaps 
Gig workers are typically paid per task or per delivery, with 
no guaranteed minimum wage. Demand fluctuations, in­
tense competition, and platform fees lead to highly irregular 
earnings. ILO (2021) noted that the overwhelming majority of 
workers on location-based platforms and about one-third of 
the workers on online web-based platforms stated that plat­
form work was their primary source of income, while many

Algorithmic asymmetry
Gig workers are managed not by human supervisors, but by 
algorithms and data-driven systems embedded in the apps. 
These algorithms assign tasks, set prices, evaluate perfor­
mance (via ratings), and can even deactivate workers from 
the platform. This algorithmic control extends to surveillance 
and performance pressure, nudging workers to work faster 
or longer. This can create a constant pressure to be available 
and productive. The process is typically opaque; workers of­
ten do not know how decisions are made or how to contest 
them. This creates a stark power imbalance between individ­
ual gig workers and the platform companies. Workers may 
be penalised or removed due to an algorithmic decision (for 
instance, automated account suspension for falling below a 
rating threshold) with little recourse or human review. Lack 
of transparency and accountability in these systems remains 
a significant challenge.

Gig work intersects with megatrends related to artificial 
intelligence (AI), climate change, the green transition, and 
global digital integration, which require anticipatory gover­
nance and resilience planning.
AI and automation
AI technologies have been evolving both as complements 
and competitors to gig workers. AI can generate new gig 
opportunities, such as increased demand for data label­
ling, AI model training, and “prompt engineering,” as well 
as boosting productivity for skilled freelancers who use AI 
tools. However, many of these new opportunities in the AI 
supply chain are often precarious, characterised by low 
wages, a lack of employment benefits, and fragmented 
working conditions. These conditions can even expose 
workers to psychological harm, for example, from labelling 
toxic or traumatic content (Canadian Affairs, 2025).12
At the same time, some platform companies are actively 
seeking to mitigate vulnerabilities by leveraging platform 
data and partnerships to support workers’ development, 
such as skill acquisition and worker representation. For 
example, Grab Singapore has introduced initiatives such 
as GrabBenefits 2.0 and formal recognition of worker as­
sociations under the Platform Workers Act, offering subsi­
dised healthcare, upskilling opportunities, and structured 
dialogue on work conditions to improve worker welfare 
(GRAB, 2025).13  
The widespread concern regarding the AI revolution is that 
it may automate many tasks currently performed by gig 
workers, potentially reducing demand for human labour in 
certain areas. For example, AI writing assistants can per­
form basic content writing or translation gigs; algorithms 
can code increasingly sophisticated programs, reducing 
demand for entry-level coders; self-driving technology 
could displace ride-share drivers. The net impact of AI is 
thus complex.  UNDP (2025)14 notes that online gig workers 
in Asia and the Pacific face a greater risk of job displace­
ment from AI than location-based gig workers providing 
in-person services. This report highlights two key factors 
that influence AI’s impact on gig work. First, when AI can

Green transition 
The global movement towards a green economy and cli­
mate action is a significant trend that may impact gig work. 
As countries invest in renewable energy, circular economy 
initiatives, and climate-resilient infrastructure, new jobs are 
expected to emerge, many of which will take the form of 
gigs or short-term contracts. For instance, renewable en­
ergy companies might hire on-demand technicians to in­
stall solar panels in remote communities.15 Additionally, the 
growth of waste management and recycling startups may 
utilise gig workers for tasks such as waste collection and 
sorting, thereby contributing to a circular economy. The gig 
workforce could play a crucial role in implementing climate 
solutions at the grassroots level (such as community solar 
maintenance, urban gardening, etc.). However, for green 
gig work to contribute meaningfully to human develop­
ment, such roles must move beyond low-paid and intermit­
tent tasks. In practice, this requires clearer skill pathways, 
predictable demand, and minimum standards around pay, 
safety, and working conditions, as well as access to training, 
equipment, and social protection. At the same time, growing 
demand for rapid, on‑demand services risks driving higher 
emissions, congestion, and energy use. Reducing this foot­
print requires integrating green‑transition priorities directly 
into platform regulation and urban mobility policy.

Global and transboundary economic and 
digital integration
The global reach of digital platforms has transformed the 
gig economy into an international, regional and cross-bor­
der opportunity. Freelancing platforms (such as Upwork, 
Freelancer, Fiverr) connect workers in developing Asia with 
international clients, enabling income generation beyond 
local wage constraints. Workers benefit from tapping into 
higher-paying global markets, while firms in developed 
countries leverage cost-effective talent. According to the 
Datta et al. (2023), low- and middle-income countries now 
account for 40% of global traffic to online gig platforms, 
highlighting the growing integration of developing coun­
tries into global digital labour markets while the gig work­
ers on the global platforms may have relatively more au­
tonomy in choosing projects and setting rates compared 
to workers on local, location-based platforms (like ride-
share), precarity is universal across the gig economy, and 
online platforms are fundamentally similar in their reliance 
on algorithmic control using opaque algorithmic rating and 
ranking systems that lack transparency for workers. The 
global integration may intensify competition among work­
ers across borders, particularly in lower-skilled segments, 
where labour oversupply can put downward pressure on 
prices and earnings. These dynamics contribute to income 
volatility, weakened bargaining power, and persistent pre­
carity, underscoring the transboundary governance chal­
lenges associated with platform-mediated work. 
Emerging political dynamics of gig work
Across the region, gig workers are increasingly making their 
voices heard. In countries like Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thai­
land, delivery riders and ride-hailing drivers have demon­
strated their ability to mobilise rapidly through social media 
and messaging platforms, staging large-scale protests over 
wages, commission structures, and social protections (CNA, 
2025).16 This activism has evolved into political participa­
tion, with some gig workers winning seats on local councils 
in Indonesia. The growing visibility and organisation of gig 
workers signal a shift from fragmented labour activism to 
a systemic force influencing policy debates and electoral 
strategies, particularly as politicians recognise their numbers 
and potential as a strategic voter bloc. 
Risks of scam platforms 
While platform-based gig work has unlocked new avenues 
for flexible employment and income generation across 
Asia and the Pacific, it is important to recognise that not 
all platform-mediated activities align with the principles 
or protections of the legitimate gig economy. The emer­
gence of scam centres and illegal digital operations, where 
individuals are coerced or deceived into fraudulent on­
line work, represents a parallel, illicit ecosystem that ex­
ploits the same technological infrastructure but operates 
outside the bounds of legal and ethical labour markets. 
Unlike mainstream gig platforms, which connect workers 
to legitimate tasks and clients, these scam platforms are 
orchestrated by criminal syndicates and often involve traf­
ficking, forced labour, and cybercrime. And such scam plat­
forms have grown big in parts of the Asia-Pacific region 
(OHCHR, 202317, UNODC, 2024, 2025)18 19. This distinction 
underscores the need for robust governance, cross-border 
regulatory cooperation, and vigilant monitoring to ensure 
that platform-based job opportunities remain a force for in­
clusive development, rather than a vector for exploitation.

barriers to entrepreneurship; for example, an artisan

standalone labour-market issue but sits within a web of

Social protection 
Ensuring social protection for the gig workers requires mov­
ing toward more inclusive and adaptable social protection 
systems by supporting the design and piloting of hybrid 
models that extend essential protections to gig workers 
without compromising the flexibility that attracts many to 
this form of work. Practical entry points include developing 
tailored micro‑insurance schemes for health, accident, or 
income loss, and facilitating gig workers’ enrolment in na­
tional health or social insurance programmes through sub­
sidised premiums or simplified contribution mechanisms. In 
this connection, insights could be drawn from the hybrid 
social protection concept for gig workers by the Malaysia 
Digital Economy Corporation (MDEC) (Annex-1). 
Portable benefits approaches are particularly relevant for 
fragmented work patterns, enabling workers to accumu­
late entitlements across multiple platforms and job types, 
provided that the design remains administratively simple 
and fiscally credible ( Annex-1). Piloting such models in 
priority sectors, such as ride‑hailing or delivery services, 
can demonstrate how small, task‑linked contributions from 
platforms, workers, and governments can build meaning­
ful protection over time. These mechanisms are especially 
important for highly mobile workers, enabling continuity of 
benefits regardless of how often they change gigs or com­
bine multiple sources of income.
To sustain these systems, fiscal policy reforms will be es­
sential. Progressive, gig‑sensitive financing options should 
be identified, including earmarked digital levies, matched 
contributions, or platform‑based transaction fees that chan­
nel resources directly into worker protection funds. By 
combining innovative benefit models with viable financing 
pathways, countries can build future‑ready social protection 
systems that reduce precarity and support more resilient 
livelihoods in an increasingly platform‑driven economy.
Gender Equality
Gig work presents new opportunities for women’s eco­
nomic participation in the Asia and the Pacific countries, 
but only if structural barriers related to access, safety, 
skills, and care responsibilities are deliberately addressed. 
As underscored in the UNDP Strategic Plan 2026–2029, 
gender equality must be treated not only as a goal but as 
an enabler of inclusive development outcomes. Gig work 
can be made more gender‑inclusive by strengthening the 
enabling environment across three practical entry points. 
First, expanding women’s access to digital infrastructure 
and skills, including targeted digital and AI‑literacy pro­
grammes, device access initiatives, and partnerships with 
platforms to onboard women into higher‑value digital work. 
Second, introducing gender‑responsive safety and design 
standards, such as verified user identities, in‑app safety 
tools, safer commuting options, and platform algorithms 
that increase visibility of women in higher‑pay segments. 
Third, integrating care‑sensitive measures, for example, 
childcare credits, flexible scheduling norms, or compen­
sated downtime, into emerging gig‑work frameworks, en­
abling women to participate without exacerbating existing 
care burdens.
Without such regulatory and programmatic action, existing 
gender inequalities will continue to restrict women’s abil­
ity to benefit from the gig economy. By combining practi­
cal pilots with upstream policy reform, gig work should be 
transformed into a more meaningful pathway for women’s 
empowerment and economic security. At the regional lev­
el, practical cooperation can include: sharing standards for 
safety-by-design on platforms, supporting regional initia­
tives to reduce the digital gender divide, and accelerating 
evidence-building on what improves women’s outcomes 
across different gig segments.

Harnessing the Gig Economy
Looking ahead, the gig economy in Asia and the Pacific re­
gion will be reshaped by rapid advances in automation, AI, 
and mobility technologies, requiring governments to pre­
pare now for the next wave of labour market disruption. As 
discussed in detail in UNDP (2025), AI tools are increasing­
ly capable of performing cognitive tasks traditionally done 
by online gig workers, from translation and transcription 
to basic content creation and entry‑level coding. As these 
tools scale, a significant share of lower-skilled digital gigs 
may become automated, potentially reducing earnings op­
portunities for workers who depend on microtasks or en­
try-level freelance work. At the same time, emerging tech­
nologies such as autonomous vehicles could transform or 
even displace segments of ride-hailing and delivery work. 
The pace and distribution of these technological shifts are 
uneven, but the direction is clear: the nature of gig work 
will change dramatically, and policy frameworks will need 
to anticipate and respond to these transitions rather than 
react after the fact.
Equally important are the new forms of gig work emerging 
from the green and digital transitions. Demand is expected 
to grow for digitally enabled green jobs, from remote solar 
diagnostics and climate data annotation to last‑mile green 
logistics, creating new entry points for workers if skills de­
velopment and regulatory support keep pace. At the same 
time, the cross‑border nature of digital gig platforms will 
raise complex policy questions around taxation, data gov­
ernance, and competition. As more workers earn income 
from platforms headquartered abroad, governments will 
need new fiscal instruments and international coordina­
tion mechanisms to ensure fair taxation, protect workers’ 
data rights, and prevent regulatory arbitrage. These future 
trends underscore the importance of anticipatory gover­
nance: building flexible, forward‑looking regulatory sys­
tems that can evolve with technological change while safe­
guarding workers’ rights, economic inclusion, and national 
development priorities.
The policy suggestions outlined in this paper reinforce a 
broader development agenda on economic transformation, 
social protection, digital governance, and innovation across 
Asia and the Pacific. As gig work is increasingly becoming 
a structural feature of labour markets in the region, it needs 
to be governed as part of a wider economic and social sys­
tem. Strengthening social protection, advancing gender‑re­

A) MDEC Hybrid Social Protection Model35 36  
Malaysia Digital Economy Corporation (MDEC) pioneered a 
hybrid approach to support gig workers, through its eRezeki 
and Sharing Economy programme. It connects over 1.2 mil­
lion Malaysians to digital freelancing opportunities and has 
generated RM 4 billion since 2016. MDEC’s model combines 
flexible digital platforms with social protection measures; of­
fers micro-insurance and optional access to national health 
and retirement schemes; promotes local cooperatives to re­
duce reliance on intermediaries. 
B) Portable benefits model37 38  
Portable benefit refers to a social protection system that al­
lows workers to carry their benefits with them across jobs 
and platforms, regardless of how often they switch employ­
ers, combine multiple gigs, or work on a part-time/intermit­
tent basis. Instead of benefits being tied to a single employ­
er, they are linked to the worker through an individual, often 
digital, account.
Under this model, contributions are pooled from multiple 
sources, such as platform companies, government, and 
workers themselves, and deposited into a worker’s porta­
ble account whenever they complete a task or earn income. 
These contributions help build entitlements to health insur­
ance, accident and injury protection, pensions, paid leave, 
unemployment support, or training funds. Because benefits 
accumulate even when workers move between platforms 
(e.g., food delivery one month, online freelancing the next), 
portable benefits reduce precarity and offer a fairer system 
for gig and informal workers. Since 2024, Central Provident 
Fund contributions have been made mandatory for platform 
workers under 30 in Singapore.39

C) Minimum Pay Floors 
Minimum pay floors are regulatory or negotiated mecha­
nisms that establish a baseline level of remuneration for gig 
workers, below which pay cannot fall. These floors may be 
defined as a minimum hourly earnings rate (accounting for 
both active working time and necessary waiting time) or as 
a minimum payment per completed task or trip. By setting 
a clear lower bound on earnings, minimum pay floors help 
reduce income volatility and counter downward pressure on 
wages driven by intense competition, opaque algorithmic 
pricing, and unilateral changes to platform pay structures.
