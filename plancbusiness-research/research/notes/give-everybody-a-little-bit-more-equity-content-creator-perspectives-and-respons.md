# "Give everybody [..] a little bit more equity": Content Creator Perspectives and Responses to the Algorithmic Demonetization of Content Associated with Disadvantaged Groups

Kingsley, S., Sinha, P., Wang, C., Eslami, M., & Hong, J. I. (2022). Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. 6, CSCW2, Article 424 (November 2022), 37 pages. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3555149

## Abstract (verbatim)

"Algorithmic systems help manage the governance of digital platforms featuring user-generated content, including how money is distributed to creators from the profits a platform earns from advertising on this content. However, creators producing content about disadvantaged populations have reported these kinds of systems are biased, having associated their content with prohibited or unsafe content, leading to what creators believed were error-prone decisions to demonetize their videos. Motivated by these reports, we present the results of 20 interviews with YouTube creators and a content analysis of videos, tweets, and news about demonetization cases to understand YouTubers' perceptions of demonetization affecting videos featuring disadvantaged or vulnerable populations, as well as creator responses to demonetization, and what kinds of tools and infrastructure support they desired."

## Study design

- 20 semi-structured interviews with YouTube content creators, recruited via Twitter, Discord, Reddit, and a project GitHub site. Eligibility: 18+, current or former YouTube creator, knowledge of content demonetization. Screening survey ran Aug 2, 2021 - Sep 6, 2021: 66 completed screening, 60 were current/former YouTube creators (28 had created for 3-5 years, 16 for 5+ years, 13 for 1-2 years, 5 for <1 year). All 37 eligible respondents were invited; 20 completed a recorded 1-hour Zoom interview, compensated $35 USD gift card.
- Companion demographic/socioeconomic/monetization survey: 21 respondents (20 interviewees + 1 who completed only the survey). Mostly men (18), 2 women, 1 non-binary; race: White (10), Black (7), American Indian/Alaskan Native (2), Asian (1), White+Asian (1); ages skewed young (6 aged 18-24, 12 aged 25-34, 2 aged 45-54); sexual orientation: heterosexual/straight (15), bisexual (5), 1 "mostly heterosexual"; highly educated (9 bachelor's, 2 master's/professional, 6 some college). Locations included Belgium, Malaysia, Pakistan, USA, UK and others outside US/Europe/Asia.
- Parallel content analysis: ~50,000 tweets collected via Twitter API keyword search, a random chronological subset of 580 open-coded (176 specifically about LGBTQ demonetization), published between Sept 2016 and Nov 2020; transcripts of 29 YouTube videos (2017-2020) by 25 unique creators about LGBTQ demonetization (10 discussed economic harms = 40%, 18 discussed user auditing = 72%, 22 discussed making sense of the algorithm = 88%, 23 discussed collective support = 92%), plus 4 additional videos about harms to Black creators; and 10 news articles (2018-2020) from outlets including Washington Post, Vox, CNN, Rolling Stone, Business Insider, gathered via keyword search and LexisNexis. Grounded-theory open coding was used throughout; interview transcripts were coded in Taguette by three authors independently, then reconciled.
- IRB-approved. No inter-rater reliability statistic reported since final coding was done by one author after group reconciliation (methodological limitation acknowledged by the authors).

## Key findings relevant to the income vs. motivation quitting debate

- Creators reported YouTube's demonetization algorithm disproportionately flagged content associated with LGBTQ people/culture and Black creators/communities as "unsafe" for advertisers, causing lost direct ad income, reduced third-party brand-sponsorship income (one creator reported sponsor income cut from "$8,000 for each sponsored video"), and reduced discoverability that indirectly suppressed earnings.
- Creators described YouTube ad income as fundamentally "variable or unpredictable," and proposed three concrete economic fixes: (1) a minimum/base payment above a subscriber or view threshold to alleviate income insecurity ("If there was like a minimum wage above subscribers that would be very... helpful," P13; "when you reach a certain threshold... you should just have a base income... a more reliable source of income," P16); (2) platform-provided benefits such as health insurance; (3) a labor union for content creators, though interviewees were skeptical unions could succeed against YouTube (one cited a failed prior German union effort, another cited a "creator Union" fee scheme they believed was a scam).
- Creators explicitly frame their situation as structurally similar to gig-economy workers who "lack adequate mechanisms to protect them against economic harms of their work," and call for demonetization/compensation to be treated as a future-of-work and anti-discrimination policy issue.
- IMPORTANT NEGATIVE FINDING for the exit-survey hunt: despite recruiting both current AND former YouTube creators and asking about income harms directly, the paper contains **zero references to creators quitting, stopping, burning out, or leaving the platform** — verified via full-text keyword search for "quit," "stopped creating," "gave up," "left the platform," and "burnout" (all zero hits). The paper is about perceptions of and responses to demonetization while still active/engaged with the platform, not about attrition or exit decisions. It does NOT function as an exit-survey instrument despite including former creators in its recruiting criteria — the former-creator subset's specific reasons for having left are not reported or analyzed separately.

## Relevance caveat

This paper is about perceived algorithmic bias in demonetization of content associated with marginalized demographics (LGBTQ, Black creators) specifically — not a general study of creator income vs. motivation in continuation/attrition decisions. Cite for: (a) creators' own framing of income unpredictability as a structural harm, (b) evidence that creators conceptualize their relationship to platforms as employment-like and want wage-like protections, (c) confirmed absence of quitting/attrition data even in a sample that included former creators.
